We found that vibration or pinch of the perineal region effectively triggered 39 rhythmic activity, unilateral and bilateral to nerve stimulation. When electrically stimulating 40 nerves, a dding perineal stimulation modulate d rhythmic activity by decreasing cycle and 41 burst durations and by increasing the amplitude of flexors and extensors. Perineal stimulation 42 also disrupt ed the timing of the ipsilateral rhythm , which had been entrained by nerve 43 stimulation. Mechanically stimulat ing the perineal region decreased ipsilateral and 44 contralateral short-latency reflex responses evoked by cutaneous inputs, a phenomenon we 45 observed in muscles crossing different joints and located in different limbs. The r esults 46 suggest that the excitatory effect of perineal stimulation on locomotion and weight support 47 is not mediated by increas ing cutaneous reflex gain and instead points to an excitation of 48 central p attern-generating circuitry . This is 67 due to the presence of a network of neurons located within the lumbar cord that produces the 68 basic hindlimb locomotor pattern, the so-called central pattern generator (CPG) (McCrea & 69 Rybak, 2008; Rossignol & Frigon, 2011; Kiehn, 2016) . However, in spinal animals, 70 somatosensory feedback is required to initiate hindlimb locomotion and to adjust the pattern 71 for task demands. 72 Studies have shown that some types of somatosensory feedback inhibit locomotion while 73 others facilitate it. For example, mechanically stimulating the lumbar region inhibits weight 74 support and locomotion, or locomotor -like activity, in chronic spinal cats in various 75 preparations with the hindlimbs free to move or restrained (Frigon et al., 2012; Hurteau et 76 al., 2015; Merlet et al. , 2020) and in curarized decerebrate rabbits with intact or transected 77 spinal cords (Viala & Buser, 1974; Viala et al., 1978). In contrast, tonic stimulation of the 78 skin of the perineal region (scrotum, vulva, base of the tail and inguinal fold) facilitates 79 hindlimb locomotion in spinal mammals. For decades, perineal stimulation has been used to 80 initiate fictive locomotion in acute spinal decerebrate curarized preparations treated with 81 clonidine, an α 2-noradrenergic agonist (Forssberg & Grillner, 1973; Pearson & Rossignol, 82 1991; Pearson et al. , 1992; McCrea et al. , 1995; Bennett et al. , 1996) and to facilitate or 83 reinforce hindlimb locomotion in chronic spinal mammals (Barbeau & Rossignol, 1987; 84 Belanger et al., 1996; Leblond et al. , 2003; Langlet et al. , 2005; Hochman, 2012; Alluin et 85 al., 2015; Harnie et al., 2019). Stimulating the skin of the perineal region can trigger a well-86 coordinated pattern of hindlimb locomotion by increasing spinal neuronal excitability 87 through an undefined mechanism (Pearson & Rossignol, 1991). Indeed, several pharmacological studies 90 demonstrated that injecting noradrenergic agonists in spinal cats (Forssberg & Grillner, 1973; 91 Barbeau et al., 1987; Chau et al. , 1998a, 1998b) or serotonergic agonists in spinal mice or 92 rats (Kim et al., 2001; Antri et al., 2002, 2005; Sławińska et al., 2012) facilitates hindlimb 93 locomotion by increasing spinal neuronal excitability. Functionally, the facilitatory effect of 94 perineal region presumably underlies some important survival function, such as escape from 95 a predator (Rossignol et al., 2006). 96 Although we know that activating mechanoreceptors from the perineal region increases 97 the excitability of spinal sensorimotor circuits generating weight support and locomotion, we 98 do not know how this excitatory effect is mediated and how it interacts with the spinal 99 locomotor CPG. One approach to investigate changes in sensorimotor interactions is to evoke 100 and evaluate short-latency cutaneous reflexes from the foot because they project to several 101 motor pools bilaterally via spinal interneurons intercalated between primary afferents and 102 motoneurons (Burke et al., 1971; Lundberg et al. , 1977; Pierrot -Deseilligny et al., 1981; 103 Crone et al., 1987; Jankowska, 1992) . Cutaneous inputs from the foot also exert powerful 104 effects on spinal circuits controlling loc omotion (reviewed in Zehr & Stein, 1999; Duysens 105 et al., 2000; Rossignol et al., 2006). 106 Therefore, as a first step in determining how perineal stimulation facilitates sensorimotor 107 functions in spinal animals, we investigated how mechanoreceptive inputs from the perineal 108 region interact with short-latency cutaneous reflex responses to ipsilateral and contralateral 109 muscles evoked by electrically stimulating nerves that supply the dorsal and plantar surfaces 110 of the paw in adult chronic spinal cats. We performed two methods of mechanical stimulation 111 of the perineal region : vibration and pinch.
