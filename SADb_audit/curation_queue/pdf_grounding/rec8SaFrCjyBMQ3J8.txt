Inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord 49 play an essential role in shaping motor activity by gating the transmission of sensory information 50 and setting the pattern and rhythm of motor neurons. Her e, we identify the medial deep dorsal 51 horn of the spinal cord as a “hot zone” of convergent proprioceptive and cutaneous information 52 from the hindlimb, where inhibitory neurons show increased responsiveness to sensory input and 53 are preferentially recruited during locomotion in comparison to excitatory neurons . We identify a 54 novel population of glycinergic inhibitory neurons within the deep dorsal horn that express 55 parvalbumin (dPV) and receive convergent proprioceptive and cutaneous input from the paw. We 56 show that dPVs possess intrinsic properties that support spontaneous discharge, even in the 57 absence of synaptic input. However, a drug cocktail mimicking descending input (5 -HT, 58 dopamine, NMDA) amplifies dPV output, while cutaneous and proprioceptive inputs shape the 59 temporal dynamics of dPV activity. These findings suggest dPV-mediated inhibition is modulated 60 by behavioral state and can be fine -tuned by sensory input. Using intersectional genetic 61 strategies, we selectively target spinal cord dPVs and demons trate their capacity to provide 62 divergent ipsilateral inhibition to both pre -motor and motor networks of the ventral horn, thereby 63 controlling the timing and magnitude of cutaneous -evoked muscle activity. Manipulating the 64 activity of dPVs during treadmill locomotion results in altered limb kinematics at the transition of 65 stance to swing and altered step cycle timing at increased speeds. To investigate the effects of 66 manipulating dPV activity on broader sets of motor behaviors, we used depth vision and machine 67 learning to quantify and scale naturalistic behavior. We find that although sub-movements remain 68 stable, the transitions between sub -movements are reduced, suggesting a role in movement 69 switching. This rhythmic, patterned activity can be 78 coordinated within limb’s joints (intralimb) and across limbs (interlimb); the interplay of which 79 determines locomotion gait. To adapt to changes in the environment, such as varying surfaces 80 and speeds, it is essential for gait pa rameters (rhythm, pattern, and coordination) and limb 81 kinematics (angles of joints) to be adjustable. The foundations of our understanding of locomotion 82 networks can be attributed to the work of Charles Sherrington and Graham Brown in the early 83 20th centur y1,2. Sherrington posited that locomotion operates through a series of reflexive 84 “chains”, and Brown built upon this work to suggest the existence of intrinsic spinal networks that 85 can generate “the act of progression” in the absence of sensory input . However, the physiological properties of 90 sensorimotor circuits that allow for crosstalk between sensory signals and motor pools, thereby 91 enabling flexible modulation of pre -set motor programs remains an op en question. To 92 approximate a holistic understanding of the spinal networks of locomotion, we need a functional 93 roadmap that connects multimodal sensory information from individual limbs to the motor 94 pathways capable of coordinating complex movements. 95 96 When walking, the spinal cord receives constant feedback information from two primary sensory 97 modalities. The first, proprioception, provides information on muscle state (tension, speed, and 98 location) and is detected by receptors located in muscles, joints, and tendons. The second, touch, 99 relays information about skin indentation, vibration, and slip, and is detected by cutaneous 100 afferents, low threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMRs), innervating specialized mechanosensitive 101 organs embedded in skin, bones, and joi nts such as Merkel cell, Ruffini endings, Meissner and 102 Pacinian corpuscles9. Proprioceptive and cutaneous input work in concert to shape the rhythm of 103 locomotion by adjusting the duration of each phase of the step cycle10–12. For example, during the 104 swing phase, activation of mechanoreceptors on the dorsal paw elicits a flexo r response13,14. In 105 contrast, applying the same stimuli during the stance phase promotes extension15. Such findings 106 beg the question of whether proprioceptive and cutaneous input to the spinal cord use parallel or 107 segregated pathways to modulate motor output. Studies have shown that activation of cutaneous 108 inputs can fa cilitate proprioceptive reflexes, suggesting a convergence of proprioceptive and 109 cutaneous pathways 16,17. This facilitation exceeds a simple summation of each inpu t and is 110 thought to occur through convergence onto common pathways18. 111 112 Anatomical and physiological studies indicate that processing of convergent proprioceptive and 113 cutaneous input occurs in the medial part of the deep dorsal horn of the spinal cord 19–22. This 114 region is also home to a large population of premotor in terneurons23–28,“reflex encoders'' that 115 participate in cutaneous withdrawal reflexes 29,30, and “motor synergy encoders'' that coordinate 116 motor response across multiple muscle groups 31. Consistent with this, coordinated motor output 117 can most efficiently be elicited by electrical stimulation of the medial deep dorsal horn32. Together, 118 these studies indicate that interneurons located in the medial deep dorsal horn possess the ability 119 to integrate multimodal sensory input and generate motor output, thereby engaging in multimodal 120 sensorimotor transformations. Indeed, both excitatory and inhibitory deep dorsal h orn 121 interneurons have been implicated in multimodal sensorimotor processing. For instance, the 122 excitatory dl3 pre -motor interneurons integrate proprioceptive and LTMR input to control 123 grasping33. Additionally, inhibitory interneurons expressing the Satb2 transcription factor integrate 124 proprioceptive and noxious input to regulate the nociceptive withdrawal reflex 34. Inhibitory 125 interneurons that transiently express RORβ provide presynaptic inhibition of proprioceptive 126 afferents to ensure a fluid gait during locomotion 35. Collectively, these studies show that deep 127 dorsal horn excitatory and inhibitory interneurons are key players in sensorimotor responses 128 required for coordinated motor activity.
