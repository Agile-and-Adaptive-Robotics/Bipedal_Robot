\chapter{Synthetic Nervous System Simulation}\label{ch:sns}
\graphicspath{{../Figures/60-sns/}{figs/60-sns/}{../Figures/60-sns/AnimatLab_reference/}{figs/60-sns/AnimatLab_reference/}}

The preceding chapters validate the robot's mechanics: a force model for the braided pneumatic actuators (Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}) and a corrected model of the joint torque those actuators deliver through the biomimetic knee (Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}). This chapter builds the other half of the neuromechanical system, the controller, together with the simulation pipeline required to develop it. The controller is a synthetic nervous system (SNS): a network of conductance-based neuron and synapse models whose organization follows the mammalian spinal cord \citep{rybak_modelling_2006, mccrea_organization_2008, deng_neuromechanical_2019}. The networks are not trained in the machine-learning sense; their parameters are set from physiological data and then tuned against explicit verification tests, which is what keeps the circuits interpretable: every neuron corresponds to an identifiable cell class, so the simulated system supports lesion, stimulation, and sensory-deletion experiments that cannot be performed on living subjects (Section~\ref{sec:bg_ethics}).

The chapter has a single stated goal: a verified neuromechanical laboratory for the BPA-actuated robot, meaning a set of tools in which a controller can be built in any of several simulation engines, checked against its own re-implementations in the other engines and against the published behavior of the models it descends from, and then interrogated with the stimulus and deletion experiments that the locomotion literature applies to the biological circuits themselves. The justification for every component is the experimental capability it adds, and the standard of proof for each is the minimum demonstration that shows it works: a basic reflex pathway, the replication of a published rhythm or gait, or the removal of a single pathway class. The demonstrations are deliberately kept at that scale, because a tool that works at the scale of a knee reflex or a replicated gait does not need a walking campaign to justify itself; the heavier campaigns that do appear in this chapter are reported for the questions they answer, not as evidence that the tools exist.

Two definitions cover most of the neuroscience vocabulary used below. First, the locomotor controller is a two-level central pattern generator: a rhythm generator sets the timing of the step cycle, and a pattern-formation layer shapes that timing into phase-appropriate activation of antagonist muscle groups \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}. Each leg's rhythm generator is a half-center pair, an extensor population and a flexor population that inhibit each other so that only one is strongly active at a time. Second, in the non-spiking (graded) formulation used for most of this work, each neuron is electrically a nonlinear RC circuit: synaptic current charges a membrane capacitance through a leak conductance, and the resulting membrane voltage, tens of millivolts, is both the neuron's state and its output \citep{nourse_sns_2023}. Graded chemical synapses couple these units: a depolarized presynaptic cell opens a conductance in the postsynaptic membrane whose sign makes the connection excitatory or inhibitory. The sensory channels that close the loop are the muscle afferents, Ia signaling muscle length and velocity, Ib signaling muscle force, and group II signaling length, together with heel and toe contact sensors.

Within neuromechanical simulation, the controller built here belongs to the biologically grounded SNS line, one of the three lines compared in Section~\ref{sec:bg_neuromech}; the reflex-based and learned alternatives \citep{geyer_musclereflex_2010, song_deep_2021} are treated there. The ancestry is direct: the two-level organization implemented throughout this chapter is the rat-hindlimb CPG of \citet{deng_neuromechanical_2019}, built on the full-muscle hindlimb analysis of \citet{young_analyzing_2019}, and the research community and tooling around the approach, including SNS-Toolbox \citep{nourse_sns_2023}, are surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:bg_sns}. Two-level CPG control has reached musculoskeletal macaques and muscle-driven robotic hind legs \citep{nishizaki_gait_2026, fukuoka_autonomous_2021}, though on non-humanoid platforms or with generic musculature. What this program adds is the combination the background identifies as unclaimed: an anatomically grounded two-level controller with Ia, Ib, group II, and contact feedback, driving a model whose actuators are the experimentally characterized BPAs of Chapters~\ref{ch:methods}--\ref{ch:torque}.

Building that laboratory required a simulation pipeline that did not exist: no physics engine carried the robot's custom muscle routing together with the BPA force model and an SNS. The pipeline is therefore itself a series of tools, and the chapter documents them as such. The robot's OpenSim musculoskeletal model was converted to the MuJoCo physics engine and audited against its source \citep{delp_opensim_2007, todorov_mujoco_2012}; the quasi-static BPA force model was implemented in Python so that actuator forces inside the simulation come from the validated model of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}, not from a generic muscle approximation; the two-level spinal network was built in SNS-Toolbox and coupled to the converted model with proprioceptive and heel and toe contact feedback; and a Simulink block library assembles the same component models in a standard controls-engineering environment. Because a result that holds in only one software implementation is difficult to trust, the same contact-driven rhythm core was re-implemented in Simulink and checked against its NumPy reference, and the archived AnimatLab models of the same lineage were re-run headless so that engine-level and network-level agreement could be separated; spiking-neuron mirrors of all three simulation families additionally test whether the conclusions depend on the non-spiking coding assumption. Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker} opens with the prior AnimatLab walker whose limitations motivated this verification-first approach. Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} documents each tool as built, stating what it is, why it exists, and its minimum demonstration. Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} reports those demonstrations, the replications of published studies, and the stimulus and deletion experiments the tools enable. Sections~\ref{sec:spiking_results} and~\ref{sec:disc_spiking} report and interpret the spiking comparison.

\section{Prior Work: The AnimatLab Bipedal Walker}\label{sec:prior_walker}

Before the simulation toolchain of this chapter existed, the author and Morrow constructed a neuromechanical model of a bipedal walker in AnimatLab, a graphical environment for building biomechanical systems actuated by synthetic neural systems \citep{cofer_animatlab_2010}. That model is the direct ancestor of the work reported here: its biomechanics and its two-layer controller architecture carry through every model in this chapter, and the limitations of its hand-tuned parameters are the reason the present toolchain emphasizes systematic verification.

The biomechanical model consisted of nine rigid bodies (pelvis, thigh, shank, foot, and toe segments) connected by sagittal-plane hinge joints with human-like rotational capabilities and actuated by antagonistic pairs of Hill-type muscles at the hip, knee, and ankle. The toe joint, the author's addition to the Li design, carried a passive spring instead of a muscle pair, simplifying the system. The mechanics followed the suspended bipedal walking model of \citet{li_bipedal_2017}, whose pattern generators were entrained by heel and toe mechanosensory receptors and hip-angle position sensors standing in for the group-I loading, cutaneous, and hip-joint afferents of the biological scheme; muscle afferents were not modeled. The Li model also required support against falling: a system with only planar joints cannot actively balance side to side.

The synthetic nervous network was built from non-spiking, leaky-integrator neurons and followed the two-layer CPG organization of \citet{deng_neuromechanical_2019}. A rhythm-generator layer set the timing of the system; a pattern-formation layer reshaped the timing signals into phase-appropriate activation of antagonist muscle groups; and a sensorimotor layer containing Renshaw-cell-like recurrent inhibition (motor-neuron collaterals exciting inhibitory cells that feed back onto the same pool) drove the motor neurons. The sensorimotor layer received Ia (length and velocity) and Ib (tension) afferent feedback from the muscles, but the feedback gains were never systematically tuned, and no ground-contact signals were modeled. Every applied current, synaptic weight, and muscle-tension parameter was set by hand: each was adjusted by trial and error, one at a time, until the three layers oscillated in phase and the motor neurons produced stepping-like activation of the muscle pairs. That hand-tuning consumed the bulk of the project's effort, left no record of the sensitivity of the behavior to individual parameters, and did not generalize when new feedback pathways were added; those limitations directly motivated the verification-first approach of the remainder of this chapter. Alternating, stepping-like activity and motion were demonstrated with the walker suspended in the air, and the results were presented at the 2021 NIH BRAIN Initiative Investigators Meeting \citep{bolenTorqueValuesArtificialKnee2021June15-17}.

\section{A Neuromechanical Simulation Toolchain}\label{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}

This section documents the tools of the laboratory in a fixed form: what each tool is, why it exists, and the smallest demonstration that shows it works. The tools are the AnimatLab environment in which controllers are prototyped and the lineage's reference models are archived; the OpenSim-to-MuJoCo conversion of the robot model; the BPA force interface; the SNS-Toolbox builder of the spinal networks; the Simulink block library of the SNS component models; the cross-engine verification harness; and the spiking-neuron mirrors that test the coding assumption. Quantitative results for every minimum demonstration, the replications of published studies, and the experiments the tools enable follow in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results}.

\subsection{AnimatLab Body and Controller Organization}

The walker representation and controller hierarchy are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:animatlab_model_architecture} and~\ref{fig:animatlab_working_gui}. The hierarchy separates rhythm generation, hip control, and knee--ankle pattern formation within left- and right-side neural subsystems. As a tool, this environment exists because the program's controller lineage is archived in it: the reference models whose replication is reported in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} live as AnimatLab projects, and new circuit organizations are prototyped in the same format. The current AnimatLab working model was instrumented for a new \qty{10}{\second} phase-1 run recording joint, neural, contact-sensory, and body-motion channels; that instrumented run is the tool's minimum demonstration, and its recordings are reported in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} and examined there without treating them as evidence of stable walking or effective ground-contact gating. The run is distinct from the earlier walker study of Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker}.

Since that run, the working model has been reorganized into a complete bilateral network. The right-side rhythm-generator, hip, and knee--ankle subsystems were mirrored from the left, inhibitory commissural pathways were added between the two rhythm generators, and each leg gained four biarticular muscle pathways (gastrocnemius, biceps femoris short head, semimembranosus, and rectus femoris) with human maximum isometric forces from the Gait2392 data set. The reorganized project opens in the AnimatLab environment without errors and exports a standalone simulation that runs headless. Its rhythm-generator half-centers hold a static latch under the tonic drive levels tested so far rather than oscillating, so conductance tuning toward robust oscillation is the active step; no locomotor claim is made for the reorganized model beyond its verified assembly.

Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_controller_motifs} illustrates the rhythm-generator, pattern-formation, and sensorimotor organization at the neuron and synapse level. These reference schematics complement the subsystem view by making the antagonist pathways and sensory connections visible.

\subsection{MuJoCo Conversion and Synthetic BPA Knee}

The next two tools operate in the MuJoCo engine: a conversion pipeline that moves the robot's musculoskeletal model from OpenSim into MuJoCo \citep{delp_opensim_2007, todorov_mujoco_2012}, and a force interface that makes the converted model's actuators behave as the BPAs of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} rather than as generic muscles. The conversion exists because no physics engine carried the robot's custom muscle routing natively. The MyoConverter program performs the base conversion \citep{caggiano_myosuite_2022, wang_myosim_2022}; because an automatic conversion of a model with custom muscle routing introduces specific defects, among them muscle paths that are not mirrored correctly between the two legs and joint rotation axes with reversed sign, the conversion is followed by repair scripts, short Python programs that correct each defect and re-audit the converted model against the OpenSim source. The conversion runs in a recorded software environment, with the engine and library versions fixed, so that it can be re-run from the source model and reproduced. The force interface implements the quasi-static BPA force model of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} in Python so that it runs inside the simulation loop: at each time step it reads every actuator's length and extension rate from the MuJoCo tendon paths and returns the actuator force, including the correction-factor force balance of Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}. The minimum demonstration of the pair is a synthetic knee: a minimal SNS with reciprocal inhibition driving a synthetic knee with antagonistic flexor and extensor BPAs (Figure~\ref{fig:mujoco_model_views}, which distinguishes the converted robot from this separate knee demonstration). The demonstration establishes the controller--actuator--joint interface; it does not establish SNS control of the converted robot or validated correction-factor torque predictions in MuJoCo.

The conversion and the force interface are complete and verified. The converted model carries all 92 muscle-tendon actuators, with attachment geometry audited against the OpenSim source, and the joint sign conventions of the robot's modified muscle paths were verified dynamically: each actuator was activated on its own in simulation, and the sign of the joint torque it produced identified its action at that joint. The check has to be dynamic because the attachment points of these paths move with joint configuration, so the routing cannot be read from a fixed table of moment arms. The Python implementation reproduces the MATLAB force calculation, including the correction-factor force balance. An SNS-Toolbox network couples to MuJoCo at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, matching the physics-engine timestep, with muscle force feedback entering as Ib afferent input. On this coupling, a 410-neuron two-level spinal network has been assembled for the converted model (per-leg rhythm-generator half-centers, four phase-shifted pattern-formation groups per leg, motoneuron pools for all actuators, and Ia, Ib, and II afferent populations), and its rhythm generator has been verified in isolation to oscillate at an approximately \qty{0.9}{\second} cycle with the two legs in antiphase under tonic drive. That isolated oscillation doubles as the rhythm-generator-level stimulus test: tonic drive with no phasic input evokes the alternating rhythm, the simulation-side analogue of the tonic central and sensory stimulation that facilitates involuntary air-stepping in humans \citep{selionov_tonic_2009}. Full-body closed-loop co-simulation is not yet running: leg-joint states develop non-finite values during longer simulations, with the foot-contact meshes the primary suspect under investigation.

\subsection{Simulink SNS Library and Reflex Topology}

The Simulink tool is a block library, built so that synthetic nervous systems can also be assembled and inspected in a standard controls-engineering environment. Its seven core component blocks are a non-spiking (graded) neuron, a graded chemical synapse, a spiking leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, Ia and Ib afferent encoders, muscle activation dynamics, and a generic BPA force block (Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_sns_implementation}). The blocks implement the same neuron and synapse equations used by the SNS-Toolbox networks of the preceding subsection. The full library adds a synapse-sum junction for neurons receiving more than six synaptic inputs, three diameter-specific BPA force elements (\qtylist{10;20;40}{\mm}) implementing the fitted force surfaces of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}, and a Hill-type biological muscle element, for twelve blocks before the spiking additions of Section~\ref{sec:spiking_methods}. The library and its demonstrations run in base Simulink without additional toolboxes; the block icons derive their excitatory and inhibitory symbols automatically from the sign of each synapse, in a color-blind-safe palette, and the publication figures regenerate from scripts.

The library's minimum demonstrations are two basic reflex pathways. In the first, the core blocks are assembled into a reduced-order knee-reflex model with antagonist actuation (Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_reflex_topology}): Ia stretch feedback excites the motor neuron of the stretched muscle and inhibits that of its antagonist (reciprocal inhibition), while Ib force feedback inhibits the motor neuron of the muscle that generated the force (autogenic inhibition). In the second, an arm holds a beverage container while a pour loads it, with and without the stretch reflex enabled; the reflex limits the maximum sag to \ang{2.00} against \ang{7.85} with the reflex disabled (the committed baseline values, tabulated beside their spiking twins in Table~\ref{tab:spike_beer}, Section~\ref{sec:spiking_results}). Between them, the two demonstrations show the library reproducing the canonical spinal reflex pathways at the scale where their mechanisms are visible. To couple the controller to detailed mechanical CAD, the SolidWorks assembly is exported in URDF format and imported into Simscape Multibody; the import path has been verified on a trial knee assembly, and the tendon hardware is modeled in CAD but not yet inserted into the assembly. The demonstration model retains provisional mechanical parameters, the generic BPA force block remains a placeholder, and the saved runs are therefore not used to establish quantitative knee-regulation performance.

\subsection{Cross-Platform Verification of the Contact-Driven Rhythm Core}\label{sec:crossengine_methods}

The verification harness is a tool in its own right, and its existence is the chapter's answer to a trust problem: the toolchain spans several simulation engines, and a result that holds in only one software implementation is difficult to trust. Its minimum demonstration is replication, in two forms; the same rhythm core rebuilt in two engines must reproduce the same rhythm, and the lineage's archived models must reproduce their published behavior in their original engine. The harness is defined around the contact-driven rhythm core of the Walker-2-Layer (W2L) bipedal architecture: four rhythm-generator half-centers, one extensor/flexor pair per leg, plus graded pattern-formation and sensory cells, driven by heel-contact mechanosensory feedback. Each half-center is built from spiking neurons whose persistent sodium current, a slowly inactivating inward current, terminates each burst, so the mutually inhibiting extensor and flexor populations of a pair alternate \citep{deng_neuromechanical_2019}. The same core was implemented twice from a common connectivity specification, once as a NumPy reference integration through the SNS-Toolbox backend and once as native Simulink blocks from the library of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}, and both were driven with an identical protocol: a \qty{12}{\second} run at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, tonic drive with a brief kickoff pulse, and alternating \qty{1}{\Hz} heel-contact trains. Agreement was scored on rhythm-generator burst period, left--right antiphase correlation, burst counts, and full-trace correlation against the NumPy reference. The archived AnimatLab reference models (the bilateral contact-driven model, the modern W2L model, and the Li walker) were additionally re-run headless from their standalone simulators with instrumented charts, so that engine-level agreement and network-level agreement could be separated. The protocol covers the rhythm core and the archived models only; full-network and whole-body behavior are reported as limitations alongside the results in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results}.

\subsection{Spiking-Neuron Mirrors of the Simulation Models}\label{sec:spiking_methods}

The final tool tests an assumption rather than adding a simulation capability. The controllers documented above all compute with graded (non-spiking) neurons, in which the membrane potential itself is the analog activity variable that drives the musculoskeletal model. To test that the conclusions drawn from those models are not artifacts of graded coding, spiking-neuron mirrors of all three simulation families were built, following one design rule: the motor-neuron layer stays non-spiking, because the musculoskeletal model consumes a continuous activation command, while the afferent encoders (the neurons that convert sensor signals into synaptic input), rhythm-generator half-centers, pattern-formation cells, interneurons, and commissural (cross-side) cells become leaky integrate-and-fire neurons operating at real membrane-potential ranges, so that the network carries spikes above the motor-neuron layer and analog voltages below it \citep{szczecinski_perspective_2023, nourse_sns_2023}. Event-based coding is expected to matter most in the afferent and rhythm layers, where mechanosensor onsets and phase transitions are discrete events, and least at the motor-neuron output, which the muscle activation dynamics low-pass filter anyway.

The MuJoCo--SNS-Toolbox mirror (\texttt{build\_network\_spiking.py}) reproduces the production spinal network of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} population for population and connection for connection, and is selected at build time by a single environment-variable switch so that the default build path is untouched; that the non-spiking pipeline is unchanged was proven by re-running its defaults gate and baseline evaluation to bit-exact agreement. Because SNS-Toolbox 1.5.2 ships no spiking bursting-neuron class, the rhythm-generator half-centers are adapting integrate-and-fire cells: burst termination is carried by spike-frequency adaptation of the firing threshold, whose time constant reuses the corresponding slow-inactivation value of the non-spiking build so that tuned adaptation values transfer between the two. Spiking cells rest at \qty{-70}{\mV}, spike at \qty{-50}{\mV}, and reset to \qty{-60}{\mV}, with a tonic bias current parking their equilibrium a few millivolts below threshold, the analog cells' near-threshold operating point. The network sub-steps at \qty{0.5}{\ms} (four sub-steps inside each \qty{2}{\ms} physics step) so that spike timing is resolved without changing the physics integration, and non-spiking readout taps are appended for the runner's stance gates and neural logging so that all existing analysis code reads analog levels unchanged.

The mirror's synaptic weights are set by a calibration script rather than by hand. Spikes onto the non-spiking motor neurons are calibrated by mean conductance, so that a steady presynaptic train reproduces the non-spiking steady-state motor-neuron voltage (calibration residuals at or below \qty{3e-4}{\mV}); synapses between spiking cells are calibrated by firing-rate transfer (a \qty{40}{\hertz} source drives its target at \qty{40}{\hertz}, and a calibrated inhibitory synapse silences a \qty{40}{\hertz}-driven cell); the graded-to-spiking command pathway is scaled from the measured afferent current-to-rate map. The full calibration of record, with the measured residual of every factor, is tabulated in Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations} (Table~\ref{tab:app_spk_cal}).

In the Simulink family, three blocks were added additively to the SNS library of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} (for fifteen blocks in total): a \texttt{SpikingNeuron} with the same internal synaptic summation as the non-spiking block, an event-wired \texttt{SpikingSynapse} whose conductance jumps by a fixed increment per presynaptic spike and saturates at its maximum, and a \texttt{HybridSpikingSynapse} that carries the same conductance dynamics onto a non-spiking postsynaptic cell. The blocks were verified against the analytic single-spike conductance decay (maximum deviation \qty{6.2e-07}{\micro\siemens}), against a verbatim re-implementation of the SNS-Toolbox update equations on a hybrid two-neuron circuit (identical spike count and maximum spike-time deviation \qty{11.75}{\micro\second}), and against the SNS-Toolbox numpy backend itself (mean interspike-interval difference \qty{0.19}{\micro\second}). Spiking twins of the knee-reflex and beverage-holding demonstrations were then built by copy-and-patch: only the sensory interneuron layer and its synapses are replaced by spiking cells, while the motor neurons, muscles, joints, and measurements are identical to the committed demonstrations, and the twin is run at an operating point matched to the baseline gains.

In the AnimatLab family, spiking copies of the runnable standalone exports were produced by a conversion script that rewrites copies of the model files, leaving every original file untouched: neuron thresholds are moved from the graded regime (unreachably positive) into the spiking regime, every graded chemical synapse type is re-emitted as a per-spike synapse with the same identity and amplitude (interpreted as a per-spike conductance increment with \qty{10}{\ms} decay), and the per-connection class tag that AnimatLab stores independently of the synapse type is flipped to match. A calibrated variant sets each cell class's threshold just below its measured graded peak, and an ablation that converts the thresholds but keeps the graded synapses isolates which change does what. Each copy passes a structural validator (neuron, connection, synapse-type, chart, and stimulus sets identical to its original, and every class tag consistent) and runs headless to its configured end. The 2023 spiking walker model of the same lineage, which was tuned as a rate code from birth, serves as the spiking reference baseline for this family.

Each mirror is verified against its non-spiking twin by a fixed gate set rather than by inspection: a topology gate requires the connection multiset to be identical between the two builds under three gain configurations; a rhythm gate requires self-sustained alternating rhythm under constant drive with the period within \qty{20}{\percent} of the analog build; a basin gate requires the rhythm to persist under \qty{1}{\percent} multiplicative jitter on every scalar parameter; an air-regime smoke gate compares cadence, duty, and joint ranges of the deafferented suspended-air preparation; and a regression gate requires every committed non-spiking artifact to reproduce its recorded values exactly with the spiking blocks present in the library. The mirrors carry mapped parameters only, with no new tuning, so their results (Section~\ref{sec:spiking_results}) establish what transfers structurally and what requires re-tuning, and any fresh tuning campaign is future work.

\section{Tool Demonstrations and Experiments}\label{sec:preliminary_sim_results}

This section reports what the tools do, ordered by the questions that justify them. The AnimatLab instrumented run demonstrates the prototyping environment's recording capability. The cross-engine verification and the headless reruns replicate known behavior: the rhythm core must reproduce itself across engines, and the archived models must reproduce their published rhythms and gaits. The BPA-actuated walker demonstrates the full MuJoCo chain end to end. The gait-validation sweep then measures, honestly, how far the tuned networks remain from human reference locomotion. The final subsection presents the pathway-removal and drive-perturbation campaigns as the laboratory's stimulus and deletion experiments. Failures are reported with the same weight as successes throughout.

\subsection{AnimatLab Phase-1 Recordings}

The phase-1 run described in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} is the AnimatLab tool's minimum demonstration: it exercises the instrumented model and the recording chain end to end. \subsection{Cross-Engine Verification of the Contact-Driven Rhythm Core}\label{sec:crossengine_results}

Between the NumPy reference and the native Simulink reimplementation of the W2L rhythm core, both legs' rhythm-generator burst periods matched at \qty{1.000}{\second} versus \qty{1.000}{\second} (0.0\% error), the left--right antiphase correlation reproduced the reference exactly ($r = -0.525$), and all ten expected bursts occurred in the scored window. The full voltage traces cross-correlate at $r = 0.99996$--$0.99998$ at a $-\qty{2}{\milli\second}$ lag with an RMSE of 0.099--0.111~mV (Figure~\ref{fig:w2l_crossengine}); the residual lag is consistent with the one-step delay of the discrete Simulink solver.

The same protocol also runs the chapter's first deletion experiment, and it surfaced an architectural fact rather than a defect: with the heel-contact trains removed, the full network holds a static latch under tonic drive with kickoff alone, in every implementation, so phasic contact feedback is part of the drive architecture of the W2L design, not an optional perturbation. The result is a load dependence that echoes the decerebrate-cat locomotion reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:bg_neural}, and it is the reason the pathway-removal experiments of Section~\ref{sec:walker_variant_results} treat the contact channels as a pathway class rather than as a boundary condition.

\subsection{Headless Validation of the AnimatLab Reference Models}\label{sec:animatlab_validation}

The second half of the harness's minimum demonstration is replication of published results in the original engine. The archived AnimatLab reference models were re-run headless from their standalone simulators with instrumented charts, and eleven targetable rhythm and gait metrics were extracted automatically; all eleven pass their acceptance bands. In the bilateral contact-driven model, the rhythm generators oscillate at periods of \qty{0.450}{\second} (left) and \qty{0.467}{\second} (right) with a left--right lag of \qty{0.529}{cycle} (cross-correlation $r = 0.976$), confirming alternating gait timing. In the modern W2L model, the joints oscillate at \qty{2.25}{\Hz}; its flexor half-centers peak at $-\qty{56.4}{\mV}$ and never cross the $-\qty{55}{\mV}$ threshold, so the rhythm is carried by clean extensor/flexor antiphase ($r = -0.996$) through the graded pathway rather than by flexor spiking. The Li-model rerun \citep{li_bipedal_2017} walks at \qty{0.637}{\meter\per\second} with right and left step periods of \qty{1.299 \pm 0.024}{\second} and \qty{1.333 \pm 0.080}{\second} and duty factors of 0.443 and 0.555, reproducing the published behavior of the original model in its original engine.

\subsection{BPA-Actuated Walker in MuJoCo}\label{sec:bpa_walker}

The MuJoCo chain, conversion pipeline plus force interface plus network builder, is demonstrated end to end by a BPA-actuated walker. The walker geometry of Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker} was exported to MuJoCo and driven by antagonistic BPA actuator pairs under the split contact-driven network, with actuator forces computed by the BPA model of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}. In the air-stepping gate, with the harness carrying 70\% of the \qty{411}{\N} body weight, the walker produced clean knee-flexion excursions at a \qty{1.542}{\second} period with a knee flexion range of \ang{63.5}, peak BPA forces of \qty{880}{\N} (left) and \qty{1372}{\N} (right) at a peak pressure of \qty{372}{\kPa} (60\% of the \qty{620}{\kPa} ceiling), and 7 and 5 heel-contact episodes on the left and right. This run demonstrates the complete chain from the characterized BPA force model to a multi-joint walker stepping under synthetic neural control, with actuator sizing operating inside the characterized pressure range.

The same campaign reported its failures with equal weight. On the ground, the W2L gate model stands (full-support stance) but does not walk: heel duty is zero and forward displacement is $+\qty{0.001}{\meter}$ over the run. The MuJoCo re-implementation of the Li model produced no stance episodes in any of seven drop-protocol variants. Together with the latch result of Section~\ref{sec:crossengine_results}, these negatives localize the open problem precisely: the contact-driven rhythm architecture itself transfers across engines intact, but translating it to unsupported ground locomotion requires the balance and weight-bearing layers defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}, and those layers are not present in these models.

\subsection{Stimulus and Deletion Experiments on the Walker Networks}\label{sec:walker_variant_results}

The locomotor physiology that this program's circuits imitate was established with two interventions, deletion and stimulation, and the laboratory's first experiments apply the same logic at the levels the two-level architecture defines. In the reference works, removing proprioceptive feedback degrades locomotor patterns in mice \citep{akay_degradation_2014}, proprioceptive input regulates and adapts locomotor activity \citep{lam_role_2002}, and tonic central and sensory stimulation facilitates involuntary air-stepping in humans \citep{selionov_tonic_2009}; the two-level CPG models themselves reproduce specific deletion phenomena, such as spontaneous deletions of motoneuron activity during which the locomotor period and phase are maintained \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}. The experiments implemented here are the simulator-side analogues that the assembled tools make inexpensive: removal of an entire pathway class from a fully tuned walker, re-evaluated on the tuning reference, and stepping of the tonic drive about its tuned value. They act at the rhythm-generator level (tonic drive, interleg commissural pathways, and the weak rhythm-generator mutual excitation), at the sensorimotor level (Renshaw cells), and at the afferent level (Ia, Ib, group II, and contact channels). Systematic stimulation series at the pattern-formation and motoneuron levels, and the motoneuron skipping-deletion phenomenon itself, are defined as verification tests in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework} and have not yet been run; the experiments below are therefore reported as pathway deletions and drive stimuli, labeled by the level at which each acts.

The pathway-removal sweep (Table~\ref{tab:walker_ablations}) removes individual pathway classes from each configuration and re-evaluates the tuning-reference score. In the production configuration, removing the Ia afferent pathway freezes the model outright: the evaluation detects no gait cycles and returns the fixed $-25$ fallback. Removing the Renshaw cells, the group-II afferents, or all afferent classes at once degrades the score to $-267.3$, $-255.2$, and $-267.8$, respectively, against the full model's $-186.4$, while removing the Ib, contact, or interleg commissural channels individually shifts the score by smaller amounts toward zero ($-180.3$, $-183.9$, and $-174.9$). The six-synergy variant behaves differently: with all afferents removed it scores $-190.5$ against its full model's $-200.4$ and still walks bilaterally, with ten cycles on each leg, a knee-flexion minimum of \ang{-71.2}, and a \qty{0.684}{\second} period, so its afferent complement is not what sustains its gait. The W2L-layout variant depends on both the Ia pathway (removed: $-273.5$) and the weak rhythm-generator mutual excitation (removed: $-279.9$) against its full model's $-144.5$ with four right-leg and six left-leg cycles, while removing its Renshaw cells leaves a bilateral gait at $-142.4$.

Read against the reference physiology, the sweep's structure is familiar even where its details are specific to these networks. Deleting all afferent classes from the production configuration degrades its gait without abolishing it, which is the qualitative outcome of the deafferentation experiments \citep{akay_degradation_2014}; the network that matches that picture most closely is the six-synergy variant, which keeps a bilateral gait with every afferent class removed. The Ia-only removal freezing the production configuration is a stronger dependence than the animal experiments show, and it marks that network's tuning as built around its length feedback. The degradation under group-II removal is consistent with proprioceptive regulation of the locomotor pattern \citep{lam_role_2002}, and the equally large degradation under Renshaw-cell removal indicates that the recurrent-inhibition loop carries weight in the tuned production network. No experiment here reproduces the motoneuron-level deletion phenomena of the two-level CPG models \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}; the deletions act at the afferent, interneuron, and rhythm-generator levels, and extending them to the pattern-formation and motoneuron levels is scheduled with the verification tests of Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}.

The drive-perturbation and push tests are the stimulus side. The production configuration never fell in 15 stand and push evaluations (maximum push sway \qty{0.123}{\meter}), and its walk survives drive perturbations of $+0.01$, $-0.01$, and $-0.02$ (scores of $-201.9$, $-177.0$, and $-171.2$), freezing only at $+0.02$; its pruned derivative survives the unperturbed evaluation ($-176.1$) and the $-0.02$ perturbation ($-168.2$). The six-synergy and W2L-layout variants fall under every $\pm 0.01$ and $\pm 0.02$ drive perturbation tested, with pelvis tilt reaching \ang{39} to \ang{40}. In stimulus terms, a step change of the tonic drive within this range leaves the production lineage walking while the two variant lineages fall. The sweep therefore identifies the production configuration and its pruned derivative as the robust pair of the family, and locates the fragility of the six-synergy and W2L-layout variants under drive perturbation.

\subsection{MuJoCo Spinal-Network Mirror}

The non-spiking production baselines were first re-established on the machine of record: all three current production configurations reproduce their documented evaluation scores exactly, including the default build's kinematic score of $-161.56754173676563$, and after the mirror's selector was added the default build gate ($(410, 376, 1186)$ populations, inputs, and connections) and that same baseline score reproduce digit for digit, proving the non-spiking pipeline untouched.

The topology gate passes exactly: under the default, fully tuned, and joint-layer pattern-formation gain configurations, the spiking mirror's directed connection multiset is identical to the non-spiking build's ($1{,}186$, $7{,}908$, and $7{,}762$ connections respectively), with identical input lists, identical motor-neuron maps, and exactly the expected number of readout taps (12, 12, and 16). The mirror is structurally the same network.

Under constant drive (network alone, \qty{20}{\second}), the rhythm gate splits. \subsection{AnimatLab Spiking Conversions}

The AnimatLab family produces the campaign's clearest negative result, and it is reported as such. The graded baselines were re-established first: the two-layer walker's rhythm generator oscillates at \qty{0.443}{\second} (\qty{2.26}{\hertz}), the bilateral variant at \qty{0.462}{\second} with the two legs folded near antiphase (lag $-0.42$ cycle, $r = 0.869$), and the already-spiking 2023-lineage model, the family's spiking reference, bursts at \qty{1.305}{\second} with folded antiphase of $+0.494$ cycle ($r = 0.703$), reproducing its period of record. The current reorganized project's standalone export remains latched under the tonic drives examined, consistent with Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}.

No spiking copy of the three graded models preserves the rhythm (Table~\ref{tab:spike_animatlab}). The two-layer conversions die at every threshold and decay tried (two or zero rhythm-generator spikes in \qty{5}{\second}); the bilateral variant latches with one side's half-centers saturated at \qty{442}{\hertz}--\qty{450}{\hertz}; and the reorganized project's copy converts its latched baseline into strictly synchronous \qty{11.8}{\hertz} co-bursting with no alternation: the left extensor and flexor half-centers emit identical 58-spike trains whose spike-count envelopes correlate at $r = 1.000$ at zero lag. The thresholds-only ablation, which keeps the graded synapses, preserves near-baseline joint oscillations, isolating the synapse conversion as the change that breaks the rhythm (Figure~\ref{fig:spike_animatlab}): per-spike conductances at the graded amplitude either starve the reciprocal loop of inhibitory charge or saturate it, whereas the graded synapses read the membrane potential continuously and keep oscillating through subthreshold voltages. A spiking version of this family therefore needs its synaptic amplitudes and decays re-tuned as a rate code, which is how the 2023 spiking reference was built in the first place, rather than copied parameter by parameter.

\section{What Spiking Changes and Does Not}\label{sec:disc_spiking}

The spiking-mirror campaign (Sections~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} and~\ref{sec:spiking_results}) allows the dissertation's neural-coding assumption to be examined directly, and its outcome divides cleanly into what the coding regime changes and what it leaves alone.

What spiking does not change is the behavior of the controlled model, provided the design rule and the calibration hold. Because the motor-neuron layer stays non-spiking and the spikes onto it are calibrated by mean conductance, the interface that the actuator and torque models of this dissertation attach to, a continuous activation command per muscle, is unchanged by construction. The Simulink twins make the point quantitatively: with only the sensory interneuron layer converted, the knee-reflex transient is identical, the settle band moves by \ang{0.9}, and the beverage-holding reflex modulation moves by \ang{0.02}. To the musculoskeletal model, a calibrated \qty{40}{\hertz} train and the graded voltage it replaces are the same command; the activation dynamics of the muscle model filter the spike train the same way the biological membrane would. The actuator-level and joint-level conclusions of Chapters~\ref{ch:methods}--\ref{ch:results} therefore do not depend on the neural coding chosen above the motor-neuron layer.

What spiking changes first is timing. The mirror's self-sustained rhythm runs \qty{55.6}{\percent} slower than the analog build at the same constant drive, and its air-stepping cadence is roughly ten times faster than the non-spiking twin's in that preparation, deviations in opposite directions in the two tests, which is itself informative, since a single scaling error would move both the same way. The campaign traced the constant-drive period to two identifiable knobs, the threshold-increment magnitude and the adaptation time constant, and demonstrated alternation/period trade-offs along them; the untuned starting point, not a structural failure, is the honest reading. Any period-matched spiking controller is therefore a short tuning campaign away, and the mirror's mapped parameters are the correct starting point for it.

What spiking changes second is robustness, in the mirror's favor. The analog network's constant-drive rhythm sits close to a bifurcation, a parameter region where an exact re-implementation in a different integration stack can settle to the tonic fixed point instead of oscillating, so marginal winners risk not surviving a solver change. The spiking half-centers passed the perturbation basin gate in full, with the rhythm persisting under \qty{1}{\percent} jitter on every scalar parameter including the spiking-specific ones, at $0.89\times$ of the baseline swing. For the hardware-transfer program of Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}, where the network will be re-implemented on embedded hardware with finite-precision arithmetic, a rhythm whose basin is wide is the safer substrate, and the sub-stepping cost of resolving spikes (\qty{0.5}{\milli\second} network steps inside the unchanged \qty{2}{\milli\second} physics step) is modest.

What spiking does not tolerate is a naive parameter copy at the synapse level. The AnimatLab conversions show that converting graded synaptic amplitudes to per-spike increments of the same magnitude breaks the reciprocal half-center loop outright (the loop is either starved of inhibitory charge or saturated), while converting only the thresholds changes nothing, and the 2023 spiking walker of the same lineage walks because it was tuned as a rate code from birth. Synaptic strength in a spiking network is a rate-code quantity that must be derived from the presynaptic firing rate and the conductance decay, exactly as the MuJoCo mirror's calibration script derives it, not copied from the graded value. This is the one place where the campaign's negative results should temper expectations: spiking conversion is an exercise in re-derivation, and the calibration-first, gate-everything procedure of Section~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} is the transferable method.

Finally, the one place where the untuned spiking mirror outperforms its tuned non-spiking twin, producing a bilateral ground gait where the winner's left leg is frozen, is a single run with no statistics, and it is claimed only as a lead. But it points at the mechanism the design rule was after: the mirror's afferents are event-coded, its adaptation is spike-history-based, and its inhibitory charge arrives in discrete quanta, so the phase relationships that the analog build locks in around a frozen leg are not the only stable operating regime of the same topology. Establishing whether that regime survives tuning, and whether event-coded contact afferents are what unlock it, is precisely the closed-loop CPG comparison program defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}, and the mirror is now the instrument for running it.

The chapter's accounting, beyond the coding question, is the one its opening stated. The laboratory exists and its parts are demonstrated at their minimum scale: reflex pathways in Simulink, a replicated rhythm core and replicated published walkers across engines, an end-to-end BPA-actuated walker in MuJoCo, and a first set of deletion and stimulus experiments whose outcomes can be compared with the deafferentation and stimulation literature. Its limits are on record with equal weight: full-body closed-loop co-simulation is blocked by non-finite leg-joint states, the archived walkers do not walk unsupported on the ground, the tuned networks transfer poorly to diverse human reference cycles, and the motoneuron-level deletion phenomena of the reference models are not yet reproduced. What the tools are for is the program they define next, in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}: the balance and weight-bearing layers that unsupported walking requires, and the systematic stimulation series at the motoneuron, pattern-formation, and rhythm-generator levels against which the circuits' signs, gains, and phase structure will be validated.
