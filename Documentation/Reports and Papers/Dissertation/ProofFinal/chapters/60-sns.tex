I first transferred a published neural controller to a different biomechanical model and adjusted it by hand. The combined model produced alternating neural activity and air stepping, but not weight-bearing walking. This result motivated more efficient ways to test alternative controllers, first on the complex Gait2392 human model and then on a robot counterpart with BPAs. This chapter presents the resulting neuromechanical models, graphical neural blocks, simulation interfaces, and controlled experiments.

\section{The AnimatLab Bipedal Walker}\label{sec:prior_walker}

For a course project in ME 610: Neuromechanical Modeling, Morrow and I combined the bipedal mechanics of \citet{li_bipedal_2017} with the two-level central pattern generator (CPG) of \citet{deng_neuromechanical_2019} in AnimatLab \citep{cofer_animatlab_2010}. The mechanical model had a pelvis and bilateral thigh, shank, foot, and toe segments, with sagittal-plane joints and antagonist Hill-type muscles at the hip, knee, and ankle. I added a passive toe joint. The initial controller used one rhythm generator (RG) to control both sides, with hip and knee/ankle pattern-formation (PF) groups for each leg. We added afferent feedback at the motoneuron (MN) level. Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_model_architecture} shows the model and controller organization.

We adjusted applied currents, synaptic weights, and muscle parameters by hand. The suspended model developed alternating muscle activity and air stepping, presented at the 2021 NIH BRAIN Initiative Investigators Meeting \citep{bolenTorqueValuesArtificialKnee2021June15-17}. It did not achieve weight-bearing walking.

I subsequently replaced the shared rhythm generator with one rhythm generator on each side and linked the two through commissural pathways. The bilateral pattern-formation and motor organization was already present in the original model. I added afferent feedback to the RG and PF layers, and heel- and toe-contact signals during the subsequent development. This later model is referred to as the Walker Two-Layer (W2L) model. Joint recordings from a separate instrumented AnimatLab configuration are provided in Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_phase1_preliminary}; further circuit schematics and implementation records are in Appendix~\ref{app:simulation_verification}.

I found AnimatLab's graphical block editor usable but dated. I wanted a more efficient way to modify connections, try alternative controllers, and evaluate their interaction with the many joints and actuators of the Gait2392 model and its BPA-routed robot counterpart.

\section{Attempting W2L Integration in MATLAB}\label{sec:w2l_matlab_attempt}

I later explored MATLAB as one route to those controller experiments, using Simulink for the neural controller and Simscape Multibody for the mechanical model. This required interfaces for neural activation, muscle force, joint motion, and sensory feedback. Importing a mechanism and obtaining neural oscillation separately did not establish the behavior of their coupled model.

The MATLAB work therefore became a sequence of component and coupling tests. Selected neural implementations reproduced their reference responses, and mechanical import provided models that could be assembled and simulated. More extensive tests also exposed limits: a full-network export agreed with its reference over an early transient but later diverged, and one Simulink realization reached a steady, nonoscillating state under constant neural drive while its NumPy reference oscillated (Appendix~\ref{sec:sns_ports}). Those results did not establish a complete W2L walking implementation in MATLAB. They showed why importing a model or reproducing a short trace could not substitute for testing the neural dynamics, actuator action, and feedback loop separately.

I subsequently developed both block-programmed and scripted implementations, allowing controller experiments through SNS-Simscape or MuJoCo with SNS-Toolbox.

\subsection{Li--Deng/W2L Walker and Controller}\label{sec:sns_w2l_methods}\label{sec:crossengine_results}\label{sec:sns_feedback_test}

For cross-implementation checks of the contact-driven W2L rhythm core, I used a common connectivity specification and identical drive and contact inputs in NumPy and Simulink. Closed-loop experiments held a saved controller fixed while changing selected sensory pathways, drive, or support. Appendix~\ref{app:connectomes} documents the bilateral pathways and conductances of the independently replayed W2L configuration.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} The contact-driven W2L rhythm core was implemented in NumPy and native Simulink blocks from a common connectivity specification. Both implementations received the same input protocol. Burst periods matched at \qty{1.000}{\second}, all ten expected bursts were reproduced, and voltage-trace correlation was at least 0.99996 (Figure~\ref{fig:w2l_crossengine}). The agreement is specific to this circuit and input protocol.

Removing the phasic heel-contact inputs caused the circuit to latch in both implementations. The alternating activity therefore depends on the specified sensory drive.

\begin{samepage}
The saved W2L controller was tested with all saved effective gains delivered to the compiled network. With controller, drive, and support settings fixed, disabling all sensory feedback shifted median bilateral contact phase from 0.498 to 0.223 cycles. A phase of 0.5 cycles indicates alternating foot loading; the supported-stepping criterion accepts 0.35--0.65 cycles. Forward progression and joint cycling persisted, but the feedback-disabled condition failed this criterion for supported stepping (Figure~\ref{fig:sns_ablation_pair}). Over the \qtyrange{4.5}{30}{\second} scored window, forward displacement was \qty{2.239}{\meter} with feedback and \qty{1.996}{\meter} without it. Thus, the demonstrated feedback contribution is to bilateral contact coordination, rather than the existence of oscillation or forward motion. Additional pathway deletions and stimulus tests are summarized in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_w2l_perturbations} (Figure~\ref{fig:app_w2l_perturbations}).\par
\end{samepage}

\subsection{Gait2392-Based Models}\label{sec:sns_existing_tools}\label{sec:sns_gait_comparison_main}

My first conversion attempt used the robot model, which combined BPA routing on the Gait2392 skeleton with unchanged human muscle parameters. MyoConverter's muscle-equilibrium checks rejected infeasible combinations of routed length and muscle parameters. I therefore separated conversion of the human reference from reconstruction of the robot routing. This distinction matters because an error caused by an artificial-actuator route should not be interpreted as a property of the original human model.

The human-model conversion also required specific repairs. Three small hip rotators per side, the quadratus femoris, gemelli, and piriformis, were disabled at model load. The converted model retains 92 actuator entries, but these six contribute no force. The converted knee retains the OpenSim flexion-negative sign convention. Spatial frame transformations and individual-actuator tests check joint motion and torque directions; the CAD-derived model uses the anatomical frame specified in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_cad_supported_stance}.

The human-model repairs addressed coupled moving muscle-path points, patella tracking, joint limits, and unintended contacts. Conflicting limits were removed from coupled path-point joints while anatomical driver limits were retained; rectus femoris required a patella-tracking via point, and contact was restricted to intended foot--ground pairs. These choices restore specified geometry and contact behavior rather than establish general model equivalence. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_conversion_repairs} records the repairs and force comparison.

Comparisons with the OpenSim attachment paths and single-actuator tests checked the transferred geometry and force directions, though not that every muscle reproduces the source force throughout its range. The neural and mechanical models exchange signals at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} solver time step.

For artificial-muscle simulations, I implemented an actuator-force calculation that evaluates the quasi-static BPA model from Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles} at the current simulated actuator length and commanded pressure. The calculated tensile force acts along the model's specified tendon route. Length, velocity, force, and foot-contact measurements are then returned to the neural controller through sensory channels. This closes the neural-command--actuator-force--sensory-feedback loop. The calculation represents the measured static force dependence; it does not add valve-flow or pressure-transient dynamics. Routing geometry and dynamic force transmission therefore require their own checks beyond the isometric force fit.

The SADb of Chapter~\ref{ch:database} supplies the physiological context for selecting Ia, II, Ib, and contact pathways. A database link identifies the supporting observation; it does not by itself determine a numerical gain or guarantee the same function in another model. The implemented encoding, connection sign, phase gating, and tested response therefore remain explicit parts of the controller specification.

The human Gait2392 reference retains biological-muscle actuators, while the robot counterpart uses custom artificial-muscle paths and BPA forces. The Li--Deng/W2L walker and the SolidWorks-derived robot are separate mechanical models. Figure~\ref{fig:mujoco_model_views} contrasts the converted Gait2392 robot with the reduced-order knee model used for block demonstrations. Appendix~\ref{app:simulation_verification} distinguishes their configurations and support conditions.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} The conversion checks test the quantities needed to interpret a neural-control experiment: the direction of each muscle's joint action, the coupled motion of path points, the force scale, and the intended ground contacts. They exposed reversed hip actions, inconsistent path-point limits, unintended contact pairs, and incorrect force scales in individual actuator entries. Correcting those faults made the converted human model usable for the subsequent controller tests. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_conversion_repairs} gives the individual repairs and the full force comparison.

At matched poses and OpenSim static-optimization activations (Figure~\ref{fig:force-compare}; Table~\ref{tab:force-r2}), eight of twelve sampled muscles had force-prediction $R^2$ between 0.87 and 0.98 and mean forces within \qty{10}{\percent} of the reference. Rectus femoris and biceps femoris short head had negative $R^2$, indicating errors greater than a constant mean-force prediction. This comparison checks force predictions in the repaired MyoConverter-derived human model while identifying remaining muscle-specific differences. Because the comparison sets pose and activation frame by frame without reproducing trajectory velocities or tendon dynamics, it does not establish dynamic equivalence between the muscle formulations. That distinction matters when interpreting feedback based on muscle force.

The Gait2392-based evaluations use the same reference-library and pathway-removal procedures to compare alternative neural organizations. The full graded, pruned, six-synergy, and W2L-layout configurations show different responses to feedback removal and different agreement with the tuning and library references. Removing Ia feedback eliminated detected cycles in the tested production configuration, whereas other removals and other network organizations had different effects. The library comparisons also exposed asymmetric activity and limited transfer beyond the tuning reference (Appendix~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}). This demonstrates that the models and experiment drivers can discriminate controller dependence and transfer limits; the comparison does not establish normal human gait.

A replay of the saved human-model controller produced ten detected cycles per leg. Figure~\ref{fig:gait2392_ik_overlay} compares the joint trajectories directly with the Gait2392 inverse-kinematics reference and its associated ground-reaction forces. The model's mean stance fractions were 0.71 and 0.65 on the right and left, compared with 0.61 in the reference. Substantial hip and knee timing differences remain visible despite the detected cycles. The overlay therefore complements the scalar gait score by showing which parts of the movement disagree. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_ik_overlay} reports the angle errors and alignment method.

\subsection{SNS-Simscape Block Library and Editor}\label{sec:sns_new_tools}\label{sec:sns_simulink_demos}

The SNS-Simscape library implements neuron, synapse, afferent-encoder, activation, and muscle-force elements as editable Simulink blocks. The library retains the conductance-based neuron and synapse formulation supplied by SNS-Toolbox; it does not introduce a new neuron model. Verification uses selected circuits under matched inputs. Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_sns_implementation} shows the core blocks and graded-neuron signal flow.

The library also supports reduced-order demonstrations in which the sensory pathway and mechanical response can be inspected directly.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} The library assembles Ia stretch excitation, reciprocal inhibition, and Ib autogenic inhibition in a reduced-order antagonist knee model (Figure~\ref{fig:sns_reflex_current}C). After the model reaches a steady operating point, a \qty{2}{\nano\ampere}, \qty{30}{\milli\second} current pulse at the extensor Ia sensory-neuron input evokes \ang{1.09} of extension, peaking \qty{111}{\milli\second} after pulse onset. The knee then returns toward the matched no-stimulus trajectory, meeting the specified recovery thresholds after \qty{6.355}{\second}. The displayed configuration broadens the MN and Ia synaptic saturation ramps; the original narrow-ramp settings maintained oscillations. This is a transient test of the implemented feedback and actuator equations, rather than a physiologically calibrated tendon-tap model. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_knee_transient} gives the configuration and numerical checks, with the complete response in Figure~\ref{fig:app_knee_transient}.

\begin{samepage}
A second demonstration tests feedback in an arm holding a beverage container as it is loaded (Figure~\ref{fig:sns_beverage_current}). With the reflex enabled, arm sag was \ang{2.00}, compared with \ang{7.85} when it was disabled. As the forearm sags, biceps stretch feedback excites the biceps motoneuron and reciprocally inhibits the triceps motoneuron. The separate Ib pathways provide autogenic inhibition of each muscle. The reduced sag demonstrates a corrective effect of the implemented sensory pathways in the coupled neural and actuator blocks.\par

\subsection{MuJoCo--Simulink Integration}\label{sec:sns_bridge_methods}

The MathWorks MuJoCo--Simulink bridge provides an alternative to importing the mechanical model into Simscape. I connected SNS-Simscape neural outputs to the bridge's actuator-command interface and returned muscle length, velocity, force, and joint measurements to the controller. This arrangement allows a block-programmed neural controller to act on an existing MuJoCo model. The integration tests compare activation and sensor readback against a Python reference before interpreting the coupled motion. The bridge supplies the simulation interface; the neural and actuator integrations and their verification are the work described here.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} The MathWorks MuJoCo--Simulink bridge exchanges activation commands and mechanical measurements between a Simulink controller and an existing MuJoCo mechanical model. I paired this bridge with SNS-Simscape and tested single-muscle activation, different control rates, and sensor readback against a Python integration. Readback was bit-exact over the scored window. An exported neural network also drove all 92 actuator channels during a \qty{5}{\second} run without afferent return; a separate closed-loop reflex experiment exercised both commands and sensory measurements. These tests verify signal exchange through the interface. Under constant neural drive, one Simulink circuit realization reached a steady, nonoscillating state while its NumPy reference oscillated, showing why successful signal exchange must be followed by circuit-specific dynamical checks before transferring tuned parameters.

\subsection{Curriculum and Experiment Drivers}\label{sec:sns_curriculum_main}\label{sec:sns_repeatability_main}

The larger models require a tuning procedure as well as a working interface. I organized the evaluations as a staged curriculum, beginning with suspended rhythm generation, adding sensory feedback and standing requirements, and progressing to supported ground evaluations (Table~\ref{tab:sns_curriculum}). This ordering separates a failure to generate rhythmic commands from a failure to support or coordinate the mechanical model. Free network gains, which scale connection strengths and sensory contributions, were searched with a tree-structured Parzen estimator. This sequential search uses earlier simulation results to propose new parameter combinations; each candidate is then evaluated through simulation. Saved configurations and fixed evaluation protocols allow the candidate to be replayed before a new condition is introduced.

The experiment drivers then hold a selected controller fixed while changing sensory pathways, drive, mechanical support, or external perturbations. Finalists are also replayed under small parameter perturbations. Gait scoring requires detected cycles before a score is interpreted as movement quality; unsuccessful runs cannot be promoted by a reserved numerical failure value. The reference-library comparison tests transfer beyond the tuning reference. These procedures provide a repeatable way to identify where a controller works and which changes disrupt it, rather than making a single favorable trajectory the endpoint of tuning. Detailed scoring and configuration records are in Appendix~\ref{sec:sns_tuning_methods}.

\subsection{Literature Circuits and Spiking Variants}\label{sec:sns_literature_methods}\label{sec:sns_literature_results}\label{sec:layer_stimulus_main}

Selected literature-derived circuits were tested with matched inputs and layer-specific stimuli before interpreting coupled motion. The reduced Rybak circuit was tested without mechanics; the Deng-derived circuit drove a suspended W2L model. Spiking variants retained a continuous motor-activation interface while changing the neural dynamics. Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations} gives their equations and calibration, and Appendix~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} gives the comparisons.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} A complementary neural-only test distinguishes mechanisms that can produce a missing motor burst. In the reduced Rybak implementation, pattern-formation stimulation suppresses flexor motor output while the rhythm generator continues; rhythm-generator stimulation shifts the subsequent phase. Reproducing that distinction demonstrates the use of controlled interventions to test circuit organization before adding mechanical coupling (Appendix~\ref{sec:rybak_verification}).

A missing motor burst can arise from a disturbance of the rhythm generator or from suppression farther downstream. I applied stimuli separately to the RG, PF, and MN layers of a reduced Deng-derived circuit \citep{deng_neuromechanical_2019,nourse_sns_2023}. A resetting deletion suppresses expected motor activity and shifts the subsequent rhythm; a non-resetting deletion suppresses activity while preserving the original schedule. Matched baseline and stimulus runs distinguish the rhythm-generator clock from the motor output.

Stimuli lasting two baseline periods suppressed two expected flexor motor bursts at each layer. RG stimulation shifted both RG and motor timing by 0.211 cycles, whereas PF stimulation and direct MN inhibition preserved the original schedule. A separate one-second PF stimulus shifted motor timing by 0.208 cycles while leaving the RG clock unchanged. Thus, a motor phase shift alone did not establish resetting of the rhythm generator. The smaller-current sweep produced no observable deletions. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_layer_stimulus} gives the protocols and all stronger-current controls; Figure~\ref{fig:layer_stimulus_validation} shows representative neural responses. These are controlled tests of a translated circuit coupled to a suspended model, rather than a replication of the original rat's locomotion.

\section{Discussion: What the Tools Establish}\label{sec:sns_discussion}

Matched neural traces support the tested circuit and input protocol. Successful command and sensor exchange through the bridge establishes the interface needed to couple models, while the fixed-controller W2L comparison identifies a sensory-feedback contribution to bilateral contact coordination. The loaded-arm comparison gives a directly interpretable corrective response in the coupled neural and actuator blocks. These results answer different questions and should be interpreted at their respective scales.

The implemented workflow supports controlled circuit comparisons and virtual-harness-assisted evaluations of the human and BPA robot models. It makes alternative controllers accessible to repeated tests with specified mechanics, sensory pathways, and assistance. Chapter~\ref{ch:overall} connects these capabilities with the actuator characterization, torque models, and SADb, then considers their use in broader robot behavior and hardware experiments.
