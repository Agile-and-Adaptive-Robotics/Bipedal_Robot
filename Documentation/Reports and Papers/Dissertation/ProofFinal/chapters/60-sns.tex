I combined a published neural controller with a different biomechanical model and adjusted the controller by hand. Alternating neural activity and air stepping emerged, but the model did not achieve weight-bearing walking. This experience motivated tools for transferring, training, and testing neural controllers more efficiently. The objective was to compare biomimetic controllers on the Gait2392 human model, then carry those control principles to a robot model actuated by BPAs. This chapter follows that progression from the AnimatLab experiment to the models, graphical tools, simulation interfaces, and training procedures summarized in Table~\ref{tab:sns_capability}.

\section{The AnimatLab Bipedal Walker}\label{sec:prior_walker}

For a course project in ME 610: Neuromechanical Modeling, Morrow and I combined the bipedal mechanics of \citet{li_bipedal_2017} with the two-level central pattern generator (CPG) of \citet{deng_neuromechanical_2019} in AnimatLab \citep{cofer_animatlab_2010}. The mechanical model had a pelvis and bilateral thigh, shank, foot, and toe segments, with sagittal-plane joints and antagonist Hill-type muscles at the hip, knee, and ankle. I added a passive toe joint. The initial controller used one rhythm generator (RG) to control both sides, with hip and knee/ankle pattern-formation (PF) groups for each leg. We added afferent feedback at the motoneuron (MN) level. Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_model_architecture} shows the model and controller organization.

Morrow and I adjusted applied currents, synaptic weights, and muscle parameters by hand. In suspension, antagonist muscle activity alternated and the legs cycled through air stepping. We presented this result at the 2021 NIH BRAIN Initiative Investigators Meeting \citep{bolenTorqueValuesArtificialKnee2021June15-17}. The experiment established coupled neural and limb motion and exposed the difficulty of transferring a controller to different mechanics and loading conditions.

I subsequently replaced the shared rhythm generator with one rhythm generator on each side and linked the two through commissural pathways. The bilateral pattern-formation and motor organization was already present in the original model. I added afferent feedback to the RG and PF layers, and heel- and toe-contact signals during the subsequent development. This later model is referred to as the Walker Two-Layer (W2L) model. Aligned neural and joint recordings from a separate instrumented AnimatLab configuration are provided in Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_phase1_preliminary}; further circuit schematics and implementation records are in Appendix~\ref{app:simulation_verification}.

I found AnimatLab's graphical block editor usable but dated. I wanted a more efficient way to modify connections, try alternative controllers, and evaluate their interaction with the many joints and actuators of the Gait2392 model and its BPA-routed robot counterpart.

\section{From Air Stepping to Controller Transfer}\label{sec:w2l_matlab_attempt}

A controller transferred to another mechanical model encounters different muscle lengths, moment arms, inertias, and sensory signals. Gains that produce useful activation in the source model may produce weak motion, excessive cocontraction, or incorrectly timed feedback in the new one. Air stepping provided a first test because it revealed the relationship between antagonist neural activity and limb motion without requiring the legs to support body weight. Ground contact then introduced load-bearing and interleg coordination requirements that the manually tuned model had not met.

I needed to make this transfer process repeatable: construct a neural circuit, connect its commands and sensory pathways to a specified mechanical model, train it in stages, and inspect the resulting neural and mechanical responses together. That requirement led to the complementary scripted and graphical tools below. Their purpose is to make alternative neural controllers practical to test on the higher-fidelity Gait2392 model and, subsequently, on a robot model with BPA actuators.

\section{Knowledge Gaps and the New Tools}\label{sec:sns_gaps}

The knowledge gap is how to adapt a biomimetic neural controller developed for one neuromechanical model to another efficiently, while preserving an interpretable relationship between neural activity, sensory feedback, and movement. The Gait2392 human model provides a detailed musculoskeletal reference on which different controller organizations can be compared. A robot counterpart adds artificial-muscle routing and the BPA force model from Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}, allowing the same workflow to investigate how actuator mechanics change control.

Repeated training and perturbation experiments require scripted execution. A headless simulation runs without opening a graphical window, so a program can set parameters, run a model, and collect results automatically. An application programming interface (API) provides the commands and data interfaces for those operations; a software development kit (SDK) supplies supporting libraries and tools. These interfaces allow repeated evaluation without manual interaction.

Graphical construction addresses a complementary need: changing and inspecting neural pathways as connected blocks. For this work, Python and SNS-Toolbox supplied programmable model construction and fast simulation, while MATLAB, Simulink, and Simscape supplied the graphical user interface (GUI), block programming, and mechanical assembly tools used to build SNS-Simscape. Combining them allowed graphical controller development alongside automated tests. Table~\ref{tab:sns_capability} gives the order of the models and tools demonstrated in this chapter.

\subsection{Li--Deng/W2L Walker and Controller}\label{sec:sns_w2l_methods}\label{sec:crossengine_results}\label{sec:sns_feedback_test}

For cross-implementation checks of the contact-driven W2L rhythm core, I used a common connectivity specification and identical drive and contact inputs in NumPy and Simulink. Closed-loop experiments held a saved controller fixed while changing selected sensory pathways, drive, or support. Appendix~\ref{app:connectomes} documents the bilateral pathways and conductances of the independently replayed W2L configuration.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} The contact-driven W2L rhythm core was implemented in NumPy and native Simulink blocks from a common connectivity specification. Both implementations received the same input protocol. Burst periods matched at \qty{1.000}{\second}, all ten expected bursts were reproduced, and voltage-trace correlation was at least 0.99996 (Figure~\ref{fig:w2l_crossengine}). The agreement is specific to this circuit and input protocol.

Removing the phasic heel-contact inputs caused the circuit to latch in both implementations. The alternating activity therefore depends on the specified sensory drive.

\begin{samepage}
The saved W2L controller was tested with all saved effective gains delivered to the compiled network. With controller, drive, and support settings fixed, disabling all sensory feedback shifted median bilateral contact phase from 0.498 to 0.223 cycles. A phase of 0.5 cycles indicates alternating foot loading; the supported-stepping criterion accepts 0.35--0.65 cycles. Forward progression and joint cycling persisted, but the feedback-disabled condition failed this criterion for supported stepping (Figure~\ref{fig:sns_ablation_pair}). Over the \qtyrange{4.5}{30}{\second} scored window, forward displacement was \qty{2.239}{\meter} with feedback and \qty{1.996}{\meter} without it. Thus, the demonstrated feedback contribution is to bilateral contact coordination, rather than the existence of oscillation or forward motion. Additional pathway deletions and stimulus tests are summarized in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_w2l_perturbations} (Figure~\ref{fig:app_w2l_perturbations}).\par
\end{samepage}

\subsection{Gait2392-Based Models}\label{sec:sns_existing_tools}\label{sec:sns_gait_comparison_main}

I converted the Gait2392 human reference from OpenSim to MuJoCo with MyoConverter and connected it to SNS-Toolbox. The robot counterpart required separate treatment: its BPA paths could not simply be combined with unchanged human-muscle parameters. I therefore retained biological-muscle actuators in the human reference and implemented routed BPA forces for the robot counterpart.

The converted human model retains 92 actuator entries. Six small hip-rotator entries, the quadratus femoris, gemelli, and piriformis on each side, are disabled and contribute no force. The knee retains OpenSim's flexion-negative convention. Before interpreting a controller's motion, I checked muscle attachments, joint-action directions, force scale, and intended foot contact. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_conversion_repairs} records the conversion repairs and force comparison. Neural and mechanical signals are exchanged at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} solver time step.

For artificial-muscle simulations, I implemented an actuator-force calculation that evaluates the quasi-static BPA model from Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles} at the current simulated actuator length and commanded pressure. The calculated tensile force acts along the model's specified tendon route. Length, velocity, force, and foot-contact measurements are then returned to the neural controller through sensory channels. This closes the neural-command--actuator-force--sensory-feedback loop. The calculation represents the measured static force dependence; it does not add valve-flow or pressure-transient dynamics. Routing geometry and dynamic force transmission therefore require their own checks beyond the isometric force fit.

The SADb of Chapter~\ref{ch:database} supplies the physiological context for selecting Ia, II, Ib, and contact pathways. A database link identifies the supporting observation; it does not by itself determine a numerical gain or guarantee the same function in another model. The implemented encoding, connection sign, phase gating, and tested response therefore remain explicit parts of the controller specification.

Table~\ref{tab:sns_model_configurations} distinguishes the model bodies, actuation, and representative tests. A controller organization, such as the W2L layout, can be applied to more than one body; its name does not identify the mechanical model. Figure~\ref{fig:mujoco_model_views} contrasts the converted Gait2392 robot with the reduced-order knee model used for block demonstrations.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} Single-actuator and matched-pose tests checked the converted mechanics before closed-loop controller tests. These checks separated conversion faults from changes caused by neural control.

At matched poses and OpenSim static-optimization activations (Figure~\ref{fig:force-compare}; Table~\ref{tab:force-r2}), eight of twelve sampled muscles had force-prediction $R^2$ between 0.87 and 0.98 and mean forces within \qty{10}{\percent} of the reference. Rectus femoris and biceps femoris short head had negative $R^2$, indicating errors greater than a constant mean-force prediction. This comparison checks force predictions in the repaired MyoConverter-derived human model while identifying remaining muscle-specific differences. Because the comparison sets pose and activation frame by frame without reproducing trajectory velocities or tendon dynamics, it does not establish dynamic equivalence between the muscle formulations. That distinction matters when interpreting feedback based on muscle force.

The Gait2392-based evaluations use the same reference-library and pathway-removal procedures to compare alternative neural organizations. The full graded, pruned, six-synergy, and W2L-layout configurations show different responses to feedback removal and different agreement with the tuning and library references. The library comparisons also exposed asymmetric activity and limited transfer beyond the tuning reference (Appendix~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}). These tests distinguish how each controller uses feedback and how its motion changes when the reference is changed.

A replay of the saved human-model controller produced ten detected cycles per leg. \subsection{SNS-Simscape Block Library and Editor}\label{sec:sns_new_tools}\label{sec:sns_simulink_demos}

The SNS-Simscape library implements neuron, synapse, afferent-encoder, activation, and muscle-force elements as editable Simulink blocks. The library retains the conductance-based neuron and synapse formulation supplied by SNS-Toolbox; it does not introduce a new neuron model. Verification uses selected circuits under matched inputs. Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_sns_implementation} shows the core blocks and graded-neuron signal flow.

I used reduced-order reflex demonstrations to check the graphical blocks in a coupled model before assembling a full locomotor controller. In these examples, the sensory pathway, neural response, and mechanical correction can be inspected directly.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} The library assembles Ia stretch excitation, reciprocal inhibition, and Ib autogenic inhibition in a reduced-order antagonist knee model (Figure~\ref{fig:sns_reflex_current}\textbf{(C)}). After the model reaches a steady operating point, a \qty{2}{\nano\ampere}, \qty{30}{\milli\second} current pulse at the extensor Ia sensory-neuron input evokes \ang{1.09} of extension, peaking \qty{111}{\milli\second} after pulse onset. The knee then returns toward the matched no-stimulus trajectory, meeting the specified recovery thresholds after \qty{6.355}{\second}. The displayed configuration broadens the MN and Ia synaptic saturation ramps; the original narrow-ramp settings maintained oscillations. This is a transient test of the implemented feedback and actuator equations, rather than a physiologically calibrated tendon-tap model. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_knee_transient} gives the configuration and numerical checks, with the complete response in Figure~\ref{fig:app_knee_transient}.

\begin{samepage}
A second demonstration tests feedback in an arm holding a beverage container as it is loaded (Figure~\ref{fig:sns_beverage_current}). With the reflex enabled, arm sag was \ang{2.00}, compared with \ang{7.85} when it was disabled. As the forearm sags, biceps stretch feedback excites the biceps motoneuron and reciprocally inhibits the triceps motoneuron. The separate Ib pathways provide autogenic inhibition of each muscle. The reduced sag demonstrates a corrective effect of the implemented sensory pathways in the coupled neural and actuator blocks.\par
\end{samepage}

\subsection{MuJoCo--Simulink Integration}\label{sec:sns_bridge_methods}

The MathWorks MuJoCo--Simulink bridge couples a block-programmed neural controller to a MuJoCo mechanical model. SNS-Simscape outputs supply actuator commands, and muscle length, velocity, force, and joint measurements return to the controller. A controller can therefore be edited graphically in Simulink and tested with MuJoCo mechanics. I implemented and tested the neural and actuator signal connections supplied through this bridge.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} The MathWorks MuJoCo--Simulink bridge exchanges activation commands and mechanical measurements between a Simulink controller and an existing MuJoCo mechanical model. I paired this bridge with SNS-Simscape and tested single-muscle activation, different control rates, and sensor readback against a Python integration. Readback was bit-exact over the scored window. An exported neural network also drove all 92 actuator channels during a \qty{5}{\second} run without afferent return; a separate closed-loop reflex experiment exercised both commands and sensory measurements. These tests verify signal exchange through the interface. Under constant neural drive, one Simulink circuit realization reached a steady, nonoscillating state while its NumPy reference oscillated, showing why successful signal exchange must be followed by circuit-specific dynamical checks before transferring tuned parameters.

\subsection{Curriculum and Experiment Drivers}\label{sec:sns_curriculum_main}\label{sec:sns_repeatability_main}

The training workflow combines the four neural-training operations described by \citet{hunt_development_2017} with my five-stage physical curriculum (Table~\ref{tab:sns_curriculum}). The neural operations establish intrinsic rhythm, tune its entrainment to expected afferent signals, fit feedforward motor activation, and refine that activation with sensory feedback. The physical stages then test the coupled model in deafferented air motion, afferented air motion, standing, supported ground stepping, and refinement with reduced assistance. A controller must be assessed at each physical stage rather than advancing because its neural activity alone appears rhythmic.

For the gain searches, I used a tree-structured Parzen estimator to propose parameter combinations from earlier evaluations. Each candidate is tested through simulation, and saved configurations allow a selected controller to be replayed before the next condition is introduced.

The experiment drivers then hold a selected controller fixed while changing sensory pathways, drive, mechanical support, or external perturbations. Finalists are also replayed under small parameter perturbations. Gait scoring requires detected cycles before a score is interpreted as movement quality; unsuccessful runs cannot be promoted by a reserved numerical failure value. The reference-library comparison tests transfer beyond the tuning reference. These procedures provide a repeatable way to identify where a controller works and which changes disrupt it, rather than making a single favorable trajectory the endpoint of tuning. Detailed scoring and configuration records are in Appendix~\ref{sec:sns_tuning_methods}.

With the Gait2392 human model, one trained configuration passed deafferented and afferented air-motion checks and assisted standing. Transfer to alternating load-bearing ground steps remains unresolved. These outcomes distinguish completion of particular physical tasks from completion of the full curriculum.

\subsection{Literature Circuits and Spiking Variants}\label{sec:sns_literature_methods}\label{sec:sns_literature_results}\label{sec:layer_stimulus_main}

The model interfaces and experiment drivers also support translating circuits from the literature and testing their mechanisms. I used a reduced Rybak circuit for neural-only interventions and a Deng-derived circuit coupled to a suspended W2L model. Separate spiking variants retain a continuous motor-activation interface while changing the neural dynamics. Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations} gives the neuron equations and calibration, and Appendix~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} reports the comparisons.

\noindent\textbf{Verification.} A complementary neural-only test distinguishes mechanisms that can produce a missing motor burst. In the reduced Rybak implementation, pattern-formation stimulation suppresses flexor motor output while the rhythm generator continues; rhythm-generator stimulation shifts the subsequent phase. Reproducing that distinction demonstrates the use of controlled interventions to test circuit organization before adding mechanical coupling (Appendix~\ref{sec:rybak_verification}).

A missing motor burst can arise from a disturbance of the rhythm generator or from suppression farther downstream. I applied stimuli separately to the RG, PF, and MN layers of a reduced Deng-derived circuit \citep{deng_neuromechanical_2019,nourse_sns_2023}. A resetting deletion suppresses expected motor activity and shifts the subsequent rhythm; a non-resetting deletion suppresses activity while preserving the original schedule. Matched baseline and stimulus runs distinguish the rhythm-generator clock from the motor output.

Stimuli lasting two baseline periods suppressed two expected flexor motor bursts at each layer. RG stimulation shifted both RG and motor timing by 0.211 cycles, whereas PF stimulation and direct MN inhibition preserved the original schedule. A separate \qty{1}{\second} PF stimulus shifted motor timing by 0.208 cycles while leaving the RG clock unchanged. Thus, a motor phase shift alone did not establish resetting of the rhythm generator. The smaller-current sweep produced no observable deletions. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_layer_stimulus} gives the protocols and all stronger-current controls; Figure~\ref{fig:layer_stimulus_validation} aligns the neural hierarchy with joint responses during a PF stimulus. These are controlled tests of a translated circuit coupled to a suspended model, rather than a replication of the original rat's locomotion.

\subsection{Execution Speed and Actuator Implementations}\label{sec:sns_execution}

Repeated controller searches benefit from reducing neural and mechanical computation separately. I accelerated the neural equations with compiled kernels and checked their outputs against the reference implementations. In a 980-neuron timing test, the sparse kernel was 11.23 times faster than the preceding preallocated neural backend, with identical voltages. A complete \qty{16}{\second} moving-leg pilot decreased from \qty{69.51}{\second} to \qty{15.86}{\second} of wall time while preserving every saved trajectory array. A separate afferented pilot decreased from \qty{113.74}{\second} to \qty{34.59}{\second} under concurrent machine load. These measurements distinguish neural-kernel acceleration from the runtime of the coupled model.

Actuator mechanics introduce a separate trade-off. The implementations include the converted biological-muscle models and the quasi-static BPA force calculation; a compliant-tendon prototype adds dynamic fiber and tendon equilibrium. In an isolated benchmark, 92 compliant muscle units advanced \qty{16}{\second} of simulated time in \qty{3.55}{\second} after compilation, approximately 4.51 times real time. This faster-than-real-time result concerns the muscle bank, rather than the complete walker. The additional equilibrium calculation increases cost relative to rigid-tendon actuation. These alternatives allow actuator fidelity and execution cost to be examined separately when selecting a model for controller training or eventual hardware control.

The integration culminates in the SOLIDWORKS-derived robot assembly coupled in SNS-Simscape to a synthetic neural controller and BPAs using my force model, with a virtual harness assisting standing. This example combines graphical neural construction, CAD mechanics, artificial-muscle forces, and sensory return in one model. It provides a starting point for studying the transition from supported posture to coordinated movement.

\section{Discussion: What the Tools Establish}\label{sec:sns_discussion}

The resulting workflow connects graphical neural construction, detailed mechanics, alternative actuator laws, and automated experiments. The W2L and Gait2392 examples show how neural activity and joint motion can be compared before and after a controlled change. The reflex demonstrations provide smaller tests of sensory correction, while the curriculum organizes the harder transition from suspended motion to supported ground behavior. Together, these tools make the transfer problem identified by the original AnimatLab experiment accessible to repeatable tests.

The implemented workflow supports controlled circuit comparisons and virtual-harness-assisted evaluations of the human and BPA robot models. It makes alternative controllers accessible to repeated tests with specified mechanics, sensory pathways, and assistance. Chapter~\ref{ch:overall} connects these capabilities with the actuator characterization, torque models, and SADb, then considers their use in broader robot behavior and hardware experiments.
