\chapter{Synthetic Nervous System Simulation}\label{ch:sns}
\graphicspath{{../Figures/60-sns/}{figs/60-sns/}{../Figures/60-sns/AnimatLab_reference/}{figs/60-sns/AnimatLab_reference/}}

The preceding chapters validate the robot's mechanics: a force model for the braided pneumatic actuators (Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}) and a corrected model of the joint torque those actuators deliver through the biomimetic knee (Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}). This chapter builds the other half of the neuromechanical system, the controller, together with the simulation pipeline required to develop it. The controller is a synthetic nervous system (SNS): a network of conductance-based neuron and synapse models whose organization follows the mammalian spinal cord \citep{rybak_modelling_2006, mccrea_organization_2008, deng_neuromechanical_2019}. Its parameters are not trained in the machine-learning sense; they are set from physiological data and then tuned against explicit verification tests, which is what keeps the circuits interpretable. Every neuron corresponds to an identifiable cell class, so the simulated system supports lesion, stimulation, and sensory-deletion experiments that cannot be performed on living subjects (Section~\ref{sec:bg_ethics}).

Two definitions cover most of the neuroscience vocabulary used below. First, the locomotor controller is a two-level central pattern generator: a rhythm generator sets the timing of the step cycle, and a pattern-formation layer shapes that timing into phase-appropriate activation of antagonist muscle groups \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}. Each leg's rhythm generator is a half-center pair, an extensor population and a flexor population that inhibit each other so that only one is strongly active at a time. Second, in the non-spiking (graded) formulation used for most of this work, each neuron is electrically a nonlinear RC circuit: synaptic current charges a membrane capacitance through a leak conductance, and the resulting membrane voltage, tens of millivolts, is both the neuron's state and its output \citep{nourse_sns_2023}. Graded chemical synapses couple these units: a depolarized presynaptic cell opens a conductance in the postsynaptic membrane whose sign makes the connection excitatory or inhibitory. The sensory channels that close the loop are the muscle afferents, Ia signaling muscle length and velocity, Ib signaling muscle force, and group II signaling length, together with heel and toe contact sensors.

Within neuromechanical simulation, the controller built here belongs to the biologically grounded SNS line, one of the three lines compared in Section~\ref{sec:bg_neuromech}; the reflex-based and learned alternatives \citep{geyer_musclereflex_2010, song_deep_2021} are treated there. The ancestry is direct: the two-level organization implemented throughout this chapter is the rat-hindlimb CPG of \citet{deng_neuromechanical_2019}, built on the full-muscle hindlimb analysis of \citet{young_analyzing_2019}, and the research community and tooling around the approach, including SNS-Toolbox \citep{nourse_sns_2023}, are surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:bg_sns}. Two-level CPG control has reached musculoskeletal macaques and muscle-driven robotic hind legs \citep{nishizaki_gait_2026, fukuoka_autonomous_2021}, though on non-humanoid platforms or with generic musculature. What this program adds is the combination the background identifies as unclaimed: an anatomically grounded two-level controller with Ia, Ib, group II, and contact feedback, driving BPA-actuated models characterized in Chapters~\ref{ch:methods}--\ref{ch:torque}.

This chapter concerns tooling: its object is a verified neuromechanical laboratory for the BPA-actuated robot. In that laboratory a controller can be built in any of several simulation engines, checked against its own re-implementations in the other engines and against the published behavior of the models it descends from, and then interrogated with the stimulus and deletion experiments that the locomotion literature applies to the biological circuits themselves. None of the infrastructure components is invented here: OpenSim, MuJoCo, MyoConverter, SNS-Toolbox, AnimatLab, Simulink and Simscape Multibody, SCONE, and the Sensory Afferent Database (SADb) of Chapter~\ref{ch:database} all existed before this program, and each is used for what it does best. What is new is the combination: the full-lower-body pipeline that converts the robot's OpenSim model to MuJoCo and couples it to an SNS-Toolbox spinal network; a derivative SNS block library for Simulink and Simscape Multibody; the Deng-lineage and Li-lineage controllers re-implemented across those programs and onto the converted models; and the SolidWorks robot assembly brought into Simscape Multibody. Re-running an existing model in the environment it was built in, as the prior walker was run in AnimatLab, reproduces the original publication and adds nothing; re-implementing the same controller in a second and third environment, and checking it against the first, is what makes the circuits portable objects rather than artifacts of one simulator, and the engine differences encountered on the way are part of the result rather than a nuisance. The demonstrations are therefore kept at the scale of a knee reflex or a replicated gait, because a tool that works at that scale needs no walking demonstration to be validated; the heavier evaluation series that do appear are reported for the questions they answer.

Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker} opens with the prior AnimatLab walker whose limitations motivated the verification-first approach. Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} is the methods section: the existing tools used, the new tools built on them, and the tuning and robustness methodology. Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} reports the demonstrations, the replications of published studies, and the stimulus and deletion experiments the tools enable; Section~\ref{sec:spiking_results} reports the spiking-neuron mirrors. Section~\ref{sec:sns_discussion} then discusses the training curriculum and its scoring, the integration of the BPA force model, the controller comparison on the robot models together with a precise statement of what walks and what does not, and the BPA-actuated robot, before closing with the new tools and the program they enable for Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}.

\section{Prior Work: The AnimatLab Bipedal Walker}\label{sec:prior_walker}

Before the simulation toolchain of this chapter existed, the author and Morrow constructed a neuromechanical model of a bipedal walker in AnimatLab, a graphical environment for building biomechanical systems actuated by synthetic neural systems \citep{cofer_animatlab_2010}. That model is the direct ancestor of the work reported here: its biomechanics and its two-layer controller architecture carry through every model in this chapter, and the limitations of its hand-tuned parameters are the reason the present toolchain emphasizes systematic verification.

The biomechanical model consisted of nine rigid bodies (pelvis, thigh, shank, foot, and toe segments) connected by sagittal-plane hinge joints with human-like rotational capabilities and actuated by antagonistic pairs of Hill-type muscles at the hip, knee, and ankle. The toe joint, the author's addition to the Li design, carried a passive spring instead of a muscle pair, simplifying the system. The mechanics followed the suspended bipedal walking model of \citet{li_bipedal_2017}, whose pattern generators were entrained by heel and toe mechanosensory receptors and hip-angle position sensors standing in for the group-I loading, cutaneous, and hip-joint afferents of the biological scheme; muscle afferents were not modeled. The Li model also required support against falling: a system with only planar joints cannot actively balance side to side.

The synthetic nervous network was built from non-spiking, leaky-integrator neurons and followed the two-layer CPG organization of \citet{deng_neuromechanical_2019}. A rhythm-generator layer set the timing of the system; a pattern-formation layer reshaped the timing signals into phase-appropriate activation of antagonist muscle groups; and a sensorimotor layer containing Renshaw-cell-like recurrent inhibition (motor-neuron collaterals exciting inhibitory cells that feed back onto the same pool) drove the motor neurons. The sensorimotor layer received Ia (length and velocity) and Ib (tension) afferent feedback from the muscles, but the feedback gains were never systematically tuned, and no ground-contact signals were modeled. Every applied current, synaptic weight, and muscle-tension parameter was set by hand: each was adjusted by trial and error, one at a time, until the three layers oscillated in phase and the motor neurons produced stepping-like activation of the muscle pairs. That hand-tuning consumed the bulk of the project's effort, left no record of the sensitivity of the behavior to individual parameters, and did not generalize when new feedback pathways were added; those limitations directly motivated the verification-first approach of the remainder of this chapter. Alternating, stepping-like activity and motion were demonstrated with the walker suspended in the air, and the results were presented at the 2021 NIH BRAIN Initiative Investigators Meeting \citep{bolenTorqueValuesArtificialKnee2021June15-17}.

\section{Methods: A Neuromechanical Simulation Toolchain}\label{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}

This section documents the laboratory in build order: the existing tools it stands on, the new tools built from them, each new tool's construction and fundamental demonstration, and the methodology by which the controllers are tuned and checked. Quantitative results for every fundamental demonstration, the replications of published studies, and the experiments the tools enable follow in Sections~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} and~\ref{sec:spiking_results}.

\subsection{Existing Tools and Data Resources}\label{sec:sns_existing_tools}

The laboratory stands on tools this program did not build. OpenSim supplies the musculoskeletal modeling side, the robot's source model, and the moment-arm and torque standards the conversions are audited against \citep{delp_opensim_2007, seth_opensim_2018}. MuJoCo supplies the physics engine \citep{todorov_mujoco_2012}, and MyoConverter performs the base OpenSim-to-MuJoCo conversion \citep{caggiano_myosuite_2022, wang_myosim_2022}. SNS-Toolbox builds and integrates the spinal networks \citep{nourse_sns_2023}. AnimatLab hosts the controller lineage's archived reference models and remains the graphical prototyping environment \citep{cofer_animatlab_2010}. Simulink and Simscape Multibody supply the controls-engineering environment into which the SNS component models and the CAD-derived bodies are ported. SCONE provides the reference point for mature predictive neuromuscular simulation \citep{geijtenbeek_scone_2019}: its published balance and gait demonstrations were reproduced headless as a practice baseline, and the standing-balance stage of the training curriculum (Section~\ref{sec:sns_curriculum}) is modeled on its balance tutorial.

The SADb of Chapter~\ref{ch:database} is the data resource among the existing tools \citep{bolen_sensory_2023}. Its segments are the five-table Airtable base (Papers, Feedback, Models, Review Papers, and Recorders), the laboratory's Zotero libraries that feed it, citation mining in the Research Rabbit style that expanded it, and the laboratory's own packaged explorer application that distributes it. The SADb framed this chapter's benchmarks and validation measurements: its pathway records fix what each afferent class encodes and what each connection rule does, so the sensory channels, the reflex topologies, and the acceptance phenomena against which the controllers are validated (the deafferentation and stimulation results cited in Section~\ref{sec:walker_variant_results}) are looked up rather than re-derived from the primary literature.

\subsection{New Tools}\label{sec:sns_new_tools}

Four tool families are new, and each exists because a needed combination did not. First, the full-lower-body pipeline: the robot's OpenSim musculoskeletal model converted to MuJoCo, audited against its source, and coupled to an SNS-Toolbox spinal network through a BPA force interface, with proprioceptive and heel and toe contact feedback closing the loop. Second, the SNS-Simscape library: the neuron, synapse, afferent-encoder, and actuator models of the SNS formalism as masked Simulink blocks, together with the imported SolidWorks and OpenSim bodies that give them real geometry to control. Third, the controller ports: the Deng-organization two-level network, the contact-driven W2L core, and the Li walker re-implemented across NumPy, Simulink, MuJoCo, and Simscape Multibody and onto the converted models, so that the same controller runs under different realizations in different programs. Fourth, the verification instruments: the cross-engine harness, the circuit-structure audits, and the spiking-neuron mirrors that test whether conclusions depend on the graded-coding assumption.

Because a result that holds in only one software implementation is difficult to trust, the same contact-driven rhythm core was re-implemented in Simulink and checked against its NumPy reference, the archived AnimatLab models of the same lineage were re-run headless so that engine-level and network-level agreement could be separated, and spiking-neuron mirrors of all three simulation families test the coding assumption. The justification for every component is the experimental capability it adds, and the standard of proof for each is the fundamental demonstration that shows it works: a basic reflex pathway, the replication of a published rhythm or gait, or the removal of a single pathway class.

\subsection{AnimatLab Body and Controller Organization}

The walker representation and controller hierarchy are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:animatlab_model_architecture} and~\ref{fig:animatlab_working_gui}. The hierarchy separates rhythm generation, hip control, and knee--ankle pattern formation within left- and right-side neural subsystems. As a tool, this environment exists because the program's controller lineage is archived in it: the reference models whose replication is reported in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} live as AnimatLab projects, and new circuit organizations are prototyped in the same format. The current AnimatLab working model was instrumented for a new \qty{10}{\second} phase-1 run recording joint, neural, contact-sensory, and body-motion channels; that instrumented run is the tool's fundamental demonstration, and its recordings are reported in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} and examined there without treating them as evidence of stable walking or effective ground-contact gating. The run is distinct from the earlier walker study of Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker}.

Since that run, the working model has been reorganized into a complete bilateral network. The right-side rhythm-generator, hip, and knee--ankle subsystems were mirrored from the left, inhibitory commissural pathways were added between the two rhythm generators, and each leg gained four biarticular muscle pathways (gastrocnemius, biceps femoris short head, semimembranosus, and rectus femoris) with human maximum isometric forces from the Gait2392 data set. The reorganized project opens in the AnimatLab environment without errors and exports a standalone simulation that runs headless. Its rhythm-generator half-centers hold a static latch under the tonic drive levels tested so far rather than oscillating, so conductance tuning toward robust oscillation is the active step; no locomotor claim is made for the reorganized model beyond its verified assembly.

\subsection{MuJoCo Conversion and Synthetic BPA Knee}

The next two tools operate in the MuJoCo engine: a conversion pipeline that moves the robot's musculoskeletal model from OpenSim into MuJoCo \citep{delp_opensim_2007, todorov_mujoco_2012}, and a force interface that makes the converted model's actuators behave as the BPAs of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} rather than as generic muscles. The conversion exists because no physics engine carried the robot's custom muscle routing natively. The MyoConverter program performs the base conversion \citep{caggiano_myosuite_2022, wang_myosim_2022}; because an automatic conversion of a model with custom muscle routing introduces specific defects, among them muscle paths that are not mirrored correctly between the two legs and joint rotation axes with reversed sign, the conversion is followed by repair scripts, short Python programs that correct each defect and re-audit the converted model against the OpenSim source. The conversion runs in a recorded software environment, with the engine and library versions fixed, so that it can be re-run from the source model and reproduced. The force interface implements the quasi-static BPA force model of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} in Python so that it runs inside the simulation loop: at each time step it reads every actuator's length and extension rate from the MuJoCo tendon paths and returns the actuator force, including the correction-factor force balance of Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}. The fundamental demonstration of the pair is a synthetic knee: a minimal SNS with reciprocal inhibition driving a synthetic knee with antagonistic flexor and extensor BPAs (Figure~\ref{fig:mujoco_model_views}, which distinguishes the converted robot from this separate knee demonstration). The demonstration establishes the controller--actuator--joint interface; it does not establish SNS control of the converted robot or validated correction-factor torque predictions in MuJoCo.

The conversion and the force interface are complete and verified. The converted model carries all 92 muscle-tendon actuators, with attachment geometry audited against the OpenSim source, and the joint sign conventions of the robot's modified muscle paths were verified dynamically: each actuator was activated on its own in simulation, and the sign of the joint torque it produced identified its action at that joint. The check has to be dynamic because the attachment points of these paths move with joint configuration, so the routing cannot be read from a fixed table of moment arms. The Python implementation reproduces the MATLAB force calculation, including the correction-factor force balance. An SNS-Toolbox network couples to MuJoCo at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, matching the physics-engine timestep, with muscle force feedback entering as Ib afferent input. On this coupling, a 410-neuron two-level spinal network has been assembled for the converted model (per-leg rhythm-generator half-centers, four phase-shifted pattern-formation groups per leg, motoneuron pools for all actuators, and Ia, Ib, and II afferent populations), and its rhythm generator has been verified in isolation to oscillate at an approximately \qty{0.9}{\second} cycle with the two legs in antiphase under tonic drive. That isolated oscillation doubles as the rhythm-generator-level stimulus test: tonic drive with no phasic input evokes the alternating rhythm, the simulation-side analogue of the tonic central and sensory stimulation that facilitates involuntary air-stepping in humans \citep{selionov_tonic_2009}.

The sensory channels that close the loop trace to the SADb of Chapter~\ref{ch:database} \citep{bolen_sensory_2023}: the afferent classes the controller monitors, Ia carrying length and velocity, Ib carrying force, and group II carrying length, together with the heel and toe contact receptors that mark the stance--swing boundary, are the classes the database's curated records were assembled to cover, and the signal encoding and the connection rules each channel implements follow from those records (Tables~\ref{tab:sadb_afferents} and~\ref{tab:sadb_pathways}) rather than from ad hoc literature searches at wiring time.

Full-body closed-loop co-simulation runs on this coupling: the four production walking configurations of Section~\ref{sec:gait_library_results} complete \qty{16}{\second} ground evaluations with gait cycles detected and scored. Early full-body runs developed non-finite leg-joint states during longer simulations; those faults were traced to the converted model's moving-attachment followers, whose sampled range limits fought the coupling constraints that tie each follower to its path point, and repairing those constraints resolved them. The ground evaluations run under the support harness of the tuning pipeline, and unsupported walking is not among the claimed results (Section~\ref{sec:sns_controller_comparison} states exactly what walks and what does not).

\subsection{Simulink SNS Library and Reflex Topology}

The Simulink tool is a block library, built so that synthetic nervous systems can also be assembled and inspected in a standard controls-engineering environment. Its seven core component blocks are a non-spiking (graded) neuron, a graded chemical synapse, a spiking leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, Ia and Ib afferent encoders, muscle activation dynamics, and a generic BPA force block (Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_sns_implementation}). The blocks implement the same neuron and synapse equations used by the SNS-Toolbox networks of the preceding subsection. The full library adds a synapse-sum junction for neurons receiving more than six synaptic inputs, three diameter-specific BPA force elements (\qtylist{10;20;40}{\mm}) implementing the fitted force surfaces of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}, and a Hill-type biological muscle element, for twelve blocks before the spiking additions of Section~\ref{sec:spiking_methods}. The library and its demonstrations run in base Simulink without additional toolboxes; the block icons derive their excitatory and inhibitory symbols automatically from the sign of each synapse, in a color-blind-safe palette, and the publication figures regenerate from scripts.

The library's fundamental demonstrations are two basic reflex pathways. In the first, the core blocks are assembled into a reduced-order knee-reflex model with antagonist actuation (Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_reflex_topology}): Ia stretch feedback excites the motor neuron of the stretched muscle and inhibits that of its antagonist (reciprocal inhibition), while Ib force feedback inhibits the motor neuron of the muscle that generated the force (autogenic inhibition). In the second, an arm holds a beverage container while a pour loads it, with and without the stretch reflex enabled. Between them, the two demonstrations show the library reproducing the canonical spinal reflex pathways at the scale where their mechanisms are visible; their quantitative results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:sns_simulink_demos} beside their spiking twins. To couple the controller to detailed mechanical CAD, the SolidWorks assembly is exported and imported into Simscape Multibody. The URDF import path is verified on a trial knee assembly, and the fuller Multibody-Link XML import of the knee test assembly exposes a documented defect: the exporter drops the SolidWorks hinge mates as unsupported, so the knee degree of freedom arrives welded, and the defined fix is to re-mate each hinge as a concentric and coincident pair before re-export. The tendon hardware is modeled in CAD but not yet inserted into the assembly. The demonstration model retains provisional mechanical parameters, the generic BPA force block remains a placeholder, and the saved runs are therefore not used to establish quantitative knee-regulation performance.

\subsection{Controller and Body Ports into Simulink and Simscape Multibody}\label{sec:sns_ports}

Making the controllers and bodies portable is the third new-tool family, and it exists because the program's models must run in more than one program. On the controller side, the Deng two-layer CPG was rebuilt as separate editable Simulink files, rhythm-generator and pattern-formation layers apart, with the published parameter tables carried over \citep{deng_neuromechanical_2019, nourse_sns_2023}. The load-bearing choice is the persistent-sodium half-center's inactivation time constant, held fixed rather than voltage-dependent, which is what lets each half-center terminate its own burst; Section~\ref{sec:disc_spiking} returns to why that constant matters for portability. The tuned production spinal network is exported in the other direction: a script turns the tuned network definition into an editable Simulink model built from the library blocks of the preceding subsection, one block per neuron and one per synapse, with every value living in an editable block mask so that the model regenerates whenever the tuning changes.

On the body side, two import routes bring real geometry into Simscape Multibody. The SolidWorks knee assembly travels the URDF and Multibody-Link XML routes of the preceding subsection. The OpenSim \texttt{gait2392\_simbody} model travels OpenSim to MuJoCo to URDF to Simscape Multibody, with the massless muscle path-point slides preserved as links so that actuators can attach at the same physical points as in the source model; a URDF carries no muscles, so actuation re-enters as SNS library blocks driving the imported joints. Finally, a MuJoCo--Simulink bridge block set, rebuilt against the same engine version as the conversion, closes the loop from the Simulink side: muscle activation commands in, joint and actuator sensors out, with the model's saved reference pose applied at initialization.

\subsection{Cross-Platform Verification of the Contact-Driven Rhythm Core}\label{sec:crossengine_methods}

The verification harness is a tool in its own right, and its existence is the chapter's answer to a trust problem: the toolchain spans several simulation engines, and a result that holds in only one software implementation is difficult to trust. Its fundamental demonstration is replication, in two forms; the same rhythm core rebuilt in two engines must reproduce the same rhythm, and the lineage's archived models must reproduce their published behavior in their original engine. The harness is defined around the contact-driven rhythm core of the Walker-2-Layer (W2L) bipedal architecture: four rhythm-generator half-centers, one extensor/flexor pair per leg, plus graded pattern-formation and sensory cells, driven by heel-contact mechanosensory feedback. Each half-center is built from spiking neurons whose persistent sodium current, a slowly inactivating inward current, terminates each burst, so the mutually inhibiting extensor and flexor populations of a pair alternate \citep{deng_neuromechanical_2019}. The same core was implemented twice from a common connectivity specification, once as a NumPy reference integration through the SNS-Toolbox backend and once as native Simulink blocks from the library of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}, and both were driven with an identical protocol: a \qty{12}{\second} run at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, tonic drive with a brief kickoff pulse, and alternating \qty{1}{\Hz} heel-contact trains. Agreement was scored on rhythm-generator burst period, left--right antiphase correlation, burst counts, and full-trace correlation against the NumPy reference. The archived AnimatLab reference models (the bilateral contact-driven model, the modern W2L model, and the Li walker) were additionally re-run headless from their standalone simulators with instrumented charts, so that engine-level agreement and network-level agreement could be separated. The protocol covers the rhythm core and the archived models only; full-network and whole-body behavior are reported as limitations alongside the results in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results}.

\subsection{Spiking-Neuron Mirrors of the Simulation Models}\label{sec:spiking_methods}

The final tool tests an assumption rather than adding a simulation capability. The controllers documented above all compute with graded (non-spiking) neurons, in which the membrane potential itself is the analog activity variable that drives the musculoskeletal model. To test that the conclusions drawn from those models are not artifacts of graded coding, spiking-neuron mirrors of all three simulation families were built, following one design rule: the motor-neuron layer stays non-spiking, because the musculoskeletal model consumes a continuous activation command, while the afferent encoders (the neurons that convert sensor signals into synaptic input), rhythm-generator half-centers, pattern-formation cells, interneurons, and commissural (cross-side) cells become leaky integrate-and-fire neurons operating at real membrane-potential ranges, so that the network carries spikes above the motor-neuron layer and analog voltages below it \citep{szczecinski_perspective_2023, nourse_sns_2023}. Event-based coding is expected to matter most in the afferent and rhythm layers, where mechanosensor onsets and phase transitions are discrete events, and least at the motor-neuron output, which the muscle activation dynamics low-pass filter anyway.

The MuJoCo--SNS-Toolbox mirror (\texttt{build\_network\_spiking.py}) reproduces the production spinal network of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} population for population and connection for connection, and is selected at build time by a single environment-variable switch so that the default build path is untouched; that the non-spiking pipeline is unchanged was proven by re-running its defaults gate and baseline evaluation to bit-exact agreement. Because SNS-Toolbox 1.5.2 ships no spiking bursting-neuron class, the rhythm-generator half-centers are adapting integrate-and-fire cells: burst termination is carried by spike-frequency adaptation of the firing threshold, whose time constant reuses the corresponding slow-inactivation value of the non-spiking build so that tuned adaptation values transfer between the two. Spiking cells rest at \qty{-70}{\mV}, spike at \qty{-50}{\mV}, and reset to \qty{-60}{\mV}, with a tonic bias current parking their equilibrium a few millivolts below threshold, the analog cells' near-threshold operating point. The network sub-steps at \qty{0.5}{\ms} (four sub-steps inside each \qty{2}{\ms} physics step) so that spike timing is resolved without changing the physics integration, and non-spiking readout taps are appended for the runner's stance gates and neural logging so that all existing analysis code reads analog levels unchanged.

The mirror's synaptic weights are set by a calibration script rather than by hand. Spikes onto the non-spiking motor neurons are calibrated by mean conductance, so that a steady presynaptic train reproduces the non-spiking steady-state motor-neuron voltage (calibration residuals at or below \qty{3e-4}{\mV}); synapses between spiking cells are calibrated by firing-rate transfer (a \qty{40}{\hertz} source drives its target at \qty{40}{\hertz}, and a calibrated inhibitory synapse silences a \qty{40}{\hertz}-driven cell); the graded-to-spiking command pathway is scaled from the measured afferent current-to-rate map. The full calibration, with the measured residual of every factor, is tabulated in Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations} (Table~\ref{tab:app_spk_cal}).

In the Simulink family, three blocks were added additively to the SNS library of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} (for fifteen blocks in total): a \texttt{SpikingNeuron} with the same internal synaptic summation as the non-spiking block, an event-wired \texttt{SpikingSynapse} whose conductance jumps by a fixed increment per presynaptic spike and saturates at its maximum, and a \texttt{HybridSpikingSynapse} that carries the same conductance dynamics onto a non-spiking postsynaptic cell. The blocks were verified against the analytic single-spike conductance decay (maximum deviation \qty{6.2e-07}{\micro\siemens}), against a verbatim re-implementation of the SNS-Toolbox update equations on a hybrid two-neuron circuit (identical spike count and maximum spike-time deviation \qty{11.75}{\micro\second}), and against the SNS-Toolbox numpy backend itself (mean interspike-interval difference \qty{0.19}{\micro\second}). Spiking twins of the knee-reflex and beverage-holding demonstrations were then built by copy-and-patch: only the sensory interneuron layer and its synapses are replaced by spiking cells, while the motor neurons, muscles, joints, and measurements are identical to the committed demonstrations, and the twin is run at an operating point matched to the baseline gains.

In the AnimatLab family, spiking copies of the runnable standalone exports were produced by a conversion script that rewrites copies of the model files, leaving every original file untouched: neuron thresholds are moved from the graded regime (unreachably positive) into the spiking regime, every graded chemical synapse type is re-emitted as a per-spike synapse with the same identity and amplitude (interpreted as a per-spike conductance increment with \qty{10}{\ms} decay), and the per-connection class tag that AnimatLab stores independently of the synapse type is flipped to match. A calibrated variant sets each cell class's threshold just below its measured graded peak, and an ablation that converts the thresholds but keeps the graded synapses isolates which change does what. Each copy passes a structural validator (neuron, connection, synapse-type, chart, and stimulus sets identical to its original, and every class tag consistent) and runs headless to its configured end. The 2023 spiking walker model of the same lineage, which was tuned as a rate code from birth, serves as the spiking reference baseline for this family.

Each mirror is verified against its non-spiking twin by a fixed gate set rather than by inspection: a topology gate requires the connection multiset to be identical between the two builds under three gain configurations; a rhythm gate requires self-sustained alternating rhythm under constant drive with the period within \qty{20}{\percent} of the analog build; a basin gate requires the rhythm to persist under \qty{1}{\percent} multiplicative jitter on every scalar parameter; an air-regime smoke gate compares cadence, duty, and joint ranges of the deafferented suspended-air preparation; and a regression gate requires every committed non-spiking artifact to reproduce its recorded values exactly with the spiking blocks present in the library. The mirrors carry mapped parameters only, with no new tuning, so their results (Section~\ref{sec:spiking_results}) establish what transfers structurally and what requires re-tuning, and any fresh tuning effort is future work.

\subsection{Tuning, Robustness, and Reference Data}\label{sec:sns_tuning_methods}

Above the physiologically set values, the free gains of each network are tuned by systematic search rather than by hand: a sequential model-based optimizer (a tree-structured Parzen estimator) proposes gain sets, each proposal is scored by a full simulation, and each stage's winner seeds the next. The design rule that matters here is that no hand-tuned parameter enters a production configuration; the failure mode of the prior walker (Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker}) was precisely hand tuning that left no record of sensitivity. The stage structure and the scoring philosophy are the subject of Section~\ref{sec:sns_curriculum}.

Robustness is enforced during tuning rather than inspected afterward. Finalist configurations must retain their rhythm under \qty{1}{\percent} multiplicative jitter on every scalar parameter (the basin gate of Section~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} applies the same test to the mirrors), survive stepping of the tonic drive about the tuned value, and, for standing configurations, absorb push perturbations without falling. The requirement exists because the tuned networks sit near bifurcations of their dynamics, a property this chapter makes explicit in its cross-engine and spiking results: a winner whose basin is narrow does not survive a solver change, let alone a hardware transfer.

The reference data for tuning and evaluation are inverse-kinematics cycles from many human subjects of different sizes and speeds rather than a single subject: a 43-cycle locomotion reference library, detailed in Section~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}, comprising running trials from eight subjects at four speeds, walking references at prescribed speeds from \qty{0.5}{\metre\per\second} to \qty{2.0}{\metre\per\second} plus self-selected trials, and one predicted-walking cycle. Working against many references penalizes tuning that has memorized one subject's kinematics, in the same spirit as the perturbation gates. Circuit structure is treated as data under the same methodology: every network's connection multiset is compared programmatically against its intended topology (the topology gates), and the publication schematics are generated from the compiled network with each drawn edge asserted against the compiled connection list, so the drawings cannot drift from the circuits they document.

\subsection{Simulink Library Demonstrations and Cross-Program Ports}\label{sec:sns_simulink_demos}

The SNS-Simscape library demonstrates the canonical reflex pathways at the scale where their mechanisms are visible. In the knee-reflex demonstration, the joint reads \ang{15.79} at \qty{0.05}{\second} and settles at a mean of \ang{43.5} over the last \qty{2}{\second}, with a mild irregular antagonist alternation about the mean. In the beverage-holding demonstration, the stretch reflex limits the maximum sag of the loaded arm to \ang{2.00} against \ang{7.85} with the reflex disabled; the committed values are tabulated beside their spiking twins in Table~\ref{tab:spike_beer}, Section~\ref{sec:spiking_results}. A third demonstration closes the loop onto the actuator model itself: a half-center CPG with burst-terminating adaptation drives the antagonist BPA pair of the one-degree-of-freedom knee through the library's diameter-specific force blocks, sweeping the joint through \ang{9.7} to \ang{48.0}.

The controller ports verify at the same standard. The Deng rhythm-generator and pattern-formation files, driven by a single \qty{10}{\nA} pulse of \qty{20}{\milli\second}, oscillate continuously at a \qty{1.938}{\second} period against the \qty{1.94}{\second} NumPy reference, with the extensor and flexor half-centers anticorrelated at $r = -0.86$. The exported full-network Simulink model reproduces its NumPy reference to a maximum deviation of \qty{4.2e-06}{\milli\volt} across all 410 neurons at \qty{0.3}{\second}; beyond roughly \qty{0.4}{\second} the network's chaos makes any two integrators diverge in phase (Section~\ref{sec:disc_spiking}), so the verification window is the early transient and the faithful reproduction is the fixed-step Euler run at the production step size.

The MuJoCo--Simulink bridge was proven against an independent Python integration of the same engine version: single-muscle activation, two-rate control, and sensor-readback tests all pass, the readback bit-exact over the scored window. On the bridge, a single-muscle Ia/II/Ib reflex loop on the converted model's vastus medialis bounds the consequences of a postural collapse: without the reflex, the falling model drives transient muscle forces past \qty{40}{\kilo\newton} before the solver fails, and with it, the same loop holds muscle force at \qty{469}{\newton} while the stretch response recruits the muscle. The full exported network then ran as a Simulink model block driving all 92 muscle actuators of the converted model, completing a \qty{5}{\second} closed-loop run with all drives finite and \qty{96}{\percent} of the muscles recruited. That run also isolated a real property: under tonic drive alone, the Simulink realization settles to the tonic fixed point while the NumPy reference oscillates, the near-bifurcation behavior that Section~\ref{sec:disc_spiking} treats at length, and the production rhythm requires the full input schedule rather than drive alone.

The OpenSim-to-Simscape import completes the Simulink-side tool set. The \texttt{gait2392\_simbody} model arrives in Simscape Multibody as a mechanism of 1,446 blocks and 149 joints, of which 20 are the anatomical revolute joints, 85 are the massless path-point slides that carry the muscle attachment points, and 44 are the fixed-link offset chains the conversion route requires; the imported mechanism compiles and simulates. What the route does not carry are the muscles themselves, which re-enter as SNS library blocks driving the imported joints, the division of labor the chapter's architecture assumes throughout.

\subsection{Cross-Engine Verification of the Contact-Driven Rhythm Core}\label{sec:crossengine_results}

Between the NumPy reference and the native Simulink reimplementation of the W2L rhythm core, both legs' rhythm-generator burst periods matched at \qty{1.000}{\second} versus \qty{1.000}{\second} (0.0\% error), the left--right antiphase correlation reproduced the reference exactly ($r = -0.525$), and all ten expected bursts occurred in the scored window. The full voltage traces cross-correlate at $r = 0.99996$--$0.99998$ at a $-\qty{2}{\milli\second}$ lag with an RMSE of 0.099--0.111~mV (Figure~\ref{fig:w2l_crossengine}); the residual lag is consistent with the one-step delay of the discrete Simulink solver.

The same protocol also runs the chapter's first deletion experiment, and it surfaced an architectural fact rather than a defect: with the heel-contact trains removed, the full network holds a static latch under tonic drive with kickoff alone, in every implementation, so phasic contact feedback is part of the drive architecture of the W2L design, not an optional perturbation. The result is a load dependence that echoes the decerebrate-cat locomotion reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:bg_neural}, and it is the reason the pathway-removal experiments of Section~\ref{sec:walker_variant_results} treat the contact channels as a pathway class rather than as a boundary condition.

\subsection{Headless Validation of the AnimatLab Reference Models}\label{sec:animatlab_validation}

The second half of the harness's fundamental demonstration is replication of published results in the original engine. The archived AnimatLab reference models were re-run headless from their standalone simulators with instrumented charts, and eleven targetable rhythm and gait metrics were extracted automatically; all eleven pass their acceptance bands. In the bilateral contact-driven model, the rhythm generators oscillate at periods of \qty{0.450}{\second} (left) and \qty{0.467}{\second} (right) with a left--right lag of \qty{0.529}{cycle} (cross-correlation $r = 0.976$), confirming alternating gait timing. In the modern W2L model, the joints oscillate at \qty{2.25}{\Hz}; its flexor half-centers peak at $-\qty{56.4}{\mV}$ and never cross the $-\qty{55}{\mV}$ threshold, so the rhythm is carried by clean extensor/flexor antiphase ($r = -0.996$) through the graded pathway rather than by flexor spiking. The Li-model rerun \citep{li_bipedal_2017} walks at \qty{0.637}{\meter\per\second} with right and left step periods of \qty{1.299 \pm 0.024}{\second} and \qty{1.333 \pm 0.080}{\second} and duty factors of 0.443 and 0.555, reproducing the published behavior of the original model in its original engine.

The replication series has a defined extension, and its current boundary is stated plainly. The recent connectome-level circuit organizations of \citet{shevtsova_modeling_2016}, \citet{dirusso_investigating_2023}, and \citet{shinohara_mechanisms_2025} are specified in this program as circuit-rule translations, connection lists carrying per-edge citations, from which running models in SNS-Toolbox and SNS-Simscape would be built. On the current record they have not been built or verified, and no replication claim is made for them here; they are the next entries of the series this section's protocol defines.

\subsection{Stimulus and Deletion Experiments on the Walker Networks}\label{sec:walker_variant_results}

The locomotor physiology that this program's circuits imitate was established with two interventions, deletion and stimulation, and the laboratory's first experiments apply the same logic at the levels the two-level architecture defines. In the reference works, removing proprioceptive feedback degrades locomotor patterns in mice \citep{akay_degradation_2014}, proprioceptive input regulates and adapts locomotor activity \citep{lam_role_2002}, and tonic central and sensory stimulation facilitates involuntary air-stepping in humans \citep{selionov_tonic_2009}; the two-level CPG models themselves reproduce specific deletion phenomena, such as spontaneous deletions of motoneuron activity during which the locomotor period and phase are maintained \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}. The experiments implemented here are the simulator-side analogues that the assembled tools make inexpensive: removal of an entire pathway class from a fully tuned walker, re-evaluated on the tuning reference, and stepping of the tonic drive about its tuned value. They act at the rhythm-generator level (tonic drive, interleg commissural pathways, and the weak rhythm-generator mutual excitation), at the sensorimotor level (Renshaw cells), and at the afferent level (Ia, Ib, group II, and contact channels). Systematic stimulation series at the pattern-formation and motoneuron levels, and the motoneuron skipping-deletion phenomenon itself, are defined as verification tests in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework} and have not yet been run; the experiments below are therefore reported as pathway deletions and drive stimuli, labeled by the level at which each acts. The published models do not carry these pathway-class interventions as performed experiments, so fidelity for them rests on the receiving programs, which apply the same class-level deletions and drive-stepping protocols to the tuned networks.

The pathway-removal sweep (Table~\ref{tab:walker_ablations}) removes individual pathway classes from each configuration and re-evaluates the tuning-reference score. In the production configuration, removing the Ia afferent pathway freezes the model outright: the evaluation detects no gait cycles and returns the fixed $-25$ fallback. Removing the Renshaw cells, the group-II afferents, or all afferent classes at once degrades the score to $-267.3$, $-255.2$, and $-267.8$, respectively, against the full model's $-186.4$, while removing the Ib, contact, or interleg commissural channels individually shifts the score by smaller amounts toward zero ($-180.3$, $-183.9$, and $-174.9$). The six-synergy variant behaves differently: with all afferents removed it scores $-190.5$ against its full model's $-200.4$ and still walks bilaterally, with ten cycles on each leg, a knee-flexion minimum of \ang{-71.2}, and a \qty{0.684}{\second} period, so its afferent complement is not what sustains its gait. The W2L-layout variant depends on both the Ia pathway (removed: $-273.5$) and the weak rhythm-generator mutual excitation (removed: $-279.9$) against its full model's $-144.5$ with four right-leg and six left-leg cycles, while removing its Renshaw cells leaves a bilateral gait at $-142.4$.

Read against the reference physiology, the sweep's structure is familiar even where its details are specific to these networks. Deleting all afferent classes from the production configuration degrades its gait without abolishing it, which is the qualitative outcome of the deafferentation experiments \citep{akay_degradation_2014}; the network that matches that picture most closely is the six-synergy variant, which keeps a bilateral gait with every afferent class removed. The Ia-only removal freezing the production configuration is a stronger dependence than the animal experiments show, and it marks that network's tuning as built around its length feedback. The degradation under group-II removal is consistent with proprioceptive regulation of the locomotor pattern \citep{lam_role_2002}, and the equally large degradation under Renshaw-cell removal indicates that the recurrent-inhibition loop carries weight in the tuned production network. No experiment here reproduces the motoneuron-level deletion phenomena of the two-level CPG models \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}; the deletions act at the afferent, interneuron, and rhythm-generator levels, and extending them to the pattern-formation and motoneuron levels is scheduled with the verification tests of Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}.

The drive-perturbation and push tests are the stimulus side. The production configuration never fell in 15 stand and push evaluations (maximum push sway \qty{0.123}{\meter}), and its walk survives drive perturbations of $+0.01$, $-0.01$, and $-0.02$ (scores of $-201.9$, $-177.0$, and $-171.2$), freezing only at $+0.02$; its pruned derivative survives the unperturbed evaluation ($-176.1$) and the $-0.02$ perturbation ($-168.2$). The six-synergy and W2L-layout variants fall under every $\pm 0.01$ and $\pm 0.02$ drive perturbation tested, with pelvis tilt reaching \ang{39} to \ang{40}. In stimulus terms, a step change of the tonic drive within this range leaves the production lineage walking while the two variant lineages fall. The sweep therefore identifies the production configuration and its pruned derivative as the robust pair of the family, and locates the fragility of the six-synergy and W2L-layout variants under drive perturbation.

\section{Spiking-Neuron Mirror Results}\label{sec:spiking_results}

The spiking-neuron mirrors of Section~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} were evaluated against their non-spiking twins in the same order in which the families were introduced: the MuJoCo spinal network, the Simulink demonstrations, and the AnimatLab walker family. All numbers in this section come from the gated verification effort whose artifacts and reproduction commands are catalogued in Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations}.

\subsection{MuJoCo Spinal-Network Mirror}

The non-spiking production baselines were first re-established on the recorded software environment: all three current production configurations reproduce their documented evaluation scores exactly, including the default build's kinematic score of $-161.57$ (the full-precision value is tabulated with the gate records in Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations}), and after the mirror's selector was added the default build gate ($(410, 376, 1186)$ populations, inputs, and connections) and that same baseline score reproduce digit for digit, proving the non-spiking pipeline untouched.

The topology gate passes exactly: under the default, fully tuned, and joint-layer pattern-formation gain configurations, the spiking mirror's directed connection multiset is identical to the non-spiking build's ($1{,}186$, $7{,}908$, and $7{,}762$ connections respectively), with identical input lists, identical motor-neuron maps, and exactly the expected number of readout taps (12, 12, and 16). The mirror is structurally the same network.

Under constant drive (network alone, \qty{20}{\second}), the rhythm gate splits. The spiking mirror self-sustains clean bilateral antiphase, with rhythm-generator extensor and flexor cells firing at \qty{4.9}{\hertz} and \qty{3.2}{\hertz} per leg and the swing readout sustained in every window, but its envelope period is \qty{0.621}{\second} against the analog build's \qty{0.399}{\second}, a \qty{55.6}{\percent} lengthening that misses the gate's \qty{20}{\percent} tolerance (Figure~\ref{fig:spike_rhythm}). The basin gate passes in full: the rhythm persists under \qty{1}{\percent} multiplicative jitter on every scalar parameter in all 12 seeded trials, with a final-window swing minimum of \qty{4.58}{\mV} against the \qty{5.13}{\mV} baseline (margin $0.89\times$) and no divergent trials. In the deafferented suspended-air preparation, the mirror air-steps rhythmically with full-amplitude joint swings (knee range \ang{106.0} against the twin's \ang{117.9}), but its cadence is roughly ten times faster than the non-spiking twin's \qty{0.47}{\hertz} air-stepping regime in this preparation, so the air-regime gate fails on the cadence clause alone.

\subsection{AnimatLab Spiking Conversions}

The AnimatLab family produces the clearest negative result of the spiking comparison, and it is reported as such. The graded baselines were re-established first: the two-layer walker's rhythm generator oscillates at \qty{0.443}{\second} (\qty{2.26}{\hertz}), the bilateral variant at \qty{0.462}{\second} with the two legs folded near antiphase (lag $-0.42$ cycle, $r = 0.869$), and the already-spiking 2023-lineage model, the family's spiking reference, bursts at \qty{1.305}{\second} with folded antiphase of $+0.494$ cycle ($r = 0.703$), reproducing its documented period. The current reorganized project's standalone export remains latched under the tonic drives examined, consistent with Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}.

No spiking copy of the three graded models preserves the rhythm (Table~\ref{tab:spike_animatlab}). The two-layer conversions die at every threshold and decay tried (two or zero rhythm-generator spikes in \qty{5}{\second}); the bilateral variant latches with one side's half-centers saturated at \qty{442}{\hertz}--\qty{450}{\hertz}; and the reorganized project's copy converts its latched baseline into strictly synchronous \qty{11.8}{\hertz} co-bursting with no alternation: the left extensor and flexor half-centers emit identical 58-spike trains whose spike-count envelopes correlate at $r = 1.000$ at zero lag. The thresholds-only ablation, which keeps the graded synapses, preserves near-baseline joint oscillations, isolating the synapse conversion as the change that breaks the rhythm (Figure~\ref{fig:spike_animatlab}): per-spike conductances at the graded amplitude either starve the reciprocal loop of inhibitory charge or saturate it, whereas the graded synapses read the membrane potential continuously and keep oscillating through subthreshold voltages. A spiking version of this family therefore needs its synaptic amplitudes and decays re-tuned as a rate code, which is how the 2023 spiking reference was built in the first place, rather than copied parameter by parameter.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:sns_discussion}

This section consolidates what the results mean for the program: the spiking comparison first, because its conclusions constrain the rest; then the training curriculum and its scoring behind the tuned configurations; then the integration of the BPA force model, the standing of the robot models under their different controllers, and the BPA-actuated robot; and finally the new tools themselves as the chapter's contribution.

\subsection{What Spiking Changes and Does Not}\label{sec:disc_spiking}

The spiking-mirror comparison (Sections~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} and~\ref{sec:spiking_results}) allows the dissertation's neural-coding assumption to be examined directly, and its outcome divides cleanly into what the coding regime changes and what it leaves alone.

What spiking does not change is the behavior of the controlled model, provided the design rule and the calibration hold. Because the motor-neuron layer stays non-spiking and the spikes onto it are calibrated by mean conductance, the interface that the actuator and torque models of this dissertation attach to, a continuous activation command per muscle, is unchanged by construction. The Simulink twins make the point quantitatively: with only the sensory interneuron layer converted, the knee-reflex transient is identical, the settle band moves by \ang{0.9}, and the beverage-holding reflex modulation moves by \ang{0.02}. To the musculoskeletal model, a calibrated \qty{40}{\hertz} train and the graded voltage it replaces are the same command; the activation dynamics of the muscle model filter the spike train the same way the biological membrane would. The actuator-level and joint-level conclusions of Chapters~\ref{ch:methods}--\ref{ch:results} therefore do not depend on the neural coding chosen above the motor-neuron layer.

What spiking changes first is timing. The mirror's self-sustained rhythm runs \qty{55.6}{\percent} slower than the analog build at the same constant drive, and its air-stepping cadence is roughly ten times faster than the non-spiking twin's in that preparation, deviations in opposite directions in the two tests, which is itself informative, since a single scaling error would move both the same way. The comparison traced the constant-drive period to two identifiable knobs, the threshold-increment magnitude and the adaptation time constant, and demonstrated alternation/period trade-offs along them; the untuned starting point, not a structural failure, is the honest reading. Any period-matched spiking controller is therefore a short tuning effort away, and the mirror's mapped parameters are the correct starting point for it.

What spiking changes second is robustness, in the mirror's favor. The analog network's constant-drive rhythm sits close to a bifurcation, a parameter region where an exact re-implementation in a different integration stack can settle to the tonic fixed point instead of oscillating, so marginal winners risk not surviving a solver change. The spiking half-centers passed the perturbation basin gate in full, with the rhythm persisting under \qty{1}{\percent} jitter on every scalar parameter including the spiking-specific ones, at $0.89\times$ of the baseline swing. For the hardware-transfer program of Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}, where the network will be re-implemented on embedded hardware with finite-precision arithmetic, a rhythm whose basin is wide is the safer substrate, and the sub-stepping cost of resolving spikes (\qty{0.5}{\milli\second} network steps inside the unchanged \qty{2}{\milli\second} physics step) is modest.

What spiking does not tolerate is a naive parameter copy at the synapse level. The AnimatLab conversions show that converting graded synaptic amplitudes to per-spike increments of the same magnitude breaks the reciprocal half-center loop outright (the loop is either starved of inhibitory charge or saturated), while converting only the thresholds changes nothing, and the 2023 spiking walker of the same lineage walks because it was tuned as a rate code from birth. Synaptic strength in a spiking network is a rate-code quantity that must be derived from the presynaptic firing rate and the conductance decay, exactly as the MuJoCo mirror's calibration script derives it, not copied from the graded value. This is the one place where the negative results should temper expectations: spiking conversion is an exercise in re-derivation, and the calibration-first, gate-everything procedure of Section~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} is the transferable method.

Finally, the one place where the untuned spiking mirror outperforms its tuned non-spiking twin, producing a bilateral ground gait where the winner's left leg is frozen, is a single run with no statistics, and it is claimed only as a lead. But it points at the mechanism the design rule was after: the mirror's afferents are event-coded, its adaptation is spike-history-based, and its inhibitory charge arrives in discrete quanta, so the phase relationships that the analog build locks in around a frozen leg are not the only stable operating regime of the same topology. Establishing whether that regime survives tuning, and whether event-coded contact afferents are what unlock it, is precisely the closed-loop CPG comparison program defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}, and the mirror is now the instrument for running it.

\subsection{The Five-Stage Training Curriculum and Its Scoring}\label{sec:sns_curriculum}

The tuned configurations of this chapter were not tuned all at once. The training proceeds through five stages, each adding one capability and each seeded with the previous stage's winner so that only the newly activated pathway gains are searched: deafferented air stepping first, in which the half-centers' oscillation is verified with the legs suspended and every sensory channel silent; afferented air stepping second, with heel, toe, and load feedback active in the same suspended preparation; standing balance third, the stage modeled on the SCONE balance tutorial (Section~\ref{sec:sns_existing_tools}) and scored on maintaining posture under push perturbations; ground walking fourth; and ground walking with the contact-driven pathway variant fifth. Standing before walking is the design rule: gait tuning begins only after a configuration holds posture, and the failures the curriculum surfaces early are the cheap ones.

The scoring is stage-appropriate and gated. The air stages are scored on rhythm and swing depth with the objective rhythm-gated, so a configuration that freezes cannot win by holding a static pose. The balance stage is scored on posture maintenance under push. The walking stages use the production pipeline's cycle-shape kinematic score against a human reference cycle, in which 0 denotes perfect agreement and a fixed fallback marks the absence of detectable gait cycles, a value that sits below every genuine gait so that a frozen model cannot out-score a walker. Scores are never read alone: finalists must pass the perturbation and basin requirements of Section~\ref{sec:sns_tuning_methods}, because a score achieved on a narrow basin is a property of the solver rather than of the circuit, a lesson the cross-engine results of this chapter turn into a gate.

\subsection{Integration of the BPA Force Model}\label{sec:sns_bpa_integration}

The actuator model travels with the controller. Inside the MuJoCo loop, actuator force comes from the quasi-static BPA model of Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}, including the correction-factor force balance of Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}, rather than from a generic muscle approximation; in Simulink, the library's three diameter-specific force blocks implement the same fitted force surfaces, so a circuit assembled there commands the same actuators as its MuJoCo counterpart. The demonstrations that close the loop onto the actuator model, the synthetic knee of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}, the BPA-CPG leg demonstration of Section~\ref{sec:sns_simulink_demos}, and the BPA-actuated robot below, all draw their forces from this one validated source, which is what keeps actuator-level conclusions consistent across engines. The BPA-actuated robot's peak forces sit at 60\% of the pressure ceiling, so the demonstrated gaits operate inside the characterized pressure range rather than extrapolating past it. What the integration does not yet carry is actuator dynamics: the force model is quasi-static, and the pressure--force dynamics under pulse-modulated valve control \citep{elzein_pressure_2025} enter the program as future work (Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}).

\subsection{The BPA-Actuated Robot in MuJoCo}\label{sec:bpa_walker}

The end-to-end demonstration of the MuJoCo chain, conversion pipeline plus force interface plus network builder, is the BPA-actuated robot, and it belongs in this discussion because it is the tooling's standing answer to the question the chapter opened with: what the laboratory is for. The walker geometry of Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker} was exported to MuJoCo and driven by antagonistic BPA actuator pairs under the split contact-driven network, with actuator forces computed by the BPA model of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}. In the air-stepping preparation, with the harness carrying 70\% of the \qty{411}{\N} body weight, the walker produced clean knee-flexion excursions at a \qty{1.542}{\second} period with a knee flexion range of \ang{63.5}, peak BPA forces of \qty{880}{\N} (left) and \qty{1372}{\N} (right) at a peak pressure of \qty{372}{\kPa} (60\% of the \qty{620}{\kPa} ceiling), and 7 and 5 heel-contact episodes on the left and right. This run demonstrates the complete chain from the characterized BPA force model to a multi-joint walker stepping under synthetic neural control, with actuator sizing operating inside the characterized pressure range.

The same evaluation series reported its failures with equal weight. On the ground, the W2L gate model under its untuned default gains stands (full-support stance) but does not walk: heel duty is zero and forward displacement is $+\qty{0.001}{\meter}$ over the run. With the left and right drives separated and the feedback gains re-tuned, the same robot alternates in antiphase (inter-leg phase 0.487) and progresses \qty{1.72}{\meter} over a \qty{30}{\second} window (\qty{0.066}{\metre\per\second}) under the harness protocol, with the neural drive weaned to \qty{60}{\percent} of its bootstrap value and the result robust under perturbation (12 of 12 basin re-runs). Below \qty{60}{\percent} drive the gait dies, the harness itself is not weaned, and foot duty remains below the strict walking gate, so the result is supported progression rather than validated walking. The MuJoCo re-implementation of the Li model produced no stance episodes in any of seven drop-protocol variants. Together with the latch result of Section~\ref{sec:crossengine_results}, these negatives localize the open problem precisely: the contact-driven rhythm architecture itself transfers across engines intact, but translating it to unsupported ground locomotion requires the balance and weight-bearing layers defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}, and those layers are not present in these models.

\subsection{Controller Comparison on the Robot Models and the Walking Status}\label{sec:sns_controller_comparison}

The robot models carry more than one controller, and the comparison is a result of the tooling rather than an afterthought. On the converted robot-body model, the four production walking configurations span the controller lineages (Section~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}), and scored with the same cycle-shape kinematic score against the 43-reference library, the family-level fact is that the robot-walker-lineage configuration, not the human-derived ones, agrees most closely with the library, while the six-synergy configuration, the most strongly human-derived in its pattern formation, agrees least closely; closing that gap is a tuning-program question rather than a tool question, and it is not pursued further here. On the physical-model side, the SolidWorks knee assembly is imported into Simscape Multibody with its degree-of-freedom defect documented and its fix defined (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}), and the imported \texttt{gait2392\_simbody} mechanism awaits its muscle blocks; neither physical model yet carries a tuned controller, so their controller comparisons remain the MuJoCo-side results above.

The walking status of the tuned configurations deserves a precise statement, because the chapter's records support exactly this and no more. Each tuned configuration was brought through the curriculum's air and balance stages: deafferented and afferented air stepping with the legs suspended, and standing balance that absorbs push perturbations without falling (the production configuration survived 15 stand-and-push evaluations, Section~\ref{sec:walker_variant_results}). On the ground, all walking evaluations run under the support harness of the tuning pipeline, where the configurations produce gross walking-like progression, with cycles detected and scored but with right-leg-only or asymmetric gait patterns (Section~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}). A subsequent drive-weaning study re-tuned the feedback gains while forcing the hand-drive down a ladder of scales. Its finalist improves the default build's evaluation score from $-161.57$ to $-145.07$ with a bilateral gait of ten contact cycles per leg (contact duty 0.71 and 0.65, no non-finite states), survives a closed-loop basin check in eight of eight perturbed evaluations, and degrades gracefully against independent walking references; with its feedback held fixed, bilateral stepping persists at every drive scale down to zero hand-drive, at which the drive pathway is absent from the network entirely, whereas the un-retuned configuration collapses below \qty{85}{\percent} of full drive. The gait remains in-place alternation under the support rig, without forward progression. Unsupported, harness-free walking has not been demonstrated in this program's records: the BPA-actuated robot's ground model stands without walking under its untuned default gains and reaches only supported progression at weaned drive (Section~\ref{sec:bpa_walker}), and the archived reference walkers do not walk unsupported either. Whether any configuration walks without the harness is therefore an open question, and the balance and weight-bearing layers that would answer it are the first item of the simulation program defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}.

\subsection{The New Tools and the Program They Enable}\label{sec:sns_tools_summary}

The chapter's contribution is the tool set. The OpenSim--MyoConverter--SNS-Toolbox pipeline couples the full-lower-body converted model to a two-level spinal network with Ia, Ib, group II, and contact feedback, and it runs the full neuromechanical loop faster than real time. The SNS-Simscape library assembles the same component models in a standard controls environment, with the reflex demonstrations, the diameter-specific BPA force blocks, the exported production network, and the imported OpenSim and SolidWorks bodies behind it. The controller ports put the Deng-lineage and Li-lineage circuits into NumPy, Simulink, MuJoCo, and Simscape Multibody realizations on the same bodies, and the verification instruments (the cross-engine harness, the structure audits, the perturbation and basin gates, and the spiking mirrors) are what make the ports trustworthy. Every one of these tools is reusable by a researcher who did not build them, and the loop's speed is what makes deletion and stimulus experiments cheap enough to repeat.

The laboratory's limits are on record with equal weight. The archived walkers do not walk unsupported on the ground; the tuned networks transfer poorly to diverse human reference cycles and walk right-leg-only or asymmetrically under the harness, or alternate in place under the support rig at weaned drive; the systematic stimulation series at the pattern-formation and motoneuron levels, and the motoneuron-level deletion phenomena of the reference models, are not yet reproduced; the SolidWorks knee arrives in Simscape Multibody with its joint welded pending re-mating; and the Shinohara, Di Russo, and Shevtsova replications remain circuit-rule translations rather than running models. What the tools are for is the program they define next, in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}: the balance and weight-bearing layers that unsupported walking requires, the systematic stimulation series at the motoneuron, pattern-formation, and rhythm-generator levels against which the circuits' signs, gains, and phase structure will be validated, and the transfer of the whole stack toward real-time hardware.
