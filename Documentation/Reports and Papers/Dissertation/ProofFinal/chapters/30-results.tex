\section{Completed Foundation Studies}\label{sec:foundation}

Four studies completed during this dissertation establish the foundation for the results that follow. First, the placement optimization of Morrow, Bolen, and Hunt found artificial muscle attachment locations that reproduce human torque profiles and ranges of motion about the trunk and leg joints \citep{morrow_optimization_2020}; it converted the earlier finding that transplanted human attachment locations do not transfer directly to a BPA-actuated robot \citep{bolen_bipedal_2019, bolen_determination_2019} into a usable design tool. The route redesigns of Section~\ref{sec:ongoing} rerun that tool under the corrected torque model. Second, the Sensory Afferent Database organized sensory-afferent literature into a structured, model-oriented resource \citep{bolen_sensory_2023}, and a follow-up study evaluated AI-assisted literature triage for expanding it \citep{bolen_using_2024}; those data inform the sensory channels of the neural controller. The database has since been audited and expanded into a curated laboratory tool: of the 943 papers in the audited corpus, 833 carry structured curation notes, 95 carry an afferent classification, and 363 carry notes translating each pathway toward robot or simulation use, packaged with the corpus citation graph (18 topic clusters, 11,130 directed citation pairs) as a self-contained explorer for lab use. Third, an inverted-pendulum balance platform with BPA-driven plantarflexion and dorsiflexion demonstrated pseudo-vestibular feedback using inertial measurement units \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. Fourth, companion work by Elzein, Lutz, Bolen, and Hunt produced a state-space, optimization-based model of pressure and force dynamics in pulse-modulated BPAs \citep{elzein_pressure_2025}, supplying dynamic actuator behavior beyond the static force characterization reported below.

\section{BPA Force Characterization}\label{sec:results_force}

This section reports the results of the isometric force characterization experiments described in Section~\ref{sec:force_experiment}. First, maximum isometric force at \qty{620}{\kPa} is modeled as a function of resting length. The remaining data are then normalized to build a predictive force surface, and the resulting model is compared with the manufacturer's tool and with other published BPA models.


\subsection{Maximum Force at 620 kPa}

Data of the maximum force from BPA characterization tests of the \qty{10}{\mm} and \qty{20}{\mm} diameters at \qty{620}{\kPa} show a dependency on resting length (Figure~\ref{fig:Fmax_fit}). This is a previously unreported characteristic of these artificial muscles. Detailed analysis of the Festo \mbox{tool \citep{festo_2026}} does in fact predict a change in maximum force with the resting length; however, the Festo tool predicts increased force with shorter lengths, while our collected data indicate decreasing force with shorter lengths. The data show a force response resembling an $\arctan$ curve along the $l_0$ dimension. Using the Nonlinear Least Squares method and a Least Absolute Residual robustness, we fit an arctan curve to the data to get the maximum force at \qty{620}{\kPa} given a resting length as:
\begin{align}
    F_{620_{10}} &= \qty{303.5}{\N}\cdot \arctan{( \qty{19.03}{\per\metre} \cdot (l_0-0.0075))}  \label{eq:Fmax10} \\[10pt]
    F_{620_{20}} &= \qty{922.4}{\N} \cdot \arctan{(\qty{15.37}{\per\m} \cdot (l_0-0.013))} \label{eq:Fmax20}
\end{align}

\noindent
The length is offset by \qty{0.0075}{\m} and \qty{0.013}{\m} because solid modeling showed that the end caps contact each other at these lengths. At these lengths, the actuator would not be able to contract to produce force.

\subsection{Maximum Contraction at \qty{620}{\kPa}}

Figure~\ref{fig:F-P_contraction}(b) shows an attempt at a linear fit for maximum contraction at \qty{620}{\kPa} ($\epsilon_{620}$) as a function of resting length ($l_0)$. Error bars have been included to show the effect of $\pm$\qty{1}{\mm} accuracy with measurements of $l_0$ and $l_{620}$. There was a large amount of variance in the data, with the linear fit giving an adjusted $\text{R}^2=0.4124$ and an $\text{RMSE}=0.0083$. Since there is no direct, predicable relationship between maximum contraction and $l_0$, this value should be recorded in each muscle used on a robot to best predict the force it may produce at different pressures and contraction. 


\subsection{Force as a Function of Pressure and Contraction}

In addition to being a function of the pressure and resting length, the force produced by the actuator is also a function of the amount of actuator contraction, with less force being applied as the actuator contracts. 
To build this model, the collected data of force, pressure, and contraction are normalized by dividing by $F_{620}$, $P_{620}$, and $\epsilon_{620}$, respectively. This had the effect of compressing the data into a 3D surface ranging from 0 to 1 on all axes. Normalized data are compared with pressure isoclines of force predicted by the Festo tool divided by the maximum force equation described in the previous section in Figure~\ref{fig:GenForceFit}. With this comparison, it is clear that the Festo tool over-predicts the expected force, especially at lower pressures, contraction, and resting lengths.

\section{Isometric Knee Torque}\label{sec:results_torque}

With the actuator force model validated at the actuator level, the same model was used to predict isometric torque about the knee joint for the test stands described in Sections~\ref{sec:robot_model} and~\ref{sec:hybrid_method}. Measured torque is compared against three predictions: the original rigid-body torque model, a prediction using the improved BPA force characterization alone, and the improved torque model of Section~\ref{sec:improved_model} after optimization. The hybrid torque method (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_method}) is used to isolate the sources of error.

\subsection{Flexor on Pin Joint}

Experimentally measured torque is compared with various prediction methods while using flexor BPAs on the pinned knee joint (Fig. \ref{fig:FlxPin_group}). These results show that the original model and the improved BPA force model overpredict the measured torque at the knee for all joint angles, although the improved model performs slightly better (Fig. \ref{fig:FlxPin_group}\textbf{A} and\ref{fig:FlxPin_group}\textbf{B}). However, when the measured parameters ($l_\text{m}$ and $r_{k}$) are used in the hybrid model, then predictions closely match the experimental data. This indicates that the kinematics of the torque model are incorrectly predicting $l_\text{m}$ and/or $r_{k}$.

The torque prediction from the improved BPA characterization on the $l_{0}=\qty{45.7}{\cm}$ BPA has an $\text{RMSE}=2.9424$, $\text{FVU}=0.1545$, and a maximum absolute residual of $6.6968$.

\subsection{Extensor on Pin Joint}

When the new stiffness and length offet model was used to predict torque for the knee extensor, measured torque was slightly higher than the original model calculates for the \qty{48}{\cm} resting length BPA, while it was slightly lower than expected in the \qty{46}{\cm} BPA, and it was much lower in the \qty{42}{\cm}. Only the $K_{1}$ and $K_{2}$ terms from the previous optimization results were used with the extensor configuration. Firstly, by inspection, the zero point for the measured torque was shifted to the right of the original model, indicating an offset that was at least opposite in sign from the $\chi_{0}$ term in the previous section. Secondly, if we look at the BPA $l_{0}=\qty{46}{\cm}$ as an example, we see good correlation between the hybrid-method calculated torque and the originally predicted torque, but a divergence in the actual measured torque.

\subsection{Redesigned-Route Results on the Gait2392 Robot-Body Model}\label{sec:route_redesign_results}

The preceding paragraphs record the adopted correction terms and margin targets; this subsection records the quantitative outcomes of the redesigned routes on the Gait2392-derived robot-body model, whose muscle force and torque targets define the redesign objectives. All three result families were produced by the surrogate-plus-pattern-search pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:optimization} under the adopted correction terms (Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry}, Table~\ref{tab:app_xi_values}) with one shared actuator plan: two \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs per route, a \qty{620}{\kPa} pressure ceiling, and a maximum usable contraction (KMAX) of 0.255 in the force model. \section{Balance Platform and Inverted-Pendulum Demonstrations}\label{sec:results_balance}

The testbeds of Section~\ref{sec:balance_testbeds} were demonstrated by the author and presented in three conference posters \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}; this section summarizes what they showed.

The first-generation pendulum --- hinge ankle, selectable encoder/IMU angle feedback, and reverse-pulley transmission --- demonstrated self-balancing: under proportional control of the antagonist \qty{20}{\mm} BPA pair with IMU pseudo-vestibular feedback, the leg held itself upright on flat ground, and balance was maintained when the same controller operated on the oscillating balance platform beneath the foot. The second generation, IP 2.0 --- ball-and-socket ankle, the three-muscle plantarflexor/inverter/everter arrangement, and IMU-only feedback --- also maintained balance, on flat terrain. Both demonstrations used the same family of Festo BPAs characterized in Section~\ref{sec:results_force}.

These demonstrations establish that balance of a BPA-actuated leg under pseudo-vestibular inertial feedback is achievable in hardware on both fixed and oscillating supports. They are reported qualitatively, however: the poster materials quote no balance durations, platform oscillation amplitudes or frequencies, ankle-angle envelopes, or valve and pressure levels, so no quantitative values are claimed here. Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework} defines an extension protocol that measures disturbance rejection, sensor drift, and sustained operation under continuous platform oscillation before comparing the same feedback architecture on a full humanoid model.

\subsection{BPA-Actuated Walker in MuJoCo}\label{sec:bpa_walker}

The walker geometry of Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker} was exported to MuJoCo and driven by antagonistic BPA actuator pairs under the split contact-driven network, with actuator forces computed by the BPA model of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}. In the air-stepping gate --- the harness carrying 70\% of the \qty{411}{\N} body weight --- the walker produced clean knee-flexion excursions at a \qty{1.542}{\second} period with a knee flexion range of \ang{63.5}, peak BPA forces of \qty{880}{\N} (left) and \qty{1372}{\N} (right) at a peak pressure of \qty{372}{\kPa} --- 60\% of the \qty{620}{\kPa} ceiling --- and 7 and 5 heel-contact episodes on the left and right. This run demonstrates the complete chain from the characterized BPA force model to a multi-joint walker stepping under synthetic neural control, with actuator sizing operating inside the characterized pressure range.

The same campaign reported its failures with equal weight. On the ground, the W2L gate model stands (full-support stance) but does not walk: heel duty is zero and forward displacement is $+\qty{0.001}{\meter}$ over the run. The MuJoCo port of the Li model produced no stance episodes in any of seven drop-protocol variants. Together with the latch result of Section~\ref{sec:crossengine_results}, these negatives localize the open problem precisely: the contact-driven rhythm architecture itself transfers across engines intact, but translating it to unsupported ground locomotion requires the balance and weight-bearing layers defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework}, and those layers are not present in these models.

\subsection{Gait-Validation Sweep against the Locomotion Reference Library}\label{sec:gait_library_results}

The four production walking configurations of the converted robot-body model were evaluated against a library of 43 human locomotion reference cycles: 32 running trials from eight subjects at four speeds, ten walking references (seven at prescribed speeds from \qty{0.5}{\metre\per\second} to \qty{2.0}{\metre\per\second}, plus three self-selected), and one further reference cycle, \texttt{Case\_40\_motion}, the predicted-walking cycle of the Falisse predictive-simulation dataset (period \qty{1.11}{\second}, duty factor 0.57). The four configurations span the two controller lineages of this program. \section{Spiking-Neuron Mirror Results}\label{sec:spiking_results}

The spiking-neuron mirrors of Section~\ref{sec:spiking_methods} were evaluated against their non-spiking twins in the same order in which the families were introduced: the MuJoCo spinal network, the Simulink demonstrations, and the AnimatLab walker family. All numbers in this section come from the gated verification campaign whose artifacts and reproduction commands are catalogued in Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations}.

\subsection{MuJoCo Spinal-Network Mirror}

The non-spiking production baselines were first re-established on the machine of record: all three current production configurations reproduce their documented evaluation scores exactly, including the default build's kinematic score of $-161.56754173676563$, and after the mirror's selector was added the default build gate ($(410, 376, 1186)$ populations, inputs, and connections) and that same baseline score reproduce digit for digit, proving the non-spiking pipeline untouched.

The topology gate passes exactly: under the default, fully tuned, and joint-layer pattern-formation gain configurations, the spiking mirror's directed connection multiset is identical to the non-spiking build's ($1{,}186$, $7{,}908$, and $7{,}762$ connections respectively), with identical input lists, identical motor-neuron maps, and exactly the expected number of readout taps (12, 12, and 16). The mirror is structurally the same network.

Under constant drive (network alone, \qty{20}{\second}), the rhythm gate splits. The spiking mirror self-sustains clean bilateral antiphase --- rhythm-generator extensor and flexor cells firing at \qty{4.9}{\hertz} and \qty{3.2}{\hertz} per leg, with the swing readout sustained in every window --- but its envelope period is \qty{0.621}{\second} against the analog build's \qty{0.399}{\second}, a \qty{55.6}{\percent} lengthening that misses the gate's \qty{20}{\percent} tolerance (Figure~\ref{fig:spike_rhythm}). The basin gate passes in full: the rhythm persists under \qty{1}{\percent} multiplicative jitter on every scalar parameter in all 12 seeded trials, with a final-window swing minimum of \qty{4.58}{\mV} against the \qty{5.13}{\mV} baseline (margin $0.89\times$) and no divergent trials. In the deafferented suspended-air preparation, the mirror air-steps rhythmically with full-amplitude joint swings (knee range \ang{106.0} against the twin's \ang{117.9}), but its cadence is roughly ten times faster than the non-spiking twin's \qty{0.47}{\hertz} air-stepping regime in this preparation, so the air-regime gate fails on the cadence clause alone.

\subsection{AnimatLab Spiking Conversions}

The AnimatLab family produces the campaign's clearest negative result, and it is reported as such. The graded baselines were re-established first: the two-layer walker's rhythm generator oscillates at \qty{0.443}{\second} (\qty{2.26}{\hertz}), the bilateral variant at \qty{0.462}{\second} with the two legs folded near antiphase (lag $-0.42$ cycle, $r = 0.869$), and the already-spiking 2023-lineage model --- the family's spiking reference --- bursts at \qty{1.305}{\second} with folded antiphase of $+0.494$ cycle ($r = 0.703$), reproducing its period of record. The current reorganized project's standalone export remains latched under the tonic drives examined, consistent with Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}.

No spiking copy of the three graded models preserves the rhythm (Table~\ref{tab:spike_animatlab}). The two-layer conversions die at every threshold and decay tried (two or zero rhythm-generator spikes in \qty{5}{\second}); the bilateral variant latches with one side's half-centers saturated at \qty{442}{\hertz}--\qty{450}{\hertz}; and the reorganized project's copy converts its latched baseline into strictly synchronous \qty{11.8}{\hertz} co-bursting with no alternation --- the left extensor and flexor half-centers emit identical 58-spike trains whose spike-count envelopes correlate at $r = 1.000$ at zero lag. The thresholds-only ablation, which keeps the graded synapses, preserves near-baseline joint oscillations, isolating the synapse conversion as the breaking change (Figure~\ref{fig:spike_animatlab}): per-spike conductances at the graded amplitude either starve the reciprocal loop of inhibitory charge or saturate it, whereas the graded synapses read the membrane potential continuously and keep oscillating through subthreshold voltages. A spiking version of this family therefore needs its synaptic amplitudes and decays re-tuned as a rate code --- which is how the 2023 spiking reference was built in the first place --- rather than a parameter port.
