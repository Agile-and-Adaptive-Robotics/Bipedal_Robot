This chapter connects the actuator force characterization of Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles} to torque about the robot knee. The rigid-body torque model of the earlier robot work assumed inextensible muscle paths on rigid links, and the isometric tests reported here show where that assumption fails and by how much. The chapter proceeds from the simple pinned knee to the biomimetic joint. It first presents the mesh-optimization method that places the actuator attachment routes, a method that is compliance-unaware in its origin, and then the identification of the correction terms $\chi_{0}$ through $\chi_{3}$ that removes the rigid-body idealization from the torque prediction. The identified terms are validated on held-out tests, the route optimizers are updated to carry them, and the chapter closes with the redesigned routes and their physical torque tests. The methods, results, and discussion follow the manuscript noted above and include the physical-validation sweeps of the redesigned routes.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Robot Leg Model and Knee Test Stands}\label{sec:robot_model}


The primary components of the robot leg are the knee joint, femur, tibia, BPA assemblies, and artificial tendons (Fig. \ref{fig:testJigs}\textbf{A--C}). The artificial bone components are 3D printed using a combination of Onyx, carbon fiber, and high-strength high-temperature fiberglass on Markforged Onyx One and Mark Two printers, as well as machined links for the knee mechanism. Onyx is a proprietary Markforged material that consists of chopped carbon fiber in nylon. Artificial tendons are made with Shimano bicycle brake cable.

The leg assembly geometry and dimensions are based on bones scans of a $6 \, \text{ft}$  (\qty{1.83}{\metre}) tall person \citep{bolen_determination_2019,seth_opensim_2018,delp_interactive_1990,delp_opensim_2007}. Two knee joints were tested, one pure rotational joint, and one biomimetic joint with rotation and translation. Each joint is driven by two antagonistic Festo actuators. Both robot joints have the same center of rotation in the femur frame at $\theta_{\mathbf{k}}=0 \deg$.

Details on the biomimetic robot knee used in this study can be found in \citep{steele_biomimetic_2018,steele_development_2017,steele_biomimetic_2018-1}. In brief, this 1-DoF joint (Fig. \ref{fig:ICR}) uses a four-bar linkage to change the effective moment arm during rotation. The intersection point of the internal crossed links define the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR). This causes the joint ICR location in the femur frame to translate posteriorly in the $X$ direction and distally in the $Y$ direction except for a brief proximal move near full flexion. Reference International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) coordinate frames are given by \citet{wu_for_1995,wu_isb_2002}. This is similar to how the human knee behaves \citep{bolen_determination_2019,morrow_optimization_2020,seth_opensim_2018,delp_opensim_2007,delp_interactive_1990}. The correct location of the ICR is important for both theoretical and experimental torque calculations.

In the pinned knee joint, uniarticular knee extensor and knee flexor are used to actuate the joint. Each knee joint type was tested with a single $\diameter$\qty{10}{\mm} Festo BPA per muscle. The biomimetic knee was additionally tested with $\diameter$\qty{20}{\mm} Festo BPAs in a paired configuration: the extensor with two BPAs in parallel, and the flexor with two BPAs whose pathways are mirrored about the $Z$ axis. Each artificial muscle is pinned at the muscle origin location on the femur while the other end is either pinned or attached to the muscle insertion location on the tibia via an artificial tendon.

Torque about the knee is generated by the moment of the BPA force about the joint's instantaneous center of rotation (ICR). Two vectors enter that moment: the position vector $\mathbf{d}$, drawn from the ICR to the point where the actuator force is applied, and the force vector $\mathbf{F} = \norm{\mathbf{F}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}$, whose direction $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ is the line of action of the actuator. The moment of the force about the joint is $\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{d} \times \mathbf{F}$, and its magnitude is the force magnitude multiplied by the moment arm $r$, the perpendicular distance from the joint axis to the line of action,

\begin{equation}
    r = \norm{\mathbf{d} \times \hat{\mathbf{u}}}
    \label{eq:momentarm_def}
\end{equation}

The moment arm is therefore a scalar, and it is the same quantity whether it is computed from the position vector with Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentarm_def} or measured directly with a tape measure. In this study it is calculated using the method specified by \citet{young_analyzing_2019}. The scalar moment arm $r_{\mathbf{k}}$ about the joint axis $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{k}}$ (i.e., the $Z$ axis in the knee frame) is calculated as 

\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathbf{k}} = \mathbf{p}_{proj,xy} \cdot \frac{\mathbf{p}_{f} \times \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{k}}} { \norm{\mathbf{p}_{f} \times \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{k}}} } \label{eq:project_ma}
\end{equation}

A muscle path may be modeled as a single segment running from origin to insertion, or as a chain of segments joined at via points. The segment that crosses from one reference frame into the other, here from the femur frame to the tibia frame, is the free muscle segment: because its endpoints ride on different bodies, its length and its direction respond directly to joint rotation, whereas a segment contained within one body's frame rotates rigidly with that body and changes neither. Here $\mathbf{p}_{f}$ is the force-path segment vector, and $\mathbf{p}_{proj,xy}$ locates a point on that line relative to the joint, projected onto the plane normal to the joint axis. The normalized cross product in Equation~\eqref{eq:project_ma} is the in-plane normal to the force path. Its dot product with the projected position gives the signed moment arm (Figure~\ref{fig:masters_routing_and_arms}). Moment arm is calculated in the tibial body frame.

Details of the existing model and the torque calculation methodology in the modeled robot are given by \citet{bolen_determination_2019, morrow_optimization_2020}.

\subsection{Knee Torque Data Collection and Equipment}\label{sec:torque_equipment}


A test jig was built to collect knee torque measurements at different knee angles over its range of motion. The test stand frame is made predominantly out of 80/20 1515 series extruded aluminum components. The knee joint is free to rotate the tibia while the femur is fixed to the frame. A force sensor has one end connected to the tibia and the other is connected to a swing arm. The swing arm is tied to a winch with $3/16\; \text{inch}$ Kevlar rope from Quality Nylon Rope routed over intermediate idler pulleys (see Fig. \ref{fig:testJigs}\textbf{B} and \ref{fig:testJigs}\textbf{C}). At each measured position of $\theta_{\mathbf{k}}$ the winch would be locked and the BPA would be inflated and then deflated. This test procedure kept the measured results as isometric as possible.  The knee joint is co-axial with gravity to eliminate gravitational torque (see Fig. \ref{fig:testJigs}\textbf{A} and Fig. \ref{fig:testJigs}\textbf{C}).

Force data was collected using one of two different sensors. The first is a MODERN STEP $\qty{300}{\kilo\gram}$ digital crane scale. The second force sensor is a CALT DYLY-103 $\qty{100}{\kilo\gram}$ S shaped load cell. The load cell is used in conjunction with a HX711 Load Cell Amplifier. Pressure is measured with a Freescale MPX5700 GP $\qty{5}{\volt}$ pressure sensor. Building air supply pressure is controlled with two pressure regulators in series. The first is a Parker model 20R113GC $0-120\; \text{psi}$ pressure regulator. The second is a Husky $3/8 \; \text{inch}$ High Performance Air Regulator HDA72200. A Festo VTUG-10-MRCR-S1T-26V20-T516LA-UL-T532S-8K valve manifold was used to supply air to the actuator. Pressure and load cell amplifier data are sent to MATLAB via an Arduino Uno style Sparkfun BlackBoard C microcontroller. 


The length measurements were made using a tape measure. When there was sufficient curvature in the BPA during knee extensor torque tests, a flexible piece of string was used to mark the axial length and then transferred to the tape measure. Angle measurements were taken with a Medigauge digital electronic goniometer or with MALENOO analog goniometers of 6, 8, or 12 inch lengths.  

\subsection{Hybrid Torque Calculation Method}\label{sec:hybrid_method}


A Hybrid Torque calculation method was developed to help uncover inconsistencies between the model and recorded torque values. This method measures the actual muscle length and angles of the robot to calculate force and joint torque. With this method, it is easier to determine which parts of the torque equation did not achieve their predicted value and provide a correction to that part of the equation. The classical mechanics way to calculate the torque about a joint is to take the cross product of the position vector $\mathbf{d}$ and the force vector $\mathbf{F}$, both defined in Section~\ref{sec:robot_model},

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{m} = \mathbf{d} \times \mathbf{F} \label{eq:M_vec}
\end{equation}

Knee torque is the scalar component of this moment about the joint axis, written $M_{\mathbf{k}}$ to make its axis explicit. Because the moment arm is the perpendicular distance from that axis to the line of action, the vector cross product of Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_vec} reduces to an ordinary scalar product,

\begin{equation}
    M_{\mathbf{k}} = r\,F \label{eq:M_scalar}
\end{equation}

where $r$ is the scalar moment arm of Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentarm_def}, here measured directly with a tape measure as the perpendicular distance from the knee axis to the actuator's line of action, and $F$ is the scalar force magnitude, calculated by measuring the BPA's $P$, $l_{0}$, $l_{620}$ and $l_\text{m}$ values and using the model fit equations determined in the following section. The hybrid method therefore replaces the model-predicted moment arm $r_{\mathbf{k}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:project_ma} and the model-predicted muscle length with their measured counterparts, leaving the force model as the only predicted quantity.

\subsection{Improved Torque Model}\label{sec:improved_model}

The classical kinematic torque model assumed rigid links and inextensible muscle paths. Data collected during the first flexor experiments (Section~\ref{sec:results_torque}) showed that this assumption does not hold for a pneumatic system: measured muscle lengths were consistently shorter than predicted, and measured torque deviated from prediction in a load-dependent manner. This section describes the corrections that were developed to account for a constant length offset, series compliance in the attachment hardware and artificial tendons, and the loss of usable muscle length when a BPA wraps around the joint.

These corrections are deliberately formulated to be identified from experiment rather than derived analytically, because the physical effects they represent are distributed and not separately measurable. The lumped compliance absorbs the cantilevered three-dimensional-printed attachment brackets, whose geometry is a printed CAD part rather than a prismatic beam with a tractable second moment of area, together with the bone fixtures, the cable winch of the test stand, and the artificial tendon. Predicting that aggregate stiffness from first principles would require finite-element models of each component with material properties (anisotropic printed polymer, braided sleeve, construction-dependent tendon stiffness) that are themselves unmeasured. The correction terms therefore parameterize only what the torque model needs: $\chi_{0}$ absorbs the constant length discrepancy observed at zero load, $\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$ are effective stiffnesses in the bracket frame's principal directions, and $\chi_{3}$ scales the wrapping-length loss. Each is identified by global optimization against the measured torque rather than solved in closed form, for two reasons. First, the terms enter the prediction through an implicit force balance, because the compliance displacement depends on the force that it itself modifies, so the inverse problem has no analytic solution. Second, the parameters are only jointly identifiable: preliminary fits showed that widely different stiffness pairs can reproduce the training tests nearly equally well, so the adopted solution must be selected by cross-validated prediction on held-out tests rather than by residual minimization alone, which is what the multiobjective Pareto-front procedure of Section~\ref{sec:identification} provides.

\subsubsection{Length Offset and System Stiffness}\label{sec:offset_stiffness}

Analysis of measured muscle length data shows that it was consistently shorter than the predicted muscle length at all joint angles. This shorter length results in less muscle force, a shifted RoM, and less torque in the experimental setup. That this shorter length occurs when $\varepsilon=1$ and $F=0$ indicates that a constant length offset should be used. Therefore, to produce an improved torque prediction model, $l_\text{m}$ was recalculated as

\begin{equation}
    l_\text{m} = l_{LMT} - 2\;l_{fitting} - \chi_{0} 
    \label{eq:Lm}
\end{equation}

where $l_{LMT}$ is the path length of the musculotendon from origin to insertion point, $l_{fitting}$ is the length of the end caps, and $\chi_{0}$ is a constant length offset. 

Additionally, as the muscles produce more force, compliant components in the limb segments and attachment hardware deform. This deformation changes the effective braided pneumatic actuator (BPA) length, which alters contraction and therefore changes the force predicted by the BPA model. This effect is most apparent at high forces and low contractions. The purpose of the compliance model developed next is predictive, not structural: it exists so that the torque calculation can correct the actuator length for load-dependent deformation, and it makes no claim about the stiffness of any one component in isolation. Accordingly, the stiffness values that populate the model, $\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$, are not measured on the hardware directly; they are identified by the optimization of Section~\ref{sec:identification} from the measured torque, and every stiffness value quoted later in this chapter is an optimizer output. The combined hardware compliance is approximated using Hooke's law in the bracket's principal directions.

A local bracket frame was established at the attachment bracket. The $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{br}$ axis was aligned with the vector from the bracket reference point to the BPA attachment point and represents the stiffest direction of the bracket. The $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{br}$ axis was chosen to point in the posterior direction with respect to the tibia frame, corresponding to the primary cantilever bending direction (see Fig.~\ref{fig:bktFrame}), while the $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{br}$ axis completes the right-handed frame. In this frame the bracket is modeled as three independent linear springs along the principal axes, with stiffnesses $k_{x}$, $k_{y}$, and $k_{z}$ in the $x_{br}$, $y_{br}$, and $z_{br}$ directions.

For the two-rotation evaluator, the bracket frame was constructed from the vector joining the bracket origin to attachment point $\mathbf{p}_{A}$ or $\mathbf{p}_{B}$. The first rotation, through $\theta_z$ about the fixed space-frame axis $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{s}$, points the intermediate axis $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}$ toward the sagittal-plane projection, $\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\mathrm{sag}}$ for the origin point $\mathbf{p}_{A}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{B}^{\mathrm{sag}}$ for the insertion point $\mathbf{p}_{B}$. The second rotation, through $-\theta_y$ about the current axis $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{1}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{br}$, brings the final bracket axis $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{br}$ into alignment with the full three-dimensional point vector. Figure~\ref{fig:xiFrameTransform} shows these geometric targets. The resulting orientation and homogeneous transform are

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}_{sb}=\mathbf{R}_z(\theta_z)\mathbf{R}_y(\theta_y)^T,
\qquad
\mathbf{T}_{sb}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{R}_{sb} & \mathbf{p}_{br}\\
\mathbf{0}^{T} & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:bracket_frame_transform}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}_{br} = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(k_{x},\; k_{y},\; k_{z}\right)
\label{eq:bktstiffness_general}
\end{equation}

The principal stiffnesses are parameterized by the two fitted values $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$, with axis assignments fixed by configuration (Table~\ref{tab:app_stiffness_arrays}). For the pinned-flexor insertion bracket, the assignment is $k_x=k_z=\chi_1$ and $k_y=\chi_2$. This $y$-symmetric array is invariant to a change of frame orientation about $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{br}$, so the one- and two-rotation constructions give the same compliance for this assignment. That invariance does not hold for the other assignments. In particular, the pinned-flexor origin bracket uses $[\chi_1,\chi_1,\chi_2]$, the pinned extensor and biomimetic flexor use $[\chi_1,\chi_2,\chi_2]$, and the biomimetic extensor uses the insertion-bracket assignment. Both pinned-flexor brackets use the one-rotation frame in the evaluator of record; the pinned extensor uses the two-rotation frame. The general projected-compliance calculation below applies to each assignment, with the appropriate local matrix. For the insertion bracket,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}_{br} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\chi_{1} & 0   & 0 \\
0   & \chi_{2} & 0 \\
0   & 0   & \chi_{1} \\
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:bktstiffness}
\end{equation}

The corresponding local compliance matrix is 

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}_{br} = \mathbf{K}_{br}^{-1}
\label{eq:bktcompliance}
\end{equation}

The force applied to the bracket was expressed in the bracket frame as $\mathbf{F}_{br}$. This force vector can be written in terms of its magnitude and unit direction as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{br} = F \, \hat{\mathbf{u}}
\label{eq:bktforcevector}
\end{equation}
\noindent
where $F = \norm{\mathbf{F}}$ is the scalar force magnitude of Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_scalar} and $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = [u_{x}, u_{y}, u_{z}]^{T}$ is the BPA force direction expressed in the bracket frame. The bracket deflection vector, $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br}$, was calculated from Hooke's law as 

\begin{equation} \mathbf{F}_{br} = \mathbf{K}_{br} \: \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br}
\label{eq:bktvectorhooke}
\end{equation}

Solving Eq. \ref{eq:bktvectorhooke} for the bracket deflection gives

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br} = \mathbf{K}_{br}^{-1} \: \mathbf{F}_{br} = \mathbf{C}_{br} \: \mathbf{F}_{br}
\label{eq:bktdeflection}
\end{equation}

Substituting Eq. \ref{eq:bktforcevector} into Eq. \ref{eq:bktdeflection} gives

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br} = F \,\mathbf{K}_{br}^{-1} \,\hat{\mathbf{u}}
\label{eq:bktdeflectionforceunit}
\end{equation}

For the diagonal stiffness matrix in Eq. \ref{eq:bktstiffness}, Eq. \ref{eq:bktdeflection} is equivalent to the component-wise expression

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br} = \begin{bmatrix} F\,u_{x}/\chi_{1} \\ F\,u_{y}/\chi_{2} \\ F\,u_{z}/\chi_{1} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:bktcomponentdeflection}
\end{equation}

Although $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br}$ is a three-dimensional deflection vector, the BPA force model depends on the scalar change in effective BPA length ($\delta$). Therefore, only the component of $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br}$ along the BPA line of action directly changes the effective BPA length. This scalar bracket deflection is the projection of $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br}$ onto $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$:

\begin{equation}
{\delta}_{br} = \hat{\mathbf{u}}^{T} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{br}
\label{eq:bktprojecteddeflection}
\end{equation}

Substituting Eq. \ref{eq:bktdeflection} into Eq. \ref{eq:bktprojecteddeflection} gives

\begin{equation}
{\delta}_{br} = \hat{\mathbf{u}}^{T} \, \mathbf{K}_{br}^{-1} \, \mathbf{F}_{br}
\label{eq:bktprojecteddeflection2}
\end{equation}

Using Eq. \ref{eq:bktforcevector}, Eq. \ref{eq:bktprojecteddeflection2} becomes

\begin{equation}
\delta_{br} = F\, \hat{\mathbf{u}}^{T} \, \mathbf{K}_{br}^{-1} \, \hat{\mathbf{u}}
\label{eq:bktprojectedcompliance}
\end{equation}

The scalar stiffness along the BPA line of action due to the bracket compliance is therefore

\begin{equation}
k_{\hat{u},br} = \frac{F}{\delta_{br}} = \left( \hat{\mathbf{u}}^{T} \mathbf{K}_{br}^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{u}} \right)^{-1}
\label{eq:keff}
\end{equation}

This projected-compliance form is used because the force is applied along $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$, while the bracket is allowed to deflect independently along its local $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{br}$, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{br}$, and $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{br}$ directions. The reduced-order Hooke's law expression for the bracket deflection along the BPA line of action is therefore

\begin{equation} F = k_{eq} \: \delta
\label{eq:hookereduced}
\end{equation}

Here $k_{eq}$ is the equivalent stiffness of every compliant element in the actuator path, and the bracket-only case is the special case with no tendon. When the BPA attaches directly to the bracket, $k_{eq}$ excludes the tendon and equals $k_{\hat{u},br}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:keff}. When an artificial tendon was used, it was modeled as an axial spring with stiffness $k_{ten}$: bracket deflection and tendon stretch both shorten the effective BPA length along the actuator line of action, the two elements carry the same force, and their scalar compliances add in series,

\begin{equation} k_{eq} = \left( \frac{1}{k_{\hat{u},br}} + \frac{1}{k_{ten}} \right)^{-1}
\label{eq:equivalentstiffness}
\end{equation}

This series reduction is exact only under a specific set of conditions, each of which corresponds to a way the physical hardware could violate it. The bracket and the tendon must carry the same force, which holds for two massless elements coupled in series under quasi-static loading; an additional compliant coupling would require its own term in the series compliance. Their displacements must be kinematically additive along the line of action, with no displacement lost to off-axis geometry. Within a single iteration of the force balance the line of action $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ is treated as fixed, which is a local approximation: once the balance returns the compliance displacement, the calculation updates the attachment locations with the deflected bracket positions and re-evaluates $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ and the moment arm at the deflected geometry, as described below. The elements are assumed to carry no moment, so rotational compliance and force--moment coupling at the attachments are neglected. Both elements are linear, with stiffness independent of deflection, and the tendon is modeled without preload, so its tangent stiffness does not stiffen with load state. Finally, compliance deflection shortens the actuator, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:activelengthcorrected} assumes, because a BPA produces force only in tension: under its own pulling force the compliant attachments give way in the direction that relieves that tension. A deflection that would lengthen the actuator can be produced by an antagonistic pull, but that loading lies outside this treatment.

Since $\delta$ is the scalar compliance displacement along the BPA line of action, then the compliance-corrected active BPA length used in the force balance is

\begin{equation} l_m(\delta) = l_m{(0)} - \delta
\label{eq:activelengthcorrected}
\end{equation}

where $l_m{(0)}$ is the initial muscle length calculated from \eqref{eq:Lm}.

For a specified set of correction parameters, the following calculation solves the attachment-compliance displacement at each knee angle; it is distinct from the optimization that identifies those parameters from experimental torque data (Section~\ref{sec:identification}). The corresponding relative contraction is $\varepsilon^*(\delta)$. Since the BPA force depends on contraction, and contraction depends on the compliance displacement, $\delta$ is the root of the nonlinear force-balance equation

\begin{equation} F_{\mathrm{max}} \cdot F_{\mathrm{BPA}}\!\left(\varepsilon^*(\delta),P^*\right) - k_{eq}\,\delta = 0
\label{eq:complianceforcebalance}
\end{equation}

The root is found with a bracketed scalar solver (\texttt{fzero} in MATLAB) on the physically admissible interval between zero deflection and the deflection that would bring the actuator to its maximum usable contraction. The evaluator supplies no custom solver options, so termination uses the default displacement tolerance. This numerical tolerance does not establish the accuracy of the physical model. Two safeguards act as secondary stop conditions. If the actuator already sits at or beyond its maximum usable contraction at zero deflection, no root is sought and $\delta$ is set to zero; if the residual shows no sign change across the interval, the solver likewise returns $\delta = 0$ rather than a spurious root.

After solving Eq. \ref{eq:complianceforcebalance}, the corresponding force magnitude was used in Eq. \ref{eq:bktdeflection} to compute the vector bracket deflection. This deflection was transformed back into the limb reference frame and used to update the BPA attachment location. The routed BPA length, force direction, moment arm, and torque were then recalculated using the updated attachment location. In the present implementation, the optimized stiffness terms $\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$ were treated as effective compliance parameters for the attachment system rather than direct measurements of an isolated bracket. This allowed unmodeled compliance in the surrounding hardware to be represented by the reduced-order stiffness model.

The actuator path can carry compliant hardware at both ends: the insertion-side bracket of the preceding development together with a second, origin-side bracket. Figure~\ref{fig:xiProjection} distinguishes the three-dimensional displacement of each bracket from the scalar displacement along the BPA force path. For bracket $j$, the displacement resolves into components $\eta_{1,j}$, $\eta_{2,j}$, and $\eta_{3,j}$ along the local bracket-frame basis, whereas only its projection $\delta_{br,j}$ along the corresponding force direction $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_j$ changes the actuator length. The two bracket projections act in series with the tendon elongation $\delta_{\mathrm{tendon}}$:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{br,j} &= \sum_{i=1}^{3}\eta_{i,j}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i^{(j)},\\
\delta_{br,j} &= \hat{\mathbf{u}}_j^T\boldsymbol{\eta}_{br,j}, \qquad j\in\{1,2\},\\
\delta_{\mathrm{series}} &= \delta_{br,1}+\delta_{\mathrm{tendon}}+\delta_{br,2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bracket_series_projection}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{BPA Fictive Contraction}\label{sec:fictive_contraction}

As the knee flexes, the extensor BPA contacts the anterior joint surface and bends around it (Figure~\ref{fig:bpaWrapping}). The rigid routed-length calculation does not account for the resulting change in usable contraction. I represent this effect with fictive strain: an empirical increase in the contraction seen by the force model, beyond the actuator's geometric contraction. The correction is phenomenological; it does not resolve wall stresses or the deformation of the braided actuator. A wrapping-loss term $\chi_3$ scales the associated reduction in usable length:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta l = \begin{cases}
     \chi_{3}\;R\;\abs{ \theta_{\mathrm{wrap}}-\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\;\zeta^2 & \theta_{\mathbf{k}} \leq      \theta_{\mathrm{wrap}} \\
      0 & \theta_{\mathbf{k}} \geq \theta_{\mathrm{wrap}}
     \end{cases}\label{eq:deltaL}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta l$ is the change in usable length of the BPA, $R$ is the wrapping radius, $\abs{\theta_{\mathrm{wrap}}-\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}$ is the angle through which the BPA is bent once contact begins, and $\theta_{\mathrm{wrap}}$ is the knee flexion angle at which the BPA starts to bend over the knee joint, determined from solid modeling. $\zeta$ is the additive complement to relative strain, such that

\begin{equation}
    \zeta = 1-\varepsilon^*  \\
\label{eq:comp}
\end{equation}

so the fitted wrapping loss grows quadratically toward resting length and vanishes at the normalized free-contraction limit. Combining \eqref{eq:comp} into \eqref{eq:deltaL}, and subtracting $\Delta l$ from  \eqref{eq:Lm} yields

\begin{equation}
    l_\text{m} = l_{LMT} - 2\;l_{fitting}  - \chi_{0} - \Delta l\label{eq:x3}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Identification of the Torque-Model Correction Terms}\label{sec:identification}

I identified the correction terms in two stages using \texttt{gamultiobj} in MATLAB. The flexor stage fits $(\chi_0,\chi_1,\chi_2)$ with compliance at both attachments. The adopted fold trains on the $l_0=\qty{48.5}{\cm}$ and \qty{41.0}{\cm} tests and validates on the \qty{45.7}{\cm} and \qty{40.6}{\cm} tendon tests. The extensor stage locks $(\chi_1,\chi_2)$ to that same flexor-front member and fits only $(\chi_0,\chi_3)$. Its adopted fold trains on the \qty{43.6}{\cm}, \qty{43.2}{\cm}-tendon, and \qty{45.7}{\cm} tests and validates on the \qty{40.0}{\cm} plain, \qty{40.0}{\cm}-tendon, and \qty{46.5}{\cm} tests. The extensor length offset is allowed to be negative. Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry} records the test constants, bounds, stiffness-axis assignments, and adopted values in Tables~\ref{tab:app_flxpin_tests}, \ref{tab:app_extpin_tests}, \ref{tab:app_opt_bounds}, \ref{tab:app_stiffness_arrays}, and~\ref{tab:app_xi_values}.

An initial single-bracket identification preceded the two-bracket model. Its results are retained as historical comparisons, but the two-stage procedure defines the adopted correction terms. Each candidate is evaluated using RMSE, FVU, and maximum absolute residual. These metrics jointly measure torque error; none isolates a unique source such as level offset, waveform shape, or angular misalignment.

The objective vector of the identification is defined explicitly. For a candidate correction vector $\boldsymbol{\chi}$, $(\chi_{0}, \chi_{1}, \chi_{2})$ in the flexor stage and $(\chi_{0}, \chi_{3})$ on the locked stiffness pair in the extensor refit, the predicted torque curve of test $i$ is interpolated to its $n_{i}$ measured knee angles $\theta_{ij}$, giving residuals $e_{ij} = M_{ij} - \hat{M}(\theta_{ij}; \boldsymbol{\chi})$ against the measured torque $M_{ij}$, and the three goodness-of-fit components of that test are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}_{i} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{j} e_{ij}^{2}}, \qquad
\mathrm{FVU}_{i} = \frac{n_{i}-1}{n_{i}} \cdot \frac{\sum_{j} e_{ij}^{2}}{\sum_{j} (M_{ij} - \bar{M}_{i})^{2}}, \qquad
e^{\max}_{i} = \max_{j} \abs{e_{ij}}.
\label{eq:gof_components}
\end{equation}
RMSE summarizes the overall error magnitude. FVU expresses the residual sum of squares relative to the sum of squared deviations of the measured torque from its mean $\bar{M}_{i}$, with the factor $(n_{i}-1)/n_{i}$ carrying the small-sample adjustment of the evaluators. The maximum absolute residual records the largest pointwise error. RMSE and the maximum residual have torque units, whereas FVU is dimensionless. For a given test, FVU is proportional to squared RMSE, so the two metrics are not independent measures of shape and level. Across tests, their different scaling changes the relative weighting of the errors. The solver minimizes the objective vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\chi}) = \frac{1}{\abs{\mathcal{T}}} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{T}}
\begin{bmatrix}
g_{1,i}(\boldsymbol{\chi})/g_{1,i}^{(0)} \\
g_{2,i}(\boldsymbol{\chi})/g_{2,i}^{(0)} \\
g_{3,i}(\boldsymbol{\chi})/g_{3,i}^{(0)}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:gof_objective}
\end{equation}
where $g_{1,i}$, $g_{2,i}$, and $g_{3,i}$ are the RMSE, FVU, and maximum absolute residual of Equation~\eqref{eq:gof_components}, the superscript $(0)$ marks the zero-correction baseline value of the same component, the same kinematics and force model with every correction term disabled, and $\mathcal{T}$ is the training fold. The fold mean of the baseline-normalized components makes the three objectives dimensionless and the training tests commensurate. The search carried no constraints beyond the parameter bounds of each stage, and the returned Pareto fronts were filtered to members scoring at or below the zero-correction baseline on every component of every test in that stage's identification pool.

The solver is the genetic multiobjective solver \texttt{gamultiobj}, an implementation of the elitist nondominated-sorting genetic algorithm NSGA-II \citep{deb_nsga2_2002, mathworks_optimization_2025}, which returns the Pareto front from which the adopted solution is selected by the holdout-validation procedure using held-out tests. The production runs use adaptive-feasible mutation and scattered crossover at a fraction of 0.8, with MATLAB's default Pareto fraction of 0.35; the population settings are stage-specific. The flexor two-bracket fit evolves a population of 150 for up to 600 generations and terminates early once the geometric average relative change in the spread of the Pareto front falls below $4\times10^{-3}$; the extensor refit uses a population of 50 for up to 150 generations with a spread tolerance of $1\times10^{-3}$. In both stages the parameters are searched in a normalized space, with length offsets in centimeters and stiffnesses on a base-ten logarithmic scale, so that the genetic search samples the stiffness bounds, which span an order of magnitude, uniformly by decade, and the initial population is seeded around the expected solution region. The search bounds and the adopted values are recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry}.

\subsection{Compliance-Unaware Mesh-Optimization Route Design}\label{sec:mesh_opt}\label{sec:optimization}

The attachment locations of the BPAs are themselves design variables. Morrow and I developed the mesh-placement method as part of this work \citep{morrow_optimization_2020}. The placement problem was first solved by mesh optimization: candidate origins are enumerated on the femur bone-mesh surface and candidate insertions on the tibia bone-mesh surface, the torque profile of every candidate pair is evaluated over the range of motion with the straight-route muscle calculation, and the best-scoring pair is kept. In its original form this method is compliance-unaware. Routes are chains of via points on rigid links, the attachments are inextensible, and the predicted torque is the rigid-body prediction; nothing in the search knows that the hardware between actuator and joint bends, stretches, and settles under load. The implementation minimizes a cost that rewards torque surplus in the required direction and penalizes misalignment of the human and robot torque vectors,
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{mesh}} = -\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\,w_{\mathbf{k}}\,D_{\mathbf{k},j} + \sum_{a\in\{x,y\}} w_{a}\,D_{a,j}\right] + \frac{w_{u}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(1 - \hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{j}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}\right).
\label{eq:mesh_reward}
\end{equation}
where $j$ indexes the $N$ poses of the range of motion, $D_{a,j} = M^{\mathrm{R}}_{a,j} - M^{\mathrm{H}}_{a,j}$ when $M^{\mathrm{H}}_{a,j} \geq 0$ and $D_{a,j} = M^{\mathrm{H}}_{a,j} - M^{\mathrm{R}}_{a,j}$ otherwise, so that surplus is counted in the required direction for either sign of torque, and $\hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{j}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}$ are the unit torque vectors of the human reference and the candidate route. The knee-axis component carries weight $w_{\mathbf{k}} = 5$, the off-axis components carry $w_{a} = 0.25$, and the alignment term carries $w_{u} = 10$. The factor $3N$ matches the implementation's average over the three torque components at each pose. A zero robot-torque vector is assigned the direction opposite the human vector for the alignment term. Table~\ref{tab:mesh_original_weights} lists the terms of this cost.

The dissertation's route redesign re-derives this search as a constrained continuous optimization over the design vector $\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{p}_{1},\, \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{end}},\, l_{0},\, l_{\mathrm{tendon}}]$, the BPA origin $\mathbf{p}_{1}$, insertion $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{end}}$, resting length, and tendon length (Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry}), so that the flexor and extensor configurations meet or exceed human knee torque capability over the range of motion. Because the route prediction (contact activation, wrapping, and compliance correction) is expensive to evaluate and only moderately smooth, the redesign minimizes a single scalar cost assembled from weighted penalty terms. The cost has the form
\begin{equation}
    J = w_{w}\,J_{\text{worst}} + w_{t}\,J_{\text{torque}} + \textstyle\sum_{a} J_{a} + J_{\text{geom}} + J_{\text{len}},
    \label{eq:opt_cost}
\end{equation}
where $J_{\text{worst}}$ is the largest fraction by which robot torque falls short of the human target at any angle across the range of motion, weighted heavily ($w_{w} = 10^{5}$); $J_{\text{torque}}$ is the mean-squared shortfall fraction ($w_{t} = 10^{3}$), weighted two orders of magnitude below the worst-angle term so that no shortfall at a single angle can be traded away for a better average; the $J_{a}$ are configuration-specific auxiliary penalties; and $J_{\text{geom}}$ and $J_{\text{len}}$ are regularizers that keep the endpoints and actuator lengths near their seed values. The two leading terms are built from the normalized shortfall
\begin{equation}
s_{j} = \frac{\max\!\left(0,\; (1+m_{r})\abs{M^{\mathrm{H}}_{j}} - \abs{M^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}}\right)}{\tau},
\qquad
J_{\text{worst}} = \max_{j} s_{j},
\qquad
J_{\text{torque}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j} s_{j}^{2},
\label{eq:mesh_penalty_def}
\end{equation}
where $m_{r} = 0.05$ is the required torque margin and $\tau = \max(1, \max_{j}\abs{M^{\mathrm{H}}_{j}})$ is the human torque scale. The flexor and extensor configurations carry the remaining terms of Equation~\eqref{eq:opt_cost} as
\begin{align}
J_{\mathrm{flx}} =\;& 10^{5}J_{\text{worst}} + 10^{3}J_{\text{torque}} + 10^{-2}J_{\text{shape}} + J_{\text{off}} + J_{\text{rest}} + J_{\text{strain,hi}} \nonumber\\
& + J_{\text{strain,lo}} + J_{\text{wrap}} + J_{\text{geom}} + J_{\text{len}} + J_{\text{anchor}},
\label{eq:opt_cost_flx}\\[4pt]
J_{\mathrm{ext}} =\;& 10^{5}J_{\text{worst}} + 10^{3}J_{\text{torque}} + J_{\text{over}} + J_{\text{rest}} + J_{\text{strain,hi}} \nonumber\\
& + J_{\text{strain,lo}} + J_{\text{contr}} + J_{\text{geom}} + J_{\text{len}}.
\label{eq:opt_cost_ext}
\end{align}
Table~\ref{tab:mesh_penalties} lists every term with its weight and its meaning. Candidates whose route prediction fails to build, or whose prediction returns non-finite torque or strain, are rejected outright with prohibitive sentinel values rather than scored.

Human torque targets are computed from the Gait2392 lower-extremity model \citep{delp_opensim_2007, seth_opensim_2018}: the flexor is graded against the biceps femoris short head and the extensor against vastus medialis. Each target is a maximum-isometric torque curve: OpenSim moment arms, obtained as the partial derivative of muscle-tendon length with respect to the knee coordinate, $r(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}) = \partial l_{\mathrm{MT}}/\partial\theta_{\mathbf{k}}$, are evaluated across the range of motion and multiplied by each muscle's maximum isometric force (provenance and exported tables in Appendix~\ref{app:muscle_calcs}). Hard constraints (bone clearance, wrap feasibility, actuator strain and contraction limits, tendon geometry) are enforced alongside the cost, and any candidate whose prediction fails is rejected with a prohibitive penalty.

The solution pipeline is common to both configurations. Each run begins with a full sanity evaluation of the seed design and a small smoke test of the solver setup, then executes \texttt{surrogateopt}, MATLAB's surrogate-assisted global solver for expensive black-box objectives, which builds a spatial interpolation surrogate over sampled candidate points and searches it adaptively \citep{mathworks_optimization_2025}, in parallel under per-configuration evaluation budgets of 3,000 for the extensor route and 1,000 for the flexor route (the later cam variant of Section~\ref{sec:route_redesign_results} uses 1,000 surrogate and 1,500 refinement evaluations), and finally refines the best feasible point with \texttt{patternsearch}, a derivative-free mesh-adaptive direct search method \citep{audet_mads_2006, mathworks_optimization_2025}, for 15,000 further evaluations. The surrogate budgets sit well past the observed plateau of a few hundred evaluations, so they cap run time rather than define convergence. Each stage stops on its evaluation budget; the pattern search also stops early when its mesh collapses below the mesh tolerance with the constraints satisfied, which is the usual exit once the design has converged. The entry scripts and their cost functions are archived with the code.

Although both configurations share this pipeline, their cost functions differ in ways that reflect the mechanics. The flexor BPA routes around a single moving wrap point, so clearance from the tibia and femoral condyles is enforced by continuous signed-distance constraints, and its cost adds an off-axis torque penalty (torques about the joint axes other than the knee axis, relative to the original single-BPA route) and a penalty if the wrap fails to release cleanly as the knee extends. The extensor BPA wraps over the front of the joint through as many as seven sequential contacts, so clearance is enforced instead by the contact-elimination rule that governs the route, and its cost penalizes torque overshoot above the human curve and excessive actuator contraction, which risks bottoming out at high flexion. The flexor and extensor targets alike carry a required torque margin of 5\,\% above the human curve, so that a route that merely touches the target at any angle is rejected. With two BPAs per joint, the flexor route is solved as two separately evaluated paths, while the extensor model scales the force of a single shared route by the actuator count.

In the redesign runs of this dissertation, every candidate route of Equations~\eqref{eq:opt_cost_flx} and~\eqref{eq:opt_cost_ext} is evaluated through the stiffness-aware force balance of Section~\ref{sec:improved_model}, with the identified correction terms applied. The objective family itself descends from the compliance-unaware formulation of Equation~\eqref{eq:mesh_reward}: the search moves attachment geometry against a human torque envelope in both cases, and what changed between the two is the prediction that scores the geometry, not the design variables. That evolution, and the final flexor and extensor objectives built on it, are taken up in Section~\ref{sec:ongoing}.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Reasons for the Correction Terms}\label{sec:disc_error}

The correction terms of Section~\ref{sec:improved_model} exist because the physical transmission between the actuator and the joint is neither rigid nor lossless, and each term names one family of physical reasons. A constant length offset $\chi_{0}$ exists because of manufacturing and positioning tolerances: the printed bone segments, the machined knee links, and the end-cap fittings each carry dimensional tolerance, the actuator is pinned at a measured location on that stack of tolerances, and the accumulated discrepancy appears as a length that is consistently shorter than the predicted musculotendon path at every joint angle, including at zero load where no compliance can act. The stiffness terms $\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$ exist because loaded components displace: the isometric system is not rigid, and there is compliance in the winch cable of the test stand, the artificial tendons, and the cantilevered attachment brackets that bend and stretch under actuator load. The wrapping-loss term $\chi_{3}$ exists because a BPA that bends over the joint loses usable contraction length that no straight-line model accounts for, and because the same loading could displace the actuator off its intended seat, as the observed slip off the anterior head of the femur bolt shows.

Our improved BPA characterization predicts torque more accurately than the original BPA model (see Fig.~\ref{fig:FlxPin_preopt}). This only accounts for part of the error. Many factors affect the torque results. Compliance in the testing system, positioning tolerances, flexibility of brackets, imperfect path modeling, and variation in individual BPAs are other factors.

Simplifying and testing the model revealed deficiencies in our previous analysis. The isometric system is not rigid. There is compliance in the winch cable, artificial tendons, and brackets that bend and stretch. We introduced a constant length offset term $\chi_{0}$, axial stiffness $\chi_{1}$, and bending stiffness $\chi_{2}$. The bracket frame of reference was placed at the onset of the cantilevered section and rotated about $Z$.  The optimization identified parameter values for a $\diameter$\qty{10}{\mm} BPA with $l_{0}=\qty{48.5}{\cm}$ in the simple pinned knee configuration. These results were validated with a second $\diameter$\qty{10}{\mm} BPA with $l_{0}=\qty{45.7}{\cm}$.

The modeling of extensor BPA paths that wrap around the knee joint is an approximation with inherent error (Fig. \ref{fig:Ext_passthru}). When the moment arm is short, small changes to it can have a large effect on the resultant torque \citep{young_analyzing_2019}. It was assumed that wrapping the BPA around the joint would be easier to account for than adding a patella, deterministically designing its location as a function of knee angle, and accounting for friction. At greater knee flexion angles, shorter $l_{0}$ BPAs were observed to stretch and compress (see Fig.~\ref{fig:testJigs}\textbf{C}). The same loading sometimes caused the BPA to slip off the anterior head of the bolt that holds the femur to the knee. This produced a \qty{\pm 20}{\mm} displacement in the $Z$ direction, changing both the force vector and the torque. Fig. \ref{fig:ExtPin_group} shows a deviation between measured and originally predicted results that widens as expected torque increases. This indicates system compliance. However, the hybrid-calculated torque matches or exceeds the predicted torque, except at greater flexion angles for the $l_{0}=\qty{41.5}{\cm}$ BPA, indicating a loss of functional $l_m$; this is why the $\chi_{3}$ term was created in \eqref{eq:deltaL}. Fig. \ref{fig:ExtPin_group} shows the improved fit from running the optimization that includes this term.

The biomimetic knee joint with an extensor BPA was used to validate the extensor optimization results. The corrected prediction more closely matches the measured torque values, although it remains higher than the measurements.


The nonlinear biomimetic robotic system presented here replaces rigid-body simplifications commonly used in other robotic and biomechanical systems. Instead, progressing from simple to complex systems elucidated compliance mechanisms that can lead to more accurate first-iteration designs. Technologies have been developed to predict system bending and axial compliance, a constant length offset, and a loss of usable length that occurs as artificial muscle bends around a joint. 

The quasi-static force characterization also fixes the coefficients that a dynamic actuator model needs. Writing the actuator's contraction as a second-order system, $m\ddot \varepsilon^* + c (\dot \varepsilon^* ) \dot \varepsilon^* + k ( \varepsilon^* ) \varepsilon^* = F$, the stiffness term is the tangent slope of the normalized force surface of Equation~\eqref{eq:Fstar}, $k(\varepsilon^{*}, P^{*})
= \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial \varepsilon^{*}}\right|_{P^{*}}
= -c_{0}c_{1}e^{-c_{1}\varepsilon^{*}}
  - 2c_{2}\varepsilon^{*}P^{*}e^{-c_{2}(\varepsilon^{*})^{2}}$. Setting the mass and the external force to zero then yields the equivalent viscous damping coefficient, $c (\dot \varepsilon^* ) = (k ( \varepsilon^* ) \varepsilon^*)/\dot \varepsilon^*$, the term that dynamic state-space actuator models, such as the pressure--force formulation of \citet{elzein_pressure_2025}, pair with this stiffness.

The mesh-optimization solution that preceded this identification met the torque goal it was given, and its inability to deliver that torque in hardware is precisely what the correction terms repair. The compliance-unaware mesh optimizer of Section~\ref{sec:mesh_opt} places attachment routes whose rigid-body prediction meets or exceeds the magnitude of the human torque curves, and on the model it does: the exceed-human-torque-magnitude goal was achieved by the placement solutions it returned. The isometric tests of this chapter show what the hardware did with those solutions. Measured muscle lengths ran shorter than predicted, measured torque fell below prediction in a load-dependent manner, and the shortfall grew exactly where actuator force was highest. The goal had been met by a model that did not know the system bends; meeting it in hardware required a stiffness-aware prediction, and every optimizer update below is built on one.

The identified parameters themselves hold well beyond the configuration that produced them, which is what allows the optimizers to trust the corrected prediction on new designs. The terms were identified on the pinned knee, a single-revolute joint, with $\diameter$\qty{10}{\mm} BPAs, and they carry unchanged to a different knee type, to larger actuators, and into the reinforced hardware: on the biomimetic four-bar knee the shared-pair model cuts the extensor FVU from 4.49 to 0.67; with the $\diameter$\qty{20}{\mm} actuators and the high-strength high-temperature-fiberglass-reinforced parts of the redesigned system, the carried-over flexor terms hold FVU at 0.0102 on the \qty{10}{\mm} biomimetic flexor test and 0.0499 on the \qty{20}{\mm} test; and the redesigned routes, dimensioned end to end on the corrected prediction, deliver measured torque that meets or exceeds the human-reference magnitudes over the tested range (Section~\ref{sec:redesign_physical}). One stiffness pair, identified once, spans the joint types, actuator sizes, and part materials of the whole program.

The final mesh optimizers close the loop between the two families of Section~\ref{sec:mesh_opt}, and their cost functions record the evolution explicitly. The original mesh optimization scored rigid-body torque surplus with the reward of Equation~\eqref{eq:mesh_reward}. The route redesign replaced the reward with the penalized requirement of Equations~\eqref{eq:opt_cost_flx} and~\eqref{eq:opt_cost_ext}, shortfall against a required envelope, evaluated through the stiffness-aware force balance. The extensor's final form, the cam-profile redesign, adds three terms to the family objective,
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{cam}} = J_{\mathrm{ext}}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{full}}\right) + J_{\mathrm{arm}} + J_{\mathrm{armStep}} + J_{\mathrm{reg}},
\qquad
J_{\mathrm{arm}} = -10^{2}\,\min\!\left(1,\; \min_{j} m^{\mathrm{req}}_{j}\right),
\label{eq:opt_cost_cam}
\end{equation}
where $m^{\mathrm{req}}_{j} = (\abs{M^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}} - (1+m_{r})\abs{M^{\mathrm{H}}_{j}})/\tau$ is the margin against the required envelope of Equation~\eqref{eq:mesh_penalty_def}, and the moment-arm step and cam regularizer carry the weights of Table~\ref{tab:mesh_penalties}. The reward becomes a capped payment for margin beyond the requirement, so the solver keeps buying moment arm after the envelope is met, and the step term holds the release schedule smooth while it does.

The initial route searches bounded the BPA origins and insertions near the corresponding human muscle attachments to preserve biomimetic routing. Those anatomical bounds had to be relaxed where the available BPA contraction could not cover the knee range of motion. The resulting routes then required a second tradeoff: reducing the actuator forces carried by the attachment hardware after parts failed during testing. The cam redesign changes the moment arm and contact geometry to retain the torque requirement at lower actuator force.

The final flexor optimizer applies the wrapping-loss term $\chi_{3}$, identified on the extensor, because the flexor route can also bring the BPA into contact with the tibia and the femoral condyles at high flexion, and an actuator that a possible contact could bend must be scored with the length that bending removes. Its search box was deliberately expanded as an acknowledged tradeoff with biomimicry: the muscle origin was freed to move more than \qty{100}{\mm} proximal of the femoral shaft, far from the human biceps femoris origin, purely for reserve contraction, the extra actuator length, and therefore force and torque, available at high flexion where the single-actuator tests fell short. Two \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs are carried, both to reach the required torque and to limit the off-axis torque that the single-mirrored-actuator arrangement was found to load parts past their breaking point. A mirrored two-actuator solution, the two routes exact reflections of one another about the mid-sagittal plane, was found and rejected: it is not bio-realistic if a pelvis is ever introduced, since the medial origin would sit where pelvic structure belongs. The adopted solution is a staggered-offset pair, with a separately solved route for each actuator; the second insertion mirrors the first across the sagittal plane, while its origin preserves the first route's lateral endpoint offset, so both origins sit off the same side of the knee and the two routes do not cross. Each route is given its own wrapping calculation and its own collision checks.

The final extensor optimizer faces a requirement of a different order. The extensor target is set by vastus medialis, the quadriceps muscle with the smallest maximum isometric force in the reference set, and even that weakest-quad envelope demands roughly twice the peak torque of the flexor requirement, \qty{50.32}{\newton\meter} against \qty{23.16}{\newton\meter}. The two \qty{20}{\mm} extensor BPAs nominally share one path, a single route whose force is scaled by the actuator count, because the extensor route wraps over the front of the joint through as many as seven sequential contacts and duplicating that chain would double the interference problem. That shared-path economy has a programming cost: the large solution space of the wrapped route makes the contact and release angles, the knee angles at which each wrap contact activates and releases, difficult to schedule by inspection, and the calculation that produces them is formalized in Appendix~\ref{app:contact_release}. The routing was additionally modified to reduce the force the actuators exert, because the direct route's higher actuator forces broke the printed and machined parts during testing; reducing moment-arm demand and contact severity at lower force is what led into the cam algorithm of Equation~\eqref{eq:opt_cost_cam}.

The route redesign built on these values addresses the single-muscle shortfall at high flexion angles documented in Section~\ref{sec:results_torque}: each configuration now carries two \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs per route. The flexor solution meets the flexor torque target with a minimum margin of $+5.01$\,\% at $-107^{\circ}$, with a separately solved route for each actuator. The second insertion mirrors the first across the sagittal plane, while its origin preserves the first route's lateral endpoint offset; its wrapping and clearance are evaluated independently. The direct extensor route meets or exceeds its human-scaled target with the required 5\,\% overshoot as the thinnest margin at every angle, $+5.02$\,\% at worst, while respecting the wrap-release schedule and contraction limits of the route-elimination rule (Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry}); it was later superseded by the cam redesign of Section~\ref{sec:route_redesign_results}. These are mechanical design predictions. Their complete incorporation into the converted neuromechanical models remains future integration work.

\subsubsection{Redesigned-Route Results on the Gait2392 Robot-Body Model}\label{sec:route_redesign_results}

This subsection records the quantitative outcomes of the redesigned routes on the Gait2392-derived robot-body model, whose muscle force and torque targets define the redesign objectives. All three result families were produced by the surrogate-plus-pattern-search pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:mesh_opt} under the adopted correction terms (Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry}, Table~\ref{tab:app_xi_values}) with one shared actuator plan: two \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs per route, a \qty{620}{\kPa} pressure ceiling, and a maximum usable contraction of 0.255 in the force model. Each family minimizes its own objective function, a unitless weighted sum of normalized penalty terms in which zero corresponds to no violated penalty, led by the worst-angle and mean-square torque shortfall against the required 5\,\%-overshoot human envelope, followed by overshoot, rest-length, strain, contraction, and mild design-change penalties. The three functions differ in penalty families and normalizations (the flexor and pulley objectives add off-axis-excess, wrap-release, and second-BPA anchor terms, and the pulley objective evaluates its torque requirement on the insertion-side torque after the tackle's force loss), so their numerical values are not commensurable with one another. The human-scaled requirements are the OpenSim isometric torque profiles of the reference human muscles over the same design-grid angles, the vastus medialis for the extensor and the biceps femoris short head for the flexor.

The direct extensor route exceeds the human envelope in magnitude at every angle, with zero shortfall points: across the design range the predicted two-BPA extensor torque spans \qty{35.45}{\newton\cdot\meter} to \qty{109.4}{\newton\cdot\meter} against the human-scaled requirement of \qty{20.25}{\newton\cdot\meter} to \qty{50.32}{\newton\cdot\meter}, and the required 5\,\% overshoot is consumed as the thinnest margin exactly as designed, at a minimum margin of $+5.02$\,\% at the worst-case angle of $-20.2^{\circ}$.

Allowing the tibial-side route points to trace a cam profile produced the current extensor design (Figure~\ref{fig:ExtensorRoute_redesigned}). The final cam geometry includes the optimized route and the verified adjustment of the distal route point and tendon length. Its minimum torque margin is $+30.83\,\%$ with respect to the required envelope, the human target plus the 5\,\% overshoot, and $+39.77\,\%$ with respect to the plain human target. The pressure required to meet the envelope spans \SIrange[range-phrase=--]{269}{507}{\kPa} across the range of motion, comfortably above the \qty{150}{\kPa} regulated floor of the VTUG valve manifold that supplies the hardware. The geometry confirms that the headroom is not bought with routing risk: the tibial wrap follows a bend radius of \qty{96.9}{\mm}, the guiding route point clears the femoral-condyle envelope by \qty{43.1}{\mm} at \ang{10} of knee extension, and the moment arm spans \SIrange[range-phrase=--]{59.4}{102.8}{\mm} (mean \qty{72.4}{\mm}) over the range of motion.

The flexor route, built from two separately evaluated BPA paths, meets the flexor torque target with a minimum margin fraction of 0.0501 against the required 0.05. Its predicted knee-axis torque spans \qty{-81.23}{\newton\cdot\meter} to \qty{-3.864}{\newton\cdot\meter} against the human-scaled requirement of \qty{-23.16}{\newton\cdot\meter} to \qty{-2.461}{\newton\cdot\meter} (peak magnitude \qty{23.16}{\newton\cdot\meter}), again exceeding the human envelope at every angle. The flexor redesign applies the adopted correction terms of Table~\ref{tab:app_xi_values}, including the wrapping factor identified on the extensor configuration.

The Mono/Bi-pulley algorithms extend the route search space by allowing the artificial tendon to be reeved over pulleys before insertion. On the knee-flexor route, the pulley variant converged to a collision-feasible layout with zero bound violations and a peak predicted flexion torque of \qty{85.56}{\newton\cdot\meter} on the insertion-side measure, the torque applied at the insertion after the tackle's force loss; Section~\ref{sec:disc_pulley} quantifies what the redirect adds on the knee-axis measure and the off-axis torque it bounds.

\subsection{Mono- and Bi-Pulley Tendon Routing}\label{sec:disc_pulley}

Every optimizer of the preceding sections scores its routes with the MonoPamDataExplicit-type calculation: a fixed chain of via points carrying one segment across from the parent body into the child, its contacts wrapped and released by the elimination rule, its length and moment arm read from that chain. The Mono- and Bi-pulley algorithms are the generalization of exactly that calculation. The artificial tendon is reeved over pulleys before insertion, forming a reverse pulley, a block-and-tackle transmission: with $G$ tackle line parts supporting the moving block, the tendon travel at the insertion is multiplied by $G$ while the force delivered there is divided by $G$, and at $G = 1$ the reeved route reduces to the straight-tendon route of the direct redesign. The tendon exit is either body-fixed on the proximal body so that its direction rotates with the joint, or anchored through a housing to the distal body, and the tackle's increased internal force and the pulley-block bracket deflections enter the same stiffness-aware force balance as the direct route's brackets. Read this way the pulley variants are not a new actuator concept but the general routing case of the mesh-optimization machinery, of which every direct route is the $G = 1$ special case.

At unity transmission, the knee-flexor pulley route increased peak predicted knee-axis torque from \qty{81.23}{\newton\meter} to \qty{85.74}{\newton\meter}, while limiting off-axis torque to \qty{0.803}{\newton\meter}. This comparison measures the effect of the two optimized routing configurations; their different objective functions do not isolate the pulley alone. The pulley serves as a redirect in this configuration rather than a force multiplier. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_routing_extensions} records the optimizer diagnostics and the broader route searches.

A subsequent transmission study re-solved actuator sizes for each tackle configuration at five anatomical muscle sites. A two-BPA pair through a two-part tackle preserves net actuator force while doubling insertion travel; increasing travel can extend the angle range over which a route meets its torque requirement. The corresponding torque and margin surfaces are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:PulleySurfaces} and~\ref{fig:PulleyMargins_resolved}. These calculations use the moving-force-point routing, with the adjacent joint fixed for the biarticular evaluation planes; they do not establish simultaneous performance across both joints. Physical verification of the pulley configurations remains future work.

\subsection{Actuator-Count and Transmission Study for Vastus Medialis}\label{sec:min_bpa}

The vastus medialis study evaluates one to three parallel \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs and one to three tackle line parts at \qty{620}{\kPa}, with actuator and tendon lengths re-solved for each combination. The attachment geometry is fixed by the study's routing family. Both OpenSim and cable-path moment arms are tested, with and without the compliance correction. In the corrected mode the calculation uses the pulley model directly; this model does not include the wrapping-loss term $\chi_3$, so it is not the same prediction as the cam redesign.

No tested configuration meets the target at all five graded angles. The best coverage is four of five, obtained with three BPAs through a two-part tackle using the compliance correction and the cable-path moment arm. Its four passing-angle margins are $+56.3$, $+40.1$, $+35.4$, and $+1.0$~\unit{\newton\meter}. It fails at \ang{10}, where this routing family approaches zero torque. The cable path uses a ring point \qty{0.75}{\mm} off the bone surface. These results identify a limitation of the tested routing family; they do not establish a minimum actuator count capable of meeting the complete envelope. The separate cam redesign meets its own target over its design grid with two BPAs (Section~\ref{sec:route_redesign_results}).

\subsection{Whole-Leg Transmission Capability}\label{sec:masters_updated}

The configuration survey grades each actuator against the same-named single muscle of the Gait2392 model \citep{delp_opensim_2007,seth_opensim_2018} on a $5\times5$ grid of two joint coordinates. The hip adduction domain is clipped by its flexion-dependent envelope. A cell meets the requirement when the BPA is taut and its torque has the human torque's sign and at least its magnitude. The coverage metric is the fraction of included grid cells meeting that condition; the aggregate is the unweighted mean of the per-actuator fractions, not a continuous-area integral or a whole-leg torque sum.

The BPA force model is evaluated at \qty{620}{\kPa} \citep{bolen_2026}. The comparison includes all 27 actuator sites (Table~\ref{tab:survey_dofs_sizes}). The baseline uses the master's-era attachment routing at unity transmission; its eight nominal \qty{40}{\mm} sites are evaluated with four parallel \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs, while the other sites use the survey's base configuration. The updated side selects the saved transmission-ladder winner at each site, with actuator length and tendon length re-solved per configuration. It retains the same underlying attachment routing and the original diameter specification, including \qty{40}{\mm} actuators at those eight sites. The comparison therefore combines transmission, actuator sizing, and the baseline diameter substitution; it does not isolate an effect of attachment placement or compare two all-\qty{20}{\mm} assemblies.

The mean grid coverage increases from \qty{37.28}{\percent} to \qty{65.19}{\percent}, improving at 23 sites and tying at four . A separate sign-aware torque-ratio score averages $\min(M_R/M_H,1)$ over the graded cells, assigning zero to inactive cells. Its mean falls from $-1.93$ to $-3.63$. Negative ratios penalize torque in the wrong direction, so greater coverage can coexist with a worse mean ratio where severe wrong-sign predictions remain. These metrics support different conclusions and should be considered together.

For comparison, the average of the two axis-wise mean coverages is \qty{38.39}{\percent} for the baseline and \qty{66.17}{\percent} for the updated configurations. On a complete rectangular grid, averaging row and column coverages equals the overall cell fraction exactly; it cannot reduce the influence of edge poses there. The two summaries differ only on the four hip-envelope domains with excluded cells. The two metrics and the torque-ratio score are reported together, so their different weightings remain visible.

\subsection{Physical Tests of the Redesigned Routes}\label{sec:redesign_physical}

The redesigned cam-extensor route was tested at nine isometric holds, and the two-BPA flexor route at fifteen holds. Both test sets span knee angles from \ang{-119} to \ang{5}. The test-specific analysis scripts transform the measured load-cell wrench to the moving knee center and evaluate model predictions at each measured angle and pressure. Figures~\ref{fig:extensor_physical_final} and~\ref{fig:flexor_physical_final} compare these reductions with the human-reference torques and the optimized \qty{620}{\kPa} curves.

The measured torque magnitudes reach or exceed the human-reference magnitudes over most of the tested range. Agreement with the pressure-specific prediction is uneven, particularly for the flexor at intermediate angles. The tests therefore support the redesigned routes' torque capacity while identifying prediction errors that remain to be explained. Since pressure varied between holds, the measured points do not constitute a full-pressure envelope at every angle.
