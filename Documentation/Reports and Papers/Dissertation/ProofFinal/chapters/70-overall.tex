\section{Completed Foundation Studies}\label{sec:foundation}

Four studies completed during this dissertation establish the foundation for the experimental chapters and for the controller program defined in the remainder of this chapter. First, the placement optimization of Morrow, Bolen, and Hunt found artificial muscle attachment locations that reproduce human torque profiles and ranges of motion about the trunk and leg joints \citep{morrow_optimization_2020}; it converted the earlier finding that transplanted human attachment locations do not transfer directly to a BPA-actuated robot \citep{bolen_bipedal_2019, bolen_determination_2019} into a usable design tool. The route redesigns of Section~\ref{sec:ongoing} rerun that tool under the corrected torque model. Second, the Sensory Afferent Database (Chapter~\ref{ch:database}) organized sensory-afferent literature into a structured, model-oriented resource \citep{bolen_sensory_2023}, and a follow-up study evaluated AI-assisted literature triage for expanding it \citep{bolen_using_2024}; those data inform the sensory channels of the neural controller. The database has since been audited and expanded into a curated laboratory tool: of the 943 papers in the audited corpus, 833 carry structured curation notes, 95 carry an afferent classification, and 363 carry notes translating each pathway toward robot or simulation use, packaged with the corpus citation graph (18 topic clusters, 11,130 directed citation pairs) as a self-contained explorer for lab use. Third, an inverted-pendulum balance platform with BPA-driven plantarflexion and dorsiflexion demonstrated pseudo-vestibular feedback using inertial measurement units \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. Fourth, companion work by Elzein, Lutz, Bolen, and Hunt produced a state-space, optimization-based model of pressure and force dynamics in pulse-modulated BPAs \citep{elzein_pressure_2025}, supplying dynamic actuator behavior beyond the static force characterization reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}.

\section{Synthesis: From Actuator Characterization to Neuromechanical Control}\label{sec:synthesis}

Taken together, the chapters of this dissertation form a coherent design chain for biomimetic legged robots, in which each stage feeds the next. At the actuator level, the maximum isometric force of Festo BPAs was shown to depend strongly on resting length, correcting a systematic over-prediction by the manufacturer's tool at short resting lengths, and a nondimensional force surface was derived that predicts isometric force from relative strain and relative pressure with only three diameter-dependent coefficients (Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}). At the joint level, the improved actuator model was combined with a compliance-corrected musculotendon kinematics model to predict isometric knee torque across the full range of motion for pinned and biomimetic knee joints, and the biomimetic knee driven by optimized BPAs was shown to meet or exceed human isometric torque over most of the range of motion (Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}). The remaining links connect the mechanical system to its controller: the balance-platform studies demonstrated in hardware that a BPA-actuated limb can hold itself upright under pseudo-vestibular feedback, the Sensory Afferent Database supplies the afferent organization that the controller's sensory channels mirror (Chapter~\ref{ch:database}), and the simulation framework joins the converted robot model to a synthetic nervous system so that closed-loop experiments can run before hardware exists (Chapter~\ref{ch:sns}).

The chain was assembled deliberately. The force model supplies the actuator terms for the torque model; the torque model supplies the biomechanical model for designing joint controllers; and the validated biomimetic knee demonstrates that the actuator architecture can reproduce human joint-level force capabilities. The neural layer is no longer only a proposal: the OpenSim-to-MuJoCo conversion, BPA coupling interface, SNS-Toolbox spinal architecture, sensory channels, and staged validation workflow described in Sections~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}--\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results} provide a working framework for neuromechanical experiments. The AARL's prior AnimatLab walker (Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker}) explains why that framework emphasizes systematic verification: its hand-tuned two-layer CPG produced gait-like suspended stepping, but its afferent feedback did not generalize to weight-bearing motion.

The cross-platform verification campaign (Sections~\ref{sec:crossengine_methods}--\ref{sec:bpa_walker}) adds a design constraint that runs through this chain: a synthetic nervous system is a mathematical object, and it must survive translation between the tools in which it is built, verified, and deployed. Reproducing the contact-driven rhythm core with exact period agreement and trace correlations above 0.9999 between independent NumPy and Simulink implementations, with the archived AnimatLab models reproducing their published rhythms headless in the same protocol, establishes that the SNS formalism, rather than any particular simulator, carries the dynamics. The persistent-sodium half-center with a fixed inactivation time constant, the graded synapse, and contact-driven rhythm are therefore portable primitives: they can be tuned in whichever engine is fastest and trusted in whichever engine is most physical. The campaign's negative results are equally structural. The feedforward CPG of the prior walker and the contact-driven core of the W2L architecture sit at deliberate, opposite points of the sensory-dependence spectrum. The former oscillates without feedback but could not bear weight; the latter will not oscillate at all without phasic heel feedback, a load dependence that echoes the decerebrate-cat locomotion of Section~\ref{sec:bg_neural}. The missing piece for unsupported walking is balance, a network layer rather than an engine feature, and the BPA-actuated walker's peak forces at 60\% of the pressure ceiling indicate that, once that layer exists, the actuation margin is already there.

With the mechanical characterization and the simulation framework in place, the dissertation's remaining task is the controller: a synthetic nervous system that schedules these actuators the way the nervous system schedules muscles, and, eventually, the physical robot that the controller runs on. The work that remains is described at the end of this section, in terms of what the completed framework already lets other researchers do: every experiment that follows draws directly on the validated actuator model, the corrected torque model, the afferent database, and the simulation framework as delivered. The nearest experiments bring the assembled simulation pipeline and its two-layer CPG from assembly to closed-loop tuning (Section~\ref{sec:fw_pipeline}); the later ones extend the sensorium with vestibular, ocular, and cerebellar contributions, realize the controller on treadmill hardware with embedded real-time neural control, and push beyond steady walking toward dynamic whole-body maneuvers (Section~\ref{sec:fw_senses}). Closed-loop quantification, ground-robustness comparison, and hardware transfer each stand as independent research programs enabled by the completed framework. Some of this work is planned for the remainder of the dissertation project; the later stages extend naturally into postdoctoral research.

\subsection{Future Work: Closing the Loop in Simulation}\label{sec:fw_pipeline}\label{sec:fw_cpg}

The work nearest to hand is the closed loop itself: converting the validated biomechanical design into a physics simulation that a neural controller drives, and then tuning and validating that controller. Much of the pipeline is already in place (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}): the OpenSim-to-MuJoCo conversion is complete and audited, the SNS-Toolbox coupling runs at a \qty{0.1}{\milli\second} neural step with force feedback, and the full two-layer spinal network has been assembled with its rhythm generator verified in isolation. The remaining blocker is non-finite leg-joint states in full-body co-simulation, with the foot-contact meshes the primary suspect; once it is resolved, the open work shifts from assembly to tuning and validation, and that shift is where a researcher adopting the framework enters.

The musculoskeletal half of the pipeline starts from the author's modified OpenSim model: the Gait2392-derived robot variant in which the actuator set has been reduced and adapted to the BPA hardware and the muscle origin, insertion, and wrapping points have been updated to the optimized robot routing \citep{bolen_determination_2019, morrow_optimization_2020}. The model is kept in OpenSim 4.x format and converted to MuJoCo \citep{todorov_mujoco_2012} using MyoConverter \citep{caggiano_myosuite_2022, wang_myosim_2022}, which converts OpenSim musculoskeletal models to MuJoCo models with optimized muscle kinematics and kinetics; the completed conversion and its audit are documented in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}. The conversion is checked by inspecting the generated model, verifying joint naming and ordering for later sensor and actuator mapping, confirming that the expected number of muscle-tendon actuators exists with plausible attachment geometry, and fixing any conversion gaps by revising the OpenSim model or accepting manual edits in the MuJoCo model. In the converted model, actuation is represented by muscle-like actuators that carry an internal activation state, so that SNS outputs can drive them through activation dynamics; at each timestep, MuJoCo updates the kinematics and the muscle variables (e.g., muscle-tendon lengths and velocities) needed to compute proprioceptive signals.

The neural half is implemented with SNS-Toolbox \citep{nourse_sns_2023}, which supports large networks of spiking and non-spiking neurons and couples directly to MuJoCo. At each timestep, SNS receives Ia, Ib, and II proprioceptive channels plus binary heel and toe contact signals from the MuJoCo model, processes them through the neural core described below, and returns per-muscle activation commands that are fed into the MuJoCo muscle activation states. The coupling itself is in place; its staged validation remains, and the staging is what a researcher taking up the framework executes first. Before any gains are tuned against human gait, the mechanics-only loop will be verified with open-loop activation patterns, confirming the sign and magnitude behavior of torques and forces. The network will then be exercised with a minimal sensor set, contact signals plus a single proprioceptive channel, and only later with the full Ia/Ib/II set. Once stable, the activation strengths and neuron-to-muscle mapping parameters will be tuned across a few walking speeds. A Simscape-based mechanical model could substitute for MuJoCo for validation of specific mechanical subsystems, but Simscape does not natively host the spiking and non-spiking neuron models of an SNS, so MuJoCo remains the primary platform. The AARL's quadruped has already traveled part of this road: a MuJoCo model of the BPA quadruped was coupled to SNS-Toolbox, produced closed-loop coordinated stepping, and was transferred to hardware with potentiometer and pressure feedback on a treadmill \citep{lutz_mechanical_2025}; the present pipeline extends that approach from the quadruped to a leg built on human anatomy. Comparable neuromechanical simulation frameworks, such as FARMS, are emerging in parallel \citep{arreguit_farms_2023}.

The requirements for the controller come from the prior AnimatLab walker (Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker}), which demonstrated both the promise and the limitation of this architecture; its outcome belongs on record because it defines those requirements. With the rhythm-generator, pattern-formation, and sensorimotor layers oscillating in phase, the suspended model produced alternating stepping-like motion at approximately \qty{4}{\Hz}, faster than the roughly \qty{1}{\Hz} of typical human gait, but adjustable through the sodium-channel activation parameters and, once ground contact exists, subject to further slowing from ground-reaction forces and load-dependent feedback. Preliminary results from that model were presented at the 2021 NIH BRAIN Initiative Investigators Meeting \citep{bolenTorqueValuesArtificialKnee2021June15-17}.

When the earlier demonstrated configuration was lowered onto a simulated ground plane, the same patterns could not support the body or generate forward motion: afferent pathways had been wired but not tuned, and functional ground-contact gating of stance and swing had not been implemented. The current instrumented phase-1 model records left and right foot and toe contact channels, but their use for closed-loop phase control has not yet been validated. In the suspended walker, the CPG acts as a feedforward controller: rhythm generation alone, without functional ground-contact feedback, produced gait-like stepping, exactly as the deafferented-CPG physiology of Section~\ref{sec:bg_neural} predicts. What feedforward stepping cannot do is support the body against the ground, so the critical path from stepping-like motion to weight-bearing walking is the proprioceptive and contact feedback described below, and the verification tests that tune them. That premise is well supported: biomechanics and sensory feedback have been shown to regularize the behavior of different locomotor CPG architectures \citep{deng_biomechanical_2022}, so the present work contributes an application of this principle to the suspended walker. The AnimatLab walker model, a direct descendant of that earlier work, continues to serve as the prototyping environment for the network while the MuJoCo pipeline is brought up.

The neural core follows the two-level organization of the mammalian locomotor CPG established by the Rybak group \citep{rybak_modelling_2006, mccrea_organization_2008, rybak_organization_2015}: each leg has a rhythm generator (RG) circuit, which produces the basic locomotor rhythm and controls its period, and a pattern formation (PF) circuit, which shapes the rhythm into phase-appropriate activation of the flexor and extensor motoneuron populations. A left--right rhythm generator circuit coordinates the two legs through commissural inhibition and excitation, following models of left--right coordination \citep{molkov_mechanisms_2015, danner_central_2016}. The per-leg PF output drives the motoneuron pools, here the BPA activation commands for the hip, knee, and ankle muscle groups of each leg.

Three classes of proprioceptive feedback, together with discrete contact feedback, will close the loop between the MuJoCo model and the CPG, mirroring the biological afferents organized in the Sensory Afferent Database \citep{bolen_sensory_2023}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ia afferents} (muscle spindle primary endings) signal muscle length and velocity, providing stretch reflex pathways and phase-dependent modulation of the PF network.
    \item \textbf{Ib afferents} (Golgi tendon organs) signal muscle force, providing load-dependent regulation of stance-phase extensor activity.
    \item \textbf{II afferents} (muscle spindle secondary endings) signal length, contributing to position sensing and interjoint coordination.
    \item \textbf{Contact receptors} on the heel and toe provide discrete stance and swing boundary signals, driving phase transitions and weight-support switching.
\end{itemize}

Before attempting locomotion, the network will be verified with simple simulation tests in which a tonic stimulus is applied to the muscle motoneurons, to the pattern-formation layer, and to the rhythm-generator layer, and the resulting outputs are compared with the corresponding experimental literature. The first of these tests is complete at the rhythm-generator level: under tonic drive, the isolated SNS-Toolbox rhythm generators of the assembled spinal network oscillate with a period of approximately \qty{0.9}{\second} with the two legs in antiphase (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}); in the AnimatLab instance of the network, conductance tuning toward robust rhythm-generator oscillation is still under way. The closest biological analogue is the tonic-stimulation air-stepping protocol of \citet{selionov_tonic_2009}, in which tonic central and sensory stimuli facilitate involuntary stepping in humans; analogous deletion and stimulation phenomena are reproduced by the two-level CPG model itself \citep{rybak_modelling_2006} and by neuromechanical implementations \citep{markin_afferent_2010, shevtsova_modeling_2016}. Comparing the simulated tonic-stimulation responses with these studies will validate the sign, gain, and phase structure of the implemented circuits before any walking-speed tuning is attempted. These tests are a natural entry point for a researcher adopting the framework: they exercise the network in isolation from the body model, and they map directly onto the experimental protocols from which the circuits are drawn.

\subsection{Future Work: Sensorium, Physical Realization, and Whole-Body Maneuvers}\label{sec:fw_senses}\label{sec:fw_hardware}\label{sec:fw_kickflip}

The longer arc of the program extends past closed-loop walking in three directions: the sensorium, the physical robot, and, ultimately, dynamic whole-body maneuvers. Once stepping is stable, three supraspinal contributions will be added. First, vestibular feedback will be simulated with IMU-like sensing of body orientation and angular velocity, extending the AARL's balance-platform work, in which an inverted pendulum with pseudo-vestibular IMU feedback maintained balance on an oscillating platform \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024} and analytic neural networks reproduced human balance dynamics \citep{hilts_dynamic_2019}. Second, ocular feedback will be modeled to investigate how visual stabilization cues can help reset IMU drift, a well-known practical problem for inertial sensing on robots, by treating visual measurements of the horizon and surroundings as a low-frequency correction to the vestibular estimate. Third, a cerebellar layer will be explored as an adaptive gateway between higher-level locomotion commands and the CPG: a structure that can gate between different gaits or movement patterns (e.g., walking, running, and turning) and tune the phase relationships among them, in line with the cerebellum's established role in adaptation and coordination. Multilevel spinal-plus-cerebellar synthetic nervous systems have already been demonstrated for insect-like legs \citep{grummer_multilevel_2024}, and hierarchical cortico-cerebellar-spinal models have been proposed for adaptive step-length control in bipedal locomotion \citep{chen_corticocerebellar_2025}. The proprioceptive--vestibular interaction literature \citep{akay_relative_2021} will guide how these signals are weighted.

The final stage returns to hardware. The robot will walk on a treadmill with real-time feedback and control implemented on an embedded computing stack: Teensy microcontrollers for the low-latency per-joint loops (valve control, pressure sensing, and contact sensing) and an NVIDIA Jetson-class companion computer for the SNS network evaluation and activation computation. A serial connection to a host computer will be used only for data logging and high-level commands (e.g., run, walk, or stimulate a named neural pathway), keeping the control loop fully embedded. Walking control of a physical musculoskeletal biped with McKibben muscles has recently been demonstrated with spring-loaded inverted pendulum controllers \citep{li_controller_2024}, supporting the feasibility of embedded control on anthropomorphic pneumatic legs. An actuation-level design lever also remains open: the BPA's limited contraction can force long musculotendon routings, a burden visible in the extensor redesign bounds of Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry} and encountered in the author's master's routing work \citep{bolen_determination_2019}. A block-and-tackle ``reverse pulley'' arrangement, demonstrated on the AARL's self-balancing inverted-pendulum platform, addresses this by trading transmitted force for travel: paired BPAs pulled through compound pulleys on a tendon coupled to the limb, increasing range of motion at the cost of reduced transmitted force and increased reaction force on the structure \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. Because joint torque is computed in the body frame, configurations that turn that reaction force to advantage may exist, making the arrangement a candidate for joints whose optimized routes demand impractically long actuators. This division of labor mirrors the SNS-Toolbox backend philosophy, which targets embedded computing platforms for real-time neural simulation \citep{nourse_sns_2023}.

Because the static force and torque models of Chapters~\ref{ch:muscles} and~\ref{ch:torque} are computationally inexpensive lookup-table candidates, they will serve as the feedforward model inside the real-time controller, while the dynamic pressure--force behavior under pulse-modulated control will draw on the state-space model of \citet{elzein_pressure_2025}. Embedded execution of the complete CPG-plus-reflex network on this stack is the intended demonstration of the dissertation project: a robot that walks with human-like biomechanics under a biologically grounded neural controller, and whose nervous system, unlike a living animal's, can be instrumented, stimulated, and lesioned at will.

Beyond steady walking, the long-term goal of this line of research is dynamic, whole-body maneuvering: transitions between gaits, reactive balancing, and ultimately ballistic maneuvers that require coordinating the full actuator set through brief, precisely timed activation bursts. The author's aspiration for this program is a robot that can perform a kickflip, a maneuver that, like its skateboard namesake, demands explosive coordinated extension, precise timing, aerial body orientation, and controlled landing. It is exactly the kind of behavior that exercises every layer of the architecture described above, from contact receptors and spinal pattern generation to vestibular orientation sensing and cerebellar coordination, and it would demonstrate that biomimetic actuation and neuromechanical control scale from steady locomotion to dynamic whole-body movement.
