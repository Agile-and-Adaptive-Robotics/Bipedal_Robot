This chapter considers how the actuator models, torque corrections, sensory-afferent resource, and simulation tools support biomimetic robot design, and identifies the experiments and integration work on which researchers can now build.

\section{The Work as a Whole}\label{sec:foundation}

The outcome that organizes this discussion is that improved first-pass design of a BPA-actuated biomimetic leg is now achievable: a designer can predict actuator force, joint torque, and range of motion accurately enough to commit to an actuator set, a resting length, and a route before the hardware exists (Figure~\ref{fig:design_workflow}). At the center of that outcome are the identified design factors that remove the rigid-body assumptions from the torque model (Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}): a constant length offset $\chi_{0}$, effective stiffnesses $\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$ that lump the series compliance of the attachment brackets, fixtures, and artificial tendons into the bracket frame's principal directions, and a wrapping-loss factor $\chi_{3}$ that removes usable actuator length as an extensor bends over the joint. The terms were identified on the pinned knee. Applied to the biomimetic knee, they reduce prediction error, although the remaining error depends on the configuration. The biomimetic extensor's fraction of variance unexplained falls from 4.49 to 0.67 with the shared terms (Section~\ref{sec:ongoing}). Whether the set is complete is itself now an experimental question: a fourth term $\chi_{4}$, a stiffness acting along the force path independently of force orientation and presently absorbed by the stiffness pair ($\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$), is arguably the next candidate, and the identification machinery delivered here is the instrument for settling it.

Improved BPA characterization is the first enabler (Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}). The maximum isometric force of Festo BPAs was shown to depend strongly on resting length, a previously unreported characteristic that corrects the manufacturer's tool at exactly the short lengths a leg design requires, and a nondimensional force surface predicts isometric force from relative strain and relative pressure with only three diameter-dependent coefficients. Because resting length is a primary degree of freedom in actuator selection, this model is what makes that selection rational. The quasi-static surface is complemented at the dynamic end by the state-space, optimization-based model of pressure and force dynamics in pulse-modulated BPAs \citep{elzein_pressure_2025}, which supplies the actuator behavior that the isometric maps cannot.

Attachment and routing optimization is the second enabler. Morrow, Bolen, and Hunt developed a mesh search for three lumbosacral muscle replacements, balancing torque matching against actuator length, attachment displacement, and diameter \citep{morrow_optimization_2020}. This provided a systematic response to the finding that human attachment locations do not transfer directly to a BPA-actuated robot \citep{bolen_bipedal_2019, bolen_determination_2019}. The later knee-route optimization uses the corrected torque model and a continuous search over attachment, routing, and actuator variables, with explicit mechanical constraints (Section~\ref{sec:ongoing}). In the route-design predictions, with two \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs per route and routes chosen with the actuators' bending in mind, the redesigned flexor and extensor each meet or exceed the magnitude of their human-scaled torque targets across the full knee range of motion, and the cam-profile extensor converts the direct route's thin margin into genuine design headroom (Section~\ref{sec:route_redesign_results}). Stated as a design program, the generalization is one or more BPAs replacing one or more human muscles across one or more joints, serving the two degrees of freedom that matter most at each joint, with routes grown outward from positions close to the bone and held to the spatial constraints of the candidate actuators and of the BPAs already mounted; the broader route-search diagnostics are collected in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_routing_extensions}. Where the attachments are fixed and only the route and the tendon length can change, the cam-shape solver applies: the tibial-side route points trace a designed cam profile, trading biomimicry for torque-profile optimization and reduced component forces.

The Sensory Afferent Database (SADb) is the third enabler (Chapter~\ref{ch:database}). The delivered application makes 1,393 literature and linked records searchable offline. The Papers category contains 943 entries; among those, 833 carry curation notes, 95 carry an afferent classification, and 363 carry robot-or-simulation translation notes. Its table, pivot, and citation-neighborhood views connect a broad search to the source observations and assumptions behind a proposed sensory pathway. Citation symbols encode bibliographic direction, rather than physiological excitation or inhibition. The contribution is both the curated evidence and a practical means of navigating it. Continued classification and source review remain necessary; the AI-assisted triage evaluation did not establish reliable automated categorization \citep{bolen_using_2024}.

The fourth contribution is a functioning neuromechanical experiment workflow (Chapter~\ref{ch:sns}). The AnimatLab and MATLAB integration attempts showed why a moving model, an imported mechanism, or an isolated neural rhythm was insufficient to establish the intended coupled behavior. The resulting Gait2392 and W2L implementations, SNS-Simscape blocks, MuJoCo--Simulink integration, and curriculum make those components and their interactions testable. Matched-input checks establish selected implementation agreement, reflex demonstrations show corrective feedback, and fixed-controller pathway removal exposes sensory dependence in supported stepping. The curriculum and replay procedures connect these demonstrations into a reusable method for constructing, tuning, and interrogating a model. Their limits, including retained mechanical support and incomplete transfer between models, also define the next experiments.

The coupled-model results must be interpreted under their stated support conditions. Supported stepping and forward progression establish functioning neural--mechanical loops, while removal of tonic drive tests a different dependence from removal of mechanical support. The balance-platform studies provide a separate hardware demonstration of a BPA-actuated limb under pseudo-vestibular inertial feedback \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. Together, the simulation tools and hardware precedents support further study of balance and weight bearing without treating those capabilities as already demonstrated by the stepping tests.

The proposed workflow connects these contributions through explicit checks (Figure~\ref{fig:design_workflow}). Force and route predictions must meet the human torque and motion targets before a candidate proceeds to closed-loop simulation and hardware. Failure at either check returns the design to actuator selection or route revision.

\section{Future Work}

The completed contributions support actuator selection, corrected route evaluation, source-based sensory-pathway selection, and controlled simulation experiments. The work below extends those capabilities in distinct directions: dynamic actuator characterization, routing and packaging, continued literature curation, weight-bearing control, and embedded hardware. These are further investigations built on the delivered tools, rather than prerequisites for using the results already established.

\subsection{Expanding the BPA Force Characterization}\label{sec:fw_bpa}

The isometric characterization of Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles} leaves one actuator curve unidentified and one regime unmeasured, and both are tractable next experiments. The unidentified curve is the maximum-contraction function: $\varepsilon_{620}$ could not be pinned to a predictive relationship with resting length, so each actuator's free contraction is recorded individually before use. Controlled tests designed for that relationship, across multiple product batches of the \qtylist{10;20;40}{\mm} diameters, would either produce general fit surfaces or establish batch variation as the binding uncertainty. The \qty{20}{\mm} actuators characterized here came from a single product batch, so batch-to-batch variation is unquantified and those fits in particular carry that limitation.

The unmeasured regime is everything off the isometric plateau. Biological muscle models separate active and passive force and include activation, contraction, and tendon dynamics \citep{thelen_adjustment_2003, millard_flexing_2013}. Appendix~\ref{app:muscle_calcs} presents these relations and explains their parameters. A corresponding BPA model would require separate characterization of active pressurized-braid force, passive resistance of the unpressurized actuator and sleeve, and contraction dynamics. Isokinetic and isotonic or even isobaric characterizations could then be assimilated into the same framework rather than fitted separately, with the pressure--force dynamics model of \citet{elzein_pressure_2025} supplying the actuator-side dynamics.

Tensile testing with the Onyx and aluminum end caps, under pressurized and unpressurized conditions, would test the proposed end-effect explanation for resting-length-dependent force. A complementary finite-element model could represent the rubber layers, crossed aramid braid, end constraints, and contact interfaces described in the Festo patent literature \citep{festo_2026}. Figure~\ref{fig:bpa_construction} shows the proposed geometry and boundary conditions. Comparing predicted deformation with those measurements would test the empirical force surfaces against a mechanical explanation.

In Figure~\ref{fig:bpa_construction}, longitudinal symmetry permits in-plane expansion. At the half-length boundary, axial displacement can be fixed or axial motion allowed under an optional load. Internal pressure loads the contact interfaces; cap details are schematic.

\subsection{Expanding Attachment and Routing Design}\label{sec:fw_routing}

Each routing tier of Section~\ref{sec:foundation} leaves a named next step. The knee's adopted flexor redesign evaluates two staggered paths separately. Physical verification of the two paths' clearances, load sharing, and off-axis loading remains necessary before extending that configuration to a complete leg. The bi-pulley sweep showed that reeved routes do not yet fit the anatomical corridors, where geometry rather than torque was the binding constraint, so clearance-relaxed attachment sites are where that freedom should pay. The whole-body pass needs its route-builder failure fixed and either larger actuators or relaxed corridors on the two routes that satisfy geometry but fall short of torque.

An artificial patella is a further routing option. Its position could be optimized to preserve extensor moment arm and shield the BPA from joint contact, while explicitly accounting for friction and contact transitions. The cam-profile result establishes that a moving routing surface can be optimized; it does not establish the performance of a patella-based design (Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}).

The isometric torque tests of Chapter~\ref{ch:torque} establish a static capacity envelope; they do not identify velocity dependence or damping. Isokinetic tests, torque compared across angles at controlled joint speeds, would show how contraction rate, pressure regulation, and the changing moment arm affect the torque available during movement; isotonic tests, the actuator shortening under a prescribed load, would assess shortening speed, achievable excursion, and load support. Loading--unloading sequences would quantify hysteresis, and pressure-step tests would characterize the pneumatic response. A torque-controlled motor and a less compliant loading connection would reduce winch slack and permit controlled-speed and controlled-load tests on the same rig, extending the static model toward movement and load-bearing control.

\subsection{Expanding the Sensory Afferent Database}\label{sec:fw_database}

A monthly literature-scraping extension is being implemented to identify candidate additions to the database. Its retrieval and classification must be evaluated against human decisions before it can support routine updates. Further curation can also target missing afferent classifications, pathway links, and preparation metadata in the existing records. The AI-assisted triage study establishes the relevant division of responsibility: automated tools draft and retrieve, while I retain the inclusion and interpretation decisions \citep{bolen_using_2024}.

\subsection{Closing the Loop in Simulation}\label{sec:fw_pipeline}\label{sec:fw_cpg}

The simulation laboratory of Chapter~\ref{ch:sns} is assembled, audited, and in active use. The converted models support individual checks of actuator geometry, force direction, and signal exchange, and the BPA force interface evaluates the isometric force model inside the loop; the implemented interfaces and their limits are described in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}. The 410-neuron SNS-Toolbox instantiation of the spinal network, with per-leg rhythm-generator half-centers, phase-shifted pattern-formation groups, motoneuron pools for every actuator, and Ia, Ib, and II afferent populations, couples to the converted model at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, and supported stepping configurations have been tuned against a walking reference and evaluated on the ground (Section~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}). A drive-weaning study has since produced a feedback-sustained bilateral configuration whose stepping persists with the externally supplied neural drive removed, still in place under the support rig, and the robot-side model progresses under its harness with reduced neural drive (Section~\ref{sec:sns_controller_comparison}). These evaluations identify the tasks researchers can now take: the balance and weight-bearing layers that unsupported, bilateral walking requires; transfer from a single tuning reference to the diversity of the locomotion reference library; and the systematic verification series that follows.

Further verification should apply specified stimuli to the rhythm-generator, pattern-formation, and motoneuron layers, while recording both neural timing and mechanical responses. The reduced-circuit tests in Section~\ref{sec:app_layer_stimulus} distinguish RG resetting from suppression of downstream motor output. Extending these tests to the full Gait2392 networks will require matched stimulus protocols and separate assessment of the RG clock and motor pattern. Physiological and modeling studies provide reference phenomena for these comparisons \citep{selionov_tonic_2009,rybak_modelling_2006,markin_afferent_2010,shevtsova_modeling_2016}.

The next experiments should stimulate, remove, or add specified pathways while retaining the other controller and mechanical settings. Existing removal tests establish a starting point (Section~\ref{sec:walker_variant_results}); adding a candidate sensory or commissural pathway would test whether it improves a defined response. Matched comparisons of spiking and non-spiking controllers should use the same tuning and evaluation conditions. The observed differences in their limb coordination motivate that comparison (Section~\ref{sec:disc_spiking}).

The existing integrations provide a starting point for extending the model set. A full-body OpenSim model with arms and trunk would require its own geometry and actuator checks after conversion. Its controller could then be assembled or inspected with the neural block tools and evaluated with the relevant simulation interface. The matched-circuit tests and pathway-intervention procedures provide a reusable verification method, but each new model and controller combination must establish its own behavior.

\subsection{Adding Sensory and Higher-Level Control}\label{sec:fw_senses}

Four planned extensions address sensory estimation, coordination, and action selection (Figure~\ref{fig:sensorium_hierarchy}). First, vestibular feedback will be simulated with IMU-like sensing of body orientation and angular velocity, extending the balance-platform work, in which an inverted pendulum with pseudo-vestibular IMU feedback maintained balance on an oscillating platform \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024} and analytic neural networks reproduced human balance dynamics \citep{hilts_dynamic_2019}. Second, ocular feedback will be modeled to investigate how visual stabilization cues can help reset IMU drift, a well-known practical problem for inertial sensing on robots, by treating visual measurements of the horizon and surroundings as a low-frequency correction to the vestibular estimate. Third, a cerebellar layer will be explored to adapt commands and coordination between higher-level control and the CPG: a structure that can gate between gaits or movement patterns (e.g., walking, running, and turning) and tune the phase relationships among them, in line with the cerebellum's established role in adaptation and coordination. Multilevel spinal-plus-cerebellar synthetic nervous systems have already been demonstrated for insect-like legs \citep{grummer_multilevel_2024}, and hierarchical cortico-cerebellar-spinal models have been proposed for adaptive step-length control in bipedal locomotion \citep{chen_corticocerebellar_2025}. The fourth addition, basal-ganglia circuits realizing the inhibitory and excitatory gating that selects among actions and locomotor modes, would give the controller a substrate for choosing between behaviors rather than only executing one. The database already holds entry points for this program: the human balance and posture cluster and the multisensory recalibration cluster are curated and connected to the locomotor core through the citation graph (Chapter~\ref{ch:database}), and the proprioceptive--vestibular interaction literature will guide how the signals are weighted \citep{akay_relative_2021}.

The hierarchy distinguishes implemented spinal and body-model components from proposed supraspinal and sensory additions (Figure~\ref{fig:sensorium_hierarchy}). The balance-platform work provides a vestibular-feedback precedent; integrating that channel with action selection and adaptation remains future work.

\subsection{Real-Time Hardware Realization}\label{sec:fw_hardware}

The hardware objective is an embedded SNS controller with low-latency joint loops on Teensy-class microcontrollers and network evaluation on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin. Encoders, strain gauges, pressure sensors, contact sensors, and IMUs would supply the sensory channels. Ia and II signals require length and velocity estimates; Ib force estimates must be checked against actuator length and loading direction. Computer-in-the-loop testing should precede physical deployment, with serial communication reserved for logging and high-level commands. Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations} supplies the existing neural-pathway schematics; wiring and commissioning remain integration work.

Prior hardware studies support the feasibility of further controller transfer. \citet{li_controller_2024} and colleagues demonstrated SLIP-based control of a physical PAM-actuated biped; \citet{lutz_mechanical_2025} coupled the Muscle Mutt MuJoCo model to SNS-Toolbox and implemented rough closed-loop stepping on the physical quadruped using potentiometer and pressure feedback. These are precedents, not validation of the present biped. Reverse-pulley transmissions offer a separate mechanical option: the balance-platform demonstrations traded transmitted force for tendon travel, with increased structural reaction loads \citep{bolen_balance_2024,bolen_selfbalancing_2024,bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. Their use at other joints requires route-specific force, excursion, and mounting-load checks.

Because the static force and torque models of Chapters~\ref{ch:muscles} and~\ref{ch:torque} are computationally inexpensive lookup-table candidates, they will serve as the feedforward model inside the real-time controller, while the dynamic pressure--force behavior under pulse-modulated control will draw on the state-space model of \citet{elzein_pressure_2025}. Embedded execution of the complete CPG-plus-reflex network is a future hardware objective. The simulation framework provides a place to test the controller before that transfer, including its responses to stimulation, pathway removal, and changed mechanical conditions.

\subsection{Toward Dynamic Whole-Body Maneuvers}\label{sec:fw_kickflip}

Beyond steady walking, the long-term goal of this line of research is dynamic, whole-body maneuvering: transitions between gaits, reactive balancing, and ultimately ballistic maneuvers that require coordinating the full actuator set through brief, precisely timed activation bursts. My aspiration for this program is a robot that can perform a kickflip, a maneuver that, like its skateboard namesake, demands explosive coordinated extension, precise timing, aerial body orientation, and controlled landing. It is exactly the kind of behavior that exercises every layer of the architecture described above, from contact receptors and spinal pattern generation to vestibular orientation sensing and cerebellar coordination, and it would demonstrate that biomimetic actuation and neuromechanical control scale from steady locomotion to dynamic whole-body movement.

The next experiments can therefore be selected for the question they answer: whether a dynamic actuator model predicts movement, whether a sensory pathway changes coordination, or whether a controller transfers to less-supported mechanics and hardware. Each extends an experimental foundation that is already usable. The common purpose is to make the connection between a design assumption and an observed response traceable.
