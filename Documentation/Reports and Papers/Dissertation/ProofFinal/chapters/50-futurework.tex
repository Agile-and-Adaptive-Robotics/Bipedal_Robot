\chapter{Future Work}\label{ch:futurework}
\graphicspath{{../Figures/50-futurework/}}

The actuator models, joint-torque experiments, correction methods, route-optimization tools, and neuromechanical simulation workflows developed in this dissertation provide a reusable foundation for subsequent research. This chapter identifies five concrete ways in which researchers can apply and extend that foundation: benchmark the OpenSim-to-MuJoCo neuromechanical pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:fw_pipeline}), compare two-layer CPG and sensory-feedback hypotheses (Section~\ref{sec:fw_cpg}), add vestibular, ocular, and cerebellar contributions (Section~\ref{sec:fw_senses}), transfer verified controllers to embedded hardware (Section~\ref{sec:fw_hardware}), and study transitions from steady locomotion to dynamic whole-body maneuvers (Section~\ref{sec:fw_kickflip}). Each direction begins with an artifact or method documented in this dissertation and defines an actionable experiment rather than a step required to complete the present work.

\section{A Neuromechanical Simulation Pipeline}\label{sec:fw_pipeline}

The neuromechanical pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods} links an anatomically grounded OpenSim model, a converted MuJoCo model, BPA force calculations, and an SNS-Toolbox controller. The conversion and sign-audit procedures, the \qty{0.1}{\milli\second} neural coupling, the force-feedback interface, and the assembled two-layer spinal network can be reused independently. A first research opportunity is therefore to treat the pipeline as experimental infrastructure: substitute alternative actuator or tendon models one component at a time, quantify their effects on joint loading and gait, and report the conversion and numerical-stability checks alongside the resulting controller performance.

\subsection{From OpenSim to MuJoCo}

The pipeline begins with the author's modified OpenSim model: the Gait2392-derived robot variant in which the actuator set was reduced and adapted to the BPA hardware and the muscle origin, insertion, and wrapping points were updated to the optimized robot routing \citep{bolen_determination_2019, morrow_optimization_2020}. The OpenSim 4.x model was converted to MuJoCo \citep{todorov_mujoco_2012} using MyoConverter \citep{caggiano_myosuite_2022, wang_myosim_2022}. The accompanying audit procedure inspects the generated model, verifies joint naming and ordering for sensor and actuator mapping, confirms the expected muscle-tendon actuators and attachment geometry, and tests joint signs dynamically when coupled path points make static moment-arm tables unreliable. Researchers can apply the same procedure to alternative musculoskeletal models and publish the audit results as part of the model's provenance rather than treating conversion as a black-box preprocessing step.

The MuJoCo representation uses muscle-like actuators with internal activation state, allowing SNS outputs to drive the biomechanical model through activation dynamics. At each timestep, MuJoCo supplies the kinematics and muscle variables needed to compute proprioceptive signals. This interface permits controlled comparisons among the quasi-static BPA formulation developed here, conventional rigid-tendon muscle actuators, and future series-elastic or pressure-dynamic models without changing the neural-network interface.

\subsection{Coupling the Synthetic Nervous System}

The neural controller is implemented with SNS-Toolbox \citep{nourse_sns_2023}, which supports large networks of spiking and non-spiking neurons and couples directly to MuJoCo. At each timestep, the interface passes Ia, Ib, and II proprioceptive channels plus heel and toe contact signals from the MuJoCo model to the neural core and returns per-muscle activation commands to the MuJoCo activation states. Because sensing, neural computation, and actuation are separated at this interface, investigators can replace an individual afferent model, interneuron pathway, or motor-pool mapping while holding the remaining system fixed.

The staged bring-up sequence used in this dissertation also provides a reproducible validation protocol. Researchers can first exercise the mechanics with open-loop activation patterns to confirm torque and force signs, then introduce contact sensing and one proprioceptive channel, and finally add the complete Ia/Ib/II set. Reporting performance at each stage separates mechanical, sensory, and neural failures and makes comparisons across walking speeds or controller architectures interpretable. A Simscape-based model can provide an independent check of selected mechanical subsystems, whereas MuJoCo remains suited to large closed-loop neuromechanical experiments. The AARL's quadruped provides an additional transfer benchmark: its MuJoCo--SNS controller produced closed-loop coordinated stepping and was transferred to treadmill hardware with potentiometer and pressure feedback \citep{lutz_mechanical_2025}. Researchers can use the bipedal pipeline to test which elements of that transfer generalize from a quadruped to human-inspired leg anatomy and can compare the results with emerging frameworks such as FARMS \citep{arreguit_farms_2023}.

\section{A Two-Layer CPG with Sensory Feedback}\label{sec:fw_cpg}

The prior AnimatLab walker (Section~\ref{sec:prior_walker}) and the MuJoCo--SNS framework provide complementary experimental conditions for studying two-layer CPG control. The suspended AnimatLab model produced alternating stepping-like motion at approximately 4~Hz when its rhythm-generator, pattern-formation, and sensorimotor layers oscillated in phase, but the same feedforward patterns did not support weight-bearing locomotion on a ground plane. Preliminary results from that model were presented at the 2021 NIH BRAIN Initiative Investigators Meeting \citep{bolenTorqueValuesArtificialKnee2021June15-17}. Its instrumented phase-1 data include bilateral joint, neural, foot-contact, and toe-contact channels and therefore provide a baseline feedforward condition. Researchers can compare that condition with closed-loop variants that introduce contact gating, Ia/Ib/II feedback, or both, measuring changes in cadence, stance duty factor, joint kinematics, body support, and perturbation recovery. Such comparisons operationalize the established observation that biomechanics and sensory feedback can regularize different locomotor CPG architectures \citep{deng_biomechanical_2022} and make the contribution of each feedback pathway identifiable.

\subsection{Architecture}

The neural core follows the two-level organization of the mammalian locomotor CPG established by the Rybak group \citep{rybak_modelling_2006, mccrea_organization_2008, rybak_organization_2015}: each leg has a rhythm generator (RG) circuit, which produces the basic locomotor rhythm and controls its period, and a pattern formation (PF) circuit, which shapes the rhythm into phase-appropriate activation of the flexor and extensor motoneuron populations. A left--right rhythm generator circuit coordinates the two legs through commissural inhibition and excitation, following the models of left--right coordination \citep{molkov_mechanisms_2015, danner_central_2016}. The per-leg PF output drives the motoneuron pools --- here, the BPA activation commands for the hip, knee, and ankle muscle groups of each leg.

\subsection{Sensory Feedback Channels}

The interface supports three classes of proprioceptive feedback between the MuJoCo model and the CPG, mirroring the biological afferents organized in the Sensory Afferent Database \citep{bolen_sensory_2023}. Investigators can enable, remove, or perturb these channels individually to test their predicted functions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ia afferents} (muscle spindle primary endings) signal muscle length and velocity, providing stretch reflex pathways and phase-dependent modulation of the PF network.
    \item \textbf{Ib afferents} (Golgi tendon organs) signal muscle force, providing load-dependent regulation of stance-phase extensor activity.
    \item \textbf{II afferents} (muscle spindle secondary endings) signal length, contributing to position sensing and interjoint coordination.
    \item \textbf{Contact receptors} on the heel and toe provide discrete stance and swing boundary signals, driving phase transitions and weight-support switching.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Verification Tests}

The network can be evaluated with a hierarchy of stimulation, deletion, and perturbation tests. Tonic input applied separately to motoneuron, pattern-formation, and rhythm-generator populations tests whether activity propagates with the expected sign, gain, and phase. At the rhythm-generator level, the assembled SNS-Toolbox network already provides a reference result: under tonic drive, the isolated generators oscillate with an approximately \qty{0.9}{\second} period and the two legs in antiphase (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}). Researchers can map the parameter region over which this limit cycle persists, repeat the test after deleting commissural or afferent pathways, and compare the responses with human tonic-stimulation air stepping \citep{selionov_tonic_2009}, the two-level CPG model \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}, and prior neuromechanical implementations \citep{markin_afferent_2010, shevtsova_modeling_2016}. The resulting robustness maps would be more informative than a single tuned gait because they would distinguish a stable architectural property from a narrow parameter solution.

\section{Extending the Sensorium: Vestibular, Ocular, and Cerebellar Contributions}\label{sec:fw_senses}

The modular sensor interface makes three supraspinal extensions directly testable. First, researchers can add IMU-like orientation and angular-velocity signals and compare vestibular gains against the AARL balance-platform results, in which pseudo-vestibular feedback stabilized an inverted pendulum on an oscillating platform \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}, and against analytic neural models of human balance \citep{hilts_dynamic_2019}. Second, visual horizon or scene measurements can provide a low-frequency correction to inertial drift; experiments can quantify the tradeoff between drift rejection and visual latency during platform motion or external perturbations. Third, an adaptive cerebellar layer can be inserted between high-level locomotion commands and the spinal CPG to select gait modules and tune their phase relationships. Multilevel spinal--cerebellar synthetic nervous systems for insect-like legs \citep{grummer_multilevel_2024} and hierarchical cortico-cerebellar-spinal models of bipedal step-length control \citep{chen_corticocerebellar_2025} provide comparison architectures. Factorial removal of proprioceptive, vestibular, and visual channels would then test the weighting hypotheses suggested by the proprioceptive--vestibular literature \citep{akay_relative_2021}.

\section{Physical Realization: A Treadmill Robot with Real-Time Neural Control}\label{sec:fw_hardware}

A direct translational study can deploy the controller on an embedded computing stack: Teensy microcontrollers for low-latency per-joint valve, pressure, and contact loops, and an Nvidia Jetson-class companion computer for SNS evaluation and activation computation. A host computer can remain outside the control loop and provide only data logging and high-level experimental commands, such as selecting a gait or stimulating a named neural pathway. Walking control of a physical musculoskeletal biped with McKibben muscles has been demonstrated with spring-loaded inverted-pendulum controllers \citep{li_controller_2024}, supporting the feasibility of embedded control on anthropomorphic pneumatic legs. The hardware study can also evaluate a specific actuation design tradeoff exposed by the dissertation's route calculations: a block-and-tackle ``reverse pulley'' can exchange BPA force for tendon travel when contraction limits otherwise require impractically long musculotendon routes. This arrangement was demonstrated on the AARL's self-balancing inverted-pendulum platform \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. Researchers can use the body-frame torque calculation and Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry} to optimize pulley placement, then compare range of motion, transmitted torque, structural reaction force, and pneumatic energy use against a direct tendon route. This division of computation is consistent with the embedded backends targeted by SNS-Toolbox \citep{nourse_sns_2023}.

The static force and torque models of Chapters~\ref{ch:methods}--\ref{ch:results} are computationally inexpensive enough to serve as feedforward lookup tables in the embedded controller, while the state-space model of \citet{elzein_pressure_2025} can represent pressure--force dynamics under pulse-modulated control. A useful validation sequence would compare simulation, benchtop joint, and treadmill trials under identical command profiles; introduce parameter uncertainty and sensor delay systematically; and measure tracking error, phase stability, disturbance recovery, and computation time. The instrumentable SNS then enables controlled stimulation and lesion experiments that would be difficult or unethical in a living subject, while the validated BPA and torque models keep the mechanical consequences quantitatively interpretable.

\section{Toward Dynamic Whole-Body Maneuvers}\label{sec:fw_kickflip}

The same framework supports a progression beyond steady walking: gait transitions, reactive balance, and ballistic maneuvers produced by brief, precisely timed activation bursts. Researchers can begin with transitions between walking speeds, add externally applied pushes with randomized timing and direction, and then test jumping, aerial reorientation, and controlled landing. The author's aspirational benchmark for this research program is a robot that can perform a kickflip. Like its skateboard namesake, this maneuver demands explosive coordinated extension, precise timing, aerial body orientation, and controlled landing. Although deliberately ambitious, it supplies measurable intermediate requirements for actuator power, contact detection, spinal phase control, vestibular state estimation, and adaptive coordination. Meeting those requirements sequentially would test whether the dissertation's actuator-to-controller methodology scales from static and cyclic behaviors to dynamic whole-body movement.
