\chapter{The Sensory Afferent Database}\label{ch:database}
\graphicspath{{../Figures/50-database/}{figs/50-database/}}

The Sensory Afferent Database (SADb) is a curated literature resource for studying sensory feedback in locomotion and posture. It links findings from animal and human studies to afferent classes, feedback pathways, and computational models. I developed it within the AARL's NeuroNex Communication, Coordination, and Control in Nervous Systems (C3NS) effort. The resource and the evaluation of AI-assisted literature curation were presented at Neuroscience 2023 and 2024, respectively \citep{bolen_sensory_2023,bolen_using_2024}.

SADb addresses the need to recover measured afferent properties, feedback pathways, and experimental conditions from a dispersed literature. A modeler needs to know what was measured, how a pathway was stimulated or represented, and the locomotor or postural state in which it was studied. SADb brings individual studies into a searchable collection so that researchers can compare findings, locate evidence relevant to a candidate feedback pathway, and follow each record to its source.

The primary deliverable is SADb Explorer, a browser application containing 1,393 literature and related records. It has four main tabs (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}, callout~(A)): Table searches individual records, Pivot compares classifications, Bubble map explores citation links among papers, and Search (online) finds candidate additions through OpenAlex. The records, notes, and citation graph are embedded in the application, so the first three tabs can be used without an internet connection. Search (online) requires an internet connection and lets the user save candidate records for later curation. The application preserves both the records retained for active curation and archived records from the collected material.

The database was curated in Airtable, an online database system, before being transferred to SADb Explorer. Linked tables connect papers to feedback pathways and computational models, with separate records for reviews and recording methods. Paper records contain bibliographic information, curation notes, and classification tags; pathway and model records link these sources to candidate feedback mechanisms and their implementations.

\section{Curation Methods}\label{sec:sadb_methods}

This was a curated research collection, rather than a registered systematic review or an exhaustive search of every relevant publication. I first collected records from selected AARL and personal Zotero collections used for previous papers on locomotion and postural balance, together with a few records already curated in Airtable by my advisor, Dr. Hunt. Duplicate Zotero entries describing the same publication were combined into a single record using DOI and title matching. I expanded the collection through keyword searches, references cited by relevant papers, and searches for subsequent papers citing them.

I initially imported the broader Zotero collections as groups, then narrowed the working base to sensory feedback in locomotion and posture. I retained records reporting sensory-afferent measurements, investigating feedback in locomotion or posture, or modeling a relevant sensory pathway. I removed records outside this scope from the working base. I also flagged records whose source text I could not locate, including conference posters found through ResearchRabbit, and removed them from that base. The application preserves the broader collection as archived records, which users can distinguish with the archive filter. For retained papers, I recorded the animal or human study context where applicable, afferent class, feedback pathway, and the mechanism described by the study.

I implemented a monthly literature watch that queries OpenAlex and Crossref for candidate additions matching selected authors and keywords. The script removes DOI and version duplicates, verifies shortlisted DOIs through Crossref, and writes a shortlist for manual curation. Source-access limitations are recorded with the shortlist; inclusion and classification decisions remain manual.

\section{Results: Application and Corpus Coverage}\label{sec:sadb_corpus}

The application contains 1,393 entries in five record categories: 943 Papers, 247 Models, 157 Review Papers, 42 Feedback pathways, and 4 Recorders. The searchable table includes the complete 1,393-entry collection. The Pivot and Bubble map tabs use the 943 entries in the Papers category. Archived records remain searchable in the application.

Within the application's Papers category, containing 943 entries, 833 carry curation notes, 606 have tags for the animals or human participants studied, 316 carry animal-study assessments, 119 link to feedback pathways, 95 carry an afferent classification, and 363 carry robot-or-simulation translation notes. DOI identifiers are present for 892 papers, and 318 have attached-PDF indicators. Within the Papers category, 730 papers are connected by 11,130 directed citation pairs. The Louvain algorithm groups these papers by their citation connections into 18 named topic clusters (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}, callout~(G)), which help users navigate related studies in the Bubble map tab.

The Table tab combines text search with filters for animal or human study category, pathway, afferent class, record type, archive status, year, and note or PDF availability (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_explorer}). Selecting a record opens its source information, curation notes, and notes on how the reported findings could inform a robot or simulation. The Search (online) tab queries OpenAlex for candidate additions and marks DOIs already represented in SADb.

The Bubble map tab offers two selectable layouts (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}). The year--citations layout places publication year on the horizontal axis and citation count on the vertical axis. The topic landscape instead draws papers as nodes joined by citation links. A spring-layout algorithm balances attraction along links with repulsion between nodes to arrange connected groups together. Its coordinates arrange the network; neither axis measures publication year or citation count. The user controls the layout, display style, record grouping, citation-neighborhood depth, and click action (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}, callout~(B)).

Selecting a paper makes it the reference for the displayed citation neighborhood; the application calls this the focus paper. Its source information appears in the left record area (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}, callout~(C)), together with its curation note and notes on possible applications to a robot or simulation (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}, callout~(D)). The neighborhood selector displays papers up to one, two, or three citation links from the focus paper, following either its references or papers that cite it (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}, callout~(E)). A two-link neighborhood adds one level beyond the directly connected papers. Directly connected papers retain color, while papers reached through additional links are gray. In the illustrated neighborhood of the Grillner (1985) focus paper, 57 in-corpus papers cite it, it cites 13 in-corpus papers, and 555 papers lie within two links. Green triangles identify papers that cite the focus paper, and red circles identify papers cited by it.

The connection list separates papers citing the focus paper from its references (Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_clusters}, callout~(F)). Selecting a listed paper makes it the new focus paper, allowing a reader to follow the development of an idea without repeatedly searching for individual titles. The reference-only and cited-by views isolate either direction, and the detail action opens the underlying record. A researcher can therefore start from a known experiment, inspect earlier studies supporting it, follow subsequent models or experiments citing it, and read the associated translation notes before choosing a candidate pathway for simulation.

The Pivot tab counts papers by two selected dimensions, including topic cluster, decade, primary author, animal or human study category, feedback pathway, and afferent classification. Figure~\ref{fig:sadb_pivot} crosses afferent classes with categories of animal and human studies: each cell counts papers carrying both tags. Rows and columns can be ordered alphabetically or by count. Selecting a cell opens the corresponding records in the Table tab, and a return control restores the pivot. The pivot is computed over the 943 entries in the Papers category, including archived entries, rather than all 1,393 records. Because a paper can carry several animal or afferent tags, it can contribute to several cells. The counts describe the distribution of curated records, not independent experimental observations or the strength of evidence for a pathway.

\subsection{AI-Assisted Literature Triage}\label{sec:sadb_triage}

I evaluated AI-assisted literature identification, summarization, and categorization. This work was presented at Neuroscience 2024 \citep{bolen_using_2024}. The comparison includes ResearchRabbit, Litmaps, txyz, SciSpace, and MirrorThink. Some tools helped identify related papers or summarize straightforward material, but categorization remained inadequate: none received a good rating, and only txyz was rated adequate. Complex models and nuanced physiological findings required substantial human correction. The tools therefore offered limited help with the central task of accurately assigning papers to database categories. I made the final inclusion, classification, and removal decisions.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Uses of the Database}\label{sec:sadb_use}

SADb helps researchers connect an account of sensory feedback to the experiments and models supporting it. Searchable notes, linked records, and citation neighborhoods provide complementary routes through a literature spread across animal studies, human studies, and modeling disciplines. A reader can begin with a familiar paper, follow its references and later citing studies, and compare the conditions under which a feedback mechanism was observed. This makes the resource useful for learning a research area and for identifying questions that require a closer reading of the primary evidence.

The database also supports the design of neural models for locomotion and balance. Table~\ref{tab:sadb_afferents} in Appendix~\ref{app:sadb_records} summarizes afferent classes, receptors, and encoded signals for selecting model inputs. Table~\ref{tab:sadb_pathways} gives representative feedback pathways, their actions in the step cycle, and the studies linked to those records. These source links let a modeler examine the experimental basis for a candidate connection and the assumptions needed to implement it.

Experiments and models answer complementary questions. \citet{ting_limited_2005} linked muscle-activation synergies to limb endpoint-force vectors in cats responding to support-surface translations. \citet{latash_organization_2020} compared cat stepping patterns with a mathematical model to investigate how rhythm-generator operation could change with control drive under tied-belt and split-belt conditions. The first study connects measured muscle activity to postural output; the second tests a candidate circuit explanation for locomotor phase patterns. Following the source papers exposes these differences in task, preparation, and explanation.

Closed-loop models also show why a neural mechanism must be considered with its body. \citet{geyer_musclereflex_2010} generated walking in a human musculoskeletal model using phase-dependent muscle reflexes. In a rat neuromechanical model, \citet{deng_biomechanical_2022} found that biomechanical entrainment and sensory feedback reduced behavioral differences associated with alternative CPG parameter choices. Model records provide starting points for comparing these approaches and two-level rhythm-generation and pattern-formation architectures \citep{rybak_modelling_2006}. For the controllers in Chapter~\ref{ch:sns}, pathway selection is followed by explicit choices and tests of receptor dynamics, gains, delays, and species-specific assumptions.

Robotic tests can also return predictions to biology. \citet{ijspeert_swimming_2007} implemented a spinal-cord model in a salamander robot that switched between walking and swimming as control drive changed. The model predicted lower intrinsic frequencies for limb oscillators than for body oscillators, and the authors presented biological data supporting that prediction. This exchange illustrates the broader motivation for the humanoid project: biological evidence informs a controllable model, whose behavior can generate questions for subsequent biological experiments.

The balance, posture, and multisensory-recalibration clusters provide entry points for the vestibular and visual extensions proposed in Section~\ref{sec:fw_senses}. Searches of existing animal studies let users compare the conditions and tasks behind a proposed feedback rule before testing it in a robotic or computational model. Reusing this evidence for model-based tests supports the aim of reducing reliance on further animal experimentation (Section~\ref{sec:bg_ethics}).

The application makes physiological evidence accessible for controller design, but selecting a pathway is only the beginning of a neuromechanical experiment. Its encoding, connection sign, gain, and interaction with the body must still be implemented and tested. Chapter~\ref{ch:sns} develops the models and simulation tools needed to make that next step explicit.
