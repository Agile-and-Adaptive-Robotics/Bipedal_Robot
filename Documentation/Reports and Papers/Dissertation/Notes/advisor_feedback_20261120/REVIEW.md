## Replies and engineering evidence

| Review question | What the source or experiment establishes | Change made |
|---|---|---|
| Which corrections did MyoConverter make automatically? | MyoConverter translates the OpenSim model. The project restored maximum active force in eight actuator entries, corrected reversed hip actions, and repaired patella tracking. A separate project patch handles OpenSim fiber-equilibrium failures during conversion by excluding failed poses and retaining default parameters when no force map can be used. | Sections 6.2 and D.1 distinguish translation, repair, and the limitations on the resulting force estimates. |
| Did the moment-arm discrepancy change object collision? | The muscle path sets the moment arm through which tension produces torque. Explicit contact pairs determine which geometry can exert a ground reaction. | The two issues have separate explanations and fixes. |
| Can the converted human model reproduce active muscle force during the recorded walk? | OpenSim static optimization reports active force during motion. The earlier stationary replay mixed active and passive tension and misplaced a patellar guide. With the motion, guide, and force definition matched, native active-force R² was 0.717 for rectus femoris and 0.829 for the short head of biceps femoris. Applying the OpenSim active-force equations on the converted paths gave 0.995 and 0.9996. | In a separate forward test, changing both muscles on both legs preserved foot contacts but reduced left-foot clearance from 30.5 to 6.7 mm with the same controller and rig. The human knee-torque targets in Chapter 4 came directly from OpenSim. |
| How did the CAD controller choose tension for the 54 muscle routes? | Each route contributes its tension in newtons multiplied by its signed moment arm to the joint torque. The controller solved for a nonnegative tension on each route that approximated the requested joint torques. In the CAD stepping trial, a first-order ideal actuator developed the resulting tension. In the separate pneumatic robotbody simulation, pressure and contraction determine actual tension on all 54 assigned routes. | At 21.61 s, the CAD controller requested 2,528 N in the left knee extensor. Its ideal actuator delivered 2,088 N. Under the separate standing-trial sleeve assumptions, this BPA would deliver only 1,142 N at that same route length and 620 kPa. |
| Was the BPA pressure-driven, and did it implement a published hysteresis model? | Yes to pressure drive. Pressure and contraction entered the fitted tension equation. In the separate fixture, pressure followed a delayed command, and a filtered lengthening/shortening signal could perturb the resulting force. The fixture source identifies those parameters as assumptions and gives no published hysteresis model as their source. | Chapter 6 explains the actual pressure input and what the fixture tested. Appendix D describes the functioning pressure-driven models. The engineering record alone retains the sign error in an earlier, superseded trial. |
| Was the AARL robot CAD imported into both programs? | Yes. The SOLIDWORKS design supplied the actual robot geometry in both MuJoCo and Simscape Multibody. Meshes describe shape in MuJoCo, while model definitions supply mass properties, joints, and four-bar closure. The native Simscape Multibody import generated a Simulink model with bodies and joints. SNS-Simscape supplies the neural and actuator blocks. | Section 6.3 names each destination. A separate example runs the bilateral OpenSim robot counterpart through the MathWorks MuJoCo–Simulink bridge. The counterpart and the AARL CAD assembly are different source models. |
| What did the W2L deletion experiment establish? | On level ground with the same virtual harness, drive, and forward push, the walker advanced in all three conditions. Heel strikes almost alternated with the circuit intact (0.498 of a stride) and with only Ib feedback removed (0.494). Removing the broader group shifted the relative timing to 0.223. Forward distance alone would have missed the disruption. | The experiment demonstrates a controlled test of how connections affect coordination. A group deletion does not identify one responsible connection. The section also distinguishes the shared Li–Deng rhythm generator from the bilateral W2L architecture and identifies the joints they recruit. |
| What did a foot-contact event mean in the Di Russo transfer? | The published SCONE implementation restarted the newly loaded leg’s oscillator once when its delayed foot load crossed its threshold. The adapted MuJoCo test detected the upward crossing of 20 N at the heel. Testing whether the heel was loaded on every update had repeatedly restarted the oscillator during one stance. | The original SCONE/OpenSim replay produced 16 steps. The prescribed-input circuit comparison agreed. Correcting the touchdown detector in the Gait2392 transfer still left a fall. The experiments establish where the transfer succeeded and which behavior was unproven. |
| Where do the spiking comparisons belong? | Appendix B contains equations, wiring, and the quantities used for calibration. D.5 compares movement and feedback with those implementations in several mechanical models. | Retained the results in D.5 and connected the two appendices in both directions. |
