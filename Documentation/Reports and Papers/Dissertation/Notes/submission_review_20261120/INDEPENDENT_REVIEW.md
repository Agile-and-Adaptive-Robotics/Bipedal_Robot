## Findings for submission

1. **Chapter 4, §4.1.4, p. 78. Restore the star in the definition of unloaded contraction.** The passage says “$\varepsilon=1$ and $F=0$.” In Chapter 3, $\varepsilon$ is the change in actuator length divided by its resting length. A value of one would require an actuator of zero length. The value that reaches one at the unloaded 620 kPa reference is *relative* contraction, $\varepsilon^*=\varepsilon/\varepsilon_{620}$. The corresponding sentence in the current torque paper already says “$\varepsilon^{*} = 1$ with $F = 0$.” Add the star in the dissertation. This corrects notation and leaves every experimental value intact. See `Final/chapters/30-torque.tex:99`, Chapter 3 Eqs. 3.1–3.3, Appendix A §A.3.1, and `Knee_Torque_Test/ProofFinal/Main.tex:200`.

2. **Bibliography, pp. 194 and 198. Repair two entries.** The Severin entry actually prints “Verify exact bibliographic details against Stuart (2007).” Its title also omits the muscle *receptors*. Stuart’s Fig. 7 attributes its treadmill apparatus to Fig. 1 in Severin et al. Stuart cites the English translation in *Biophysics* 12, 575–586. The Russian original is F. V. Severin, G. N. Orlovskiĭ, and M. L. Shik, “Work of muscle receptors during controlled locomotion,” *Biofizika* 12(3), 502–511 (1967). Use the original record or identify the two editions with their respective pagination. On p. 198, Wu and Cavanagh appears as “FOR STANDARDIZATION IN THE REPORTING OF KINEMATIC DATA,” and “PeterR Cavanagh.” The publisher gives Ge Wu and Peter R. Cavanagh, “ISB recommendations for standardization in the reporting of kinematic data,” *Journal of Biomechanics* 28(10), 1257–1261 (1995), DOI `10.1016/0021-9290(95)00017-C`. Preserve the BibTeX keys `severin_1967` and `wu_for_1995`. [Original Severin record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4246509/) · [Stuart’s article](https://doi.org/10.1093/icb/icm037) · [Wu and Cavanagh, publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/002192909500017C)

## Recheck of the revised Chapter 5 and Appendix E

I reread both revised sources and inspected Figures 5.2–5.5 at their actual placement in the 326-page full-dissertation proof. Chapter 5 still starts at p. 122. Chapter 6 and subsequent material in this proof start one printed page later than in the baseline review above. Chapter 5 now runs through p. 134, and Appendix E occupies pp. 303–306.

- **Methods, §5.1.1, pp. 124–125.** The example identifies and cites McCrea et al. on first mention, then explains the preparation, the two fields, the resulting proposed test, and the record lookup. This is where a reader needs those instructions. Results §5.2.1 on p. 126 also opens with the textual citation and now states what the entry reveals about the same nerve stimulus.
- **Results, §§5.2–5.2.4, pp. 125–132.** The opening explains what researchers can do with the app, why the complete collection matters, and what its three views contribute. Online search retrieves metadata and available abstracts for human curation. The software implementation also marks known DOIs and saves a suggested record. A single live OpenAlex query returned HTTP 200 with JSON using the same field selection. The full and retained record counts have clear denominators in Appendix E. The main-text numbers illustrate a research consequence, including the incompleteness of pathway tagging and the overlap of Ia and Ib groups.
- **Figures, pp. 127–131.** In Fig. 5.2 the reader can move from the tagged paper to the DOI and then compare the observation with a proposed model test. Fig. 5.3 holds the group I input and target motor pool fixed and changes the net sign by phase. An additional Rybak or Di Russo connectome would introduce interneurons and synaptic weights that this experiment cannot determine. Fig. 5.4 makes the overlapping classifications concrete. Fig. 5.5 distinguishes a paper’s references from the publications that cite it. The printed callouts in Figs. 5.2, 5.4, and 5.5 match their captions and include parentheses.
- **Experimental precision.** The original study recorded inhibitory and excitatory postsynaptic potentials. Stretch evoked a response through Ia fibers. The nerve-stimulus evidence concerns group I input, and the investigators did not isolate Ib fibers in the nerve-stimulus test. The revised text respects those distinctions. The principal editor corrected “the two spinal preparations” to “the midbrain-evoked and acute spinal preparations,” and Appendix E explains the difference in their outcomes. [McCrea et al., original paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8558481/)
- **Appendix E, pp. 303–306.** Table E1 gives the full and retained record sets side by side. The other tables explain overlapping labels, identify the papers behind named pathways, and preserve the experimental qualification that a researcher would need to reuse the worked example. They earn their place outside the main narrative.

## Festo patent follow-up

Chapter 7 originally identified the commercial wall construction from Festo’s product data. The submission-draft source called that same catalog “Festo patent literature.” Neither reference pointed to a patent. The relevant original grant is *Actuating means*, by Dieter Bergemann, Bernd Lorenz, and Axel Thallemer, issued to Festo AG & Co. on February 26, 2002, as US 6,349,746 B1. Its Figs. 2–4 and accompanying description show two concentric, oppositely wound reinforcing layers separated by the compliant hose material. The commercial catalog separately documents the pressure-sealing rubber wall and aramid reinforcement of the DMSP. The model can use that arrangement as a starting point. Specimen sections are needed for layer thicknesses, fiber angles, interfaces, and the separate fabric covering illustrated in Fig. 7.2(C). The chapter can test the explanation with force and geometry measurements on the actuators actually used.
