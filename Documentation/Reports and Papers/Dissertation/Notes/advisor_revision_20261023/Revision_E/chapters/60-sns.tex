\chapter{Synthetic Nervous System Simulation}\label{ch:sns}
\graphicspath{{figs/60-sns/}{figs/60-sns/AnimatLab_reference/}}

The actuator and torque models of Chapters~\ref{ch:muscles} and~\ref{ch:torque} describe actuator force and joint torque. Studying neural control requires connecting those mechanics to a synthetic nervous system (SNS). This chapter develops a simulation framework in which neural controllers can drive the Gait2392 musculoskeletal model and separate BPA-actuated models, receive sensory feedback, and be tested under specified support and disturbance conditions. Its purpose is to make controller behavior measurable as neural organization, actuator mechanics, and mechanical support are changed. The contribution is the implemented framework and its demonstrated tests; unsupported walking remains an open control problem.

\section{The AnimatLab Bipedal Walker}\label{sec:prior_walker}

For a course project in ME 610: Neuromechanical Modeling, Morrow and I combined the bipedal mechanics of \citet{li_bipedal_2017} with the two-level central pattern generator (CPG) of \citet{deng_neuromechanical_2019} in AnimatLab \citep{cofer_animatlab_2010}. The mechanical model had a pelvis and bilateral thigh, shank, foot, and toe segments, with sagittal-plane joints and antagonist Hill-type muscles at the hip, knee, and ankle. I added a passive toe joint. The initial controller used one rhythm generator (RG) to control both sides, with separate pattern-formation (PF) groups for each leg: one for the hip and one for the knee and ankle. The shared RG layer contained four neurons. We added afferent feedback at the motoneuron (MN) level. Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_model_architecture}(A) shows the mechanical model, and Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_model_architecture}(B) shows the original shared-RG controller that Morrow and I built.

We adjusted applied currents, synaptic weights, and muscle parameters by hand. The suspended model developed alternating muscle activity and stepping-like motion, presented at the 2021 NIH BRAIN Initiative Investigators Meeting \citep{bolenTorqueValuesArtificialKnee2021June15-17}. It did not achieve weight-bearing walking.

Years later, I split the shared RG into one RG for each side and connected them through commissural pathways. I also extended sensory feedback to the RG and PF layers and added foot-contact feedback. This later bilateral-RG extension is referred to here as the Walker Two-Layer (W2L) model; it is distinct from the original controller shown in Figure~\ref{fig:animatlab_model_architecture}(B). Section~\ref{sec:w2l_perturbation} reports its coupled-model tests; further circuit schematics and implementation records are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:simulation_verification}.

The suspended motion left an important question unresolved: could the controller maintain coordinated activity when the model contacted the ground and had to support load? Answering it required control over the amount and direction of mechanical support, access to sensory and motor signals, and repeatable changes to the controller or body. Those requirements motivated the framework developed here. The Li-derived W2L walker, the Gait2392 human model, and the BPA-routed robot model are distinct mechanical models; a result obtained with one is identified accordingly.

\section{Knowledge Gaps and the New Tools}\label{sec:sns_gaps}

The project lacked a tested way to connect its neural controllers to the human reference and artificial-muscle models and evaluate them under comparable conditions. The missing capability was more specific than generating rhythmic joint motion. I needed to choose a controller, define the mechanical model and sensory inputs, specify how the body was supported, and measure what changed after a controlled intervention. Without those controls, an improvement in motion could not be assigned to neural feedback, muscle mechanics, or external support.

I addressed this gap by connecting OpenSim-derived models to MuJoCo and SNS-Toolbox, implementing a BPA-force calculation, and adding procedures for support, stimulation, perturbation, and parameter search. On the human reference model, alternative neural organizations can be evaluated against the same mechanical description. Separate W2L and BPA-actuated models extend the workflow to different bodies and actuators. The SNS-Simscape library provides graphical circuit construction and component demonstrations in MATLAB. These environments serve different parts of the work; importing a mechanism into Simscape is a separate achievement from controlling it with the demonstrated circuits.

The framework is evaluated at three levels. Circuit tests ask whether a controller produces the intended neural response. Interface tests ask whether activation, muscle force, and sensory measurements pass correctly between the neural and mechanical models. Coupled tests ask how the resulting motion changes with support, sensory pathways, or disturbances. The results below demonstrate each level within a stated scope. They establish a working basis for controlled neuromechanical experiments while identifying the balance, model-transfer, and controller-tuning problems that remain.

Existing tools supply the components of this framework. OpenSim provides musculoskeletal geometry, MyoConverter translates that geometry to MuJoCo, and SNS-Toolbox supplies neuron and synapse models \citep{delp_opensim_2007, caggiano_myosuite_2022, todorov_mujoco_2012, nourse_sns_2023}. MuJoCo supports scripted dynamics, contact, and repeated evaluations; the version used here models rigid tendons. Simulink supplies graphical circuit assembly, and Simscape Multibody supplies mechanical imports, including CAD-derived mechanisms. AnimatLab contains the original walker and reference circuits. My contribution connects these components through the model conversions, actuator calculations, circuit implementations, and tests described below.

\section{Software Tools}\label{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}

The tools connect musculoskeletal geometry, actuator forces, and neural circuits. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows these connections and their relationship to the proposed hardware system.

\subsection{Model Conversion and Actuator Coupling}\label{sec:sns_existing_tools}

My first conversion attempt used the robot model, which combined BPA routing on the Gait2392 skeleton with unchanged human muscle parameters. MyoConverter's muscle-equilibrium checks rejected infeasible combinations of routed length and muscle parameters. I therefore separated conversion of the human reference from reconstruction of the robot routing. This distinction matters because an error caused by an artificial-actuator route should not be interpreted as a property of the original human model.

The human-model conversion also required specific repairs. Three small hip rotators per side, the quadratus femoris, gemelli, and piriformis, were disabled at model load. The converted model retains 92 actuator entries, but these six contribute no force. The model retains the OpenSim and International Society of Biomechanics coordinate convention; frame transformations and individual-actuator tests check that joint motion and torque directions have the intended signs.

The source represents patella motion through muscle insertion locations that vary with knee angle. In the converted model, limits on the joints representing these moving path points conflicted with their coupling constraints. Removing those conflicting limits allowed the coupled path points to follow their prescribed trajectories. The rectus femoris path also required a patella-tracking via point to restore its knee-extension action. Explicit collision pairs introduced unwanted contacts, so contact was restricted to the foot--ground pairs. The anatomical joint limits also had to be re-enabled; these driver joints were treated separately from the coupled path-point joints. Passive joint torques supplemented the joint behavior. These repairs explain why conversion needed checks beyond successful loading: a model could load while producing an incorrect torque direction or unintended contact force.

Comparisons with the OpenSim attachment paths and single-actuator tests checked the transferred geometry and force directions. They do not establish that every muscle reproduces the source force throughout its range or that the controller produces walking. The neural and mechanical models exchange signals at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} solver time step. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_conversion_repairs} records the individual repairs and their scope.

For the BPA-routed robot model, an actuator-force calculation connects the neural and mechanical simulations. It takes the simulated muscle length and pressure as inputs and evaluates the quasi-static force model from Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}; the resulting force acts along the simulated actuator path. This calculation does not include valve or pressure dynamics. Length, velocity, force, and foot-contact signals return to the controller through sensory channels. The correction terms of Chapter~\ref{ch:torque} do not by themselves establish that transferred routing and compliance are correct in a dynamic model; those properties require separate checks.

\subsection{Support, Stimulation, and Parameter Search}\label{sec:sns_experiment_controls}

The MuJoCo simulations allow mechanical support to be specified independently of the neural controller. A virtual harness applies forces and torques to the body: it can hold the model above the ground, lower it into contact, retain vertical support, or constrain selected directions of translation and rotation. These settings represent different experimental conditions. Suspended motion tests whether the controller and actuators can move the limbs, whereas supported ground contact adds loading and sensory feedback. Progression with retained support is reported separately from unsupported walking.

The training sequence, or curriculum, progresses from simpler conditions toward greater mechanical demand. Its stages include air stepping, supported standing, ground-contact stepping, and reduced support. Parameters can be held fixed to test transfer between stages or re-tuned at a new stage to seek a viable configuration. These procedures answer different questions: successful re-tuning shows that a configuration can be found, while a fixed-controller test shows whether an existing controller tolerates the change. A stage is demonstrated only when its stated motion and stability criteria are met.

Neural interventions include tonic currents, brief stimuli to selected circuit populations, and removal of specified feedback or coupling pathways. Mechanical interventions include timed forces at the pelvis and changes to segment mass and inertia. The recorded outputs include membrane voltage, actuator force, joint motion, foot contact, body position and orientation, and harness force. Together, these controls allow the neural response and the resulting motion to be examined in the same experiment.

Optuna automates parameter searches within specified bounds. Each candidate is evaluated using measures such as bilateral contact cycles, inter-leg phase, forward displacement, joint trajectories, posture, and survival. The score determines which candidate is selected; a separate replay checks that candidate under the stated conditions. Optuna is used as a search tool, without a claim that it finds a global optimum or outperforms other optimizers. Interpretation depends on the objective and validity checks, including confirmation that every searched parameter reaches the implemented model.

\subsection{Block Programming Neural Pathways}\label{sec:sns_new_tools}

The SNS-Simscape library implements neuron, synapse, afferent-encoder, activation, and muscle-force elements as editable Simulink blocks. The library retains the conductance-based neuron and synapse formulation supplied by SNS-Toolbox; it does not introduce a new neuron model. Verification uses selected circuits under matched inputs. Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_sns_implementation} shows the core blocks and graded-neuron signal flow.

Transcribing the source circuits also required distinguishing synapse-type parameters from per-instance editor fields. The Deng AnimatLab project contains 90 neurons, 196 synapses, and 58 named synapse types. Many instance fields retain a common editor default, while the named types specify different pathway conductances. The extraction records both fields so that a uniform default does not replace the source's pathway-specific strengths. A matching neuron count alone would not detect that error.

OpenSim-derived and SolidWorks-derived mechanisms were imported into Simscape Multibody. These mechanical imports are separate from the standalone reflex demonstrations.

\section{Verification of the Implemented Interfaces}\label{sec:preliminary_sim_results}

The tests address three distinct questions: whether a circuit produces its expected response, whether implementations agree under matched inputs, and whether the neural and mechanical models exchange signals in a closed loop.  The larger evaluation sets and their limitations are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:simulation_verification}; neuron equations and calibration details remain in Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations}.

\subsection{Reflex Response}\label{sec:sns_simulink_demos}

The library assembles Ia stretch excitation, reciprocal inhibition, and Ib autogenic inhibition in a reduced-order antagonist knee model (Figure~\ref{fig:simulink_reflex_topology}). A second demonstration tests the stretch reflex in an arm holding a beverage container as it is loaded. With the reflex enabled, arm sag was \ang{2.00}, compared with \ang{7.85} when it was disabled. The comparison checks the implemented feedback response; experimental calibration of the mechanical parameters remains necessary.

\subsection{Published Neural Behaviors}\label{sec:sns_published_behaviors}

An activity-based reduction of the two-level circuit of \citet{rybak_modelling_2006} was tested against selected published neural behaviors. At the published drive settings, increasing extensor drive produced a longer extensor phase, while increasing flexor drive reversed that relationship. The deletion tests distinguished the two circuit levels: a PF-level pulse suppressed flexor MN bursts without resetting the RG schedule, whereas an RG-level pulse shifted the subsequent rhythm. The first returning flexor burst differed from the extrapolated schedule by 0.001 periods after the PF perturbation and 0.416 periods after the RG perturbation. All 13 specified checks passed, including deterministic repeat runs.

This result reproduces selected qualitative behaviors of the published neural model. The implementation uses activity-based populations in place of the original spiking formulation, calibrated global conductances, and adapted pulse amplitudes and integration settings. It therefore tests the two-level explanation of resetting and non-resetting deletions without claiming an exact numerical reproduction of the original model or any walking mechanics. The Deng-derived and Di Russo-derived tests below have different scopes and should not inherit this result.

\subsection{Matched Neural Implementations}\label{sec:crossengine_results}

The contact-driven W2L rhythm core was implemented in NumPy and native Simulink blocks from a common connectivity specification. Both implementations received the same input protocol. Burst periods matched at \qty{1.000}{\second}, all ten expected bursts were reproduced, and voltage-trace correlation was at least 0.99996 (Figure~\ref{fig:w2l_crossengine}). The agreement is specific to this circuit and input protocol. Together with the contact-removal test, it checks the contact-driven design principle; it is not a quantitative comparison with the original Li experiment.

Removing the phasic heel-contact inputs caused the circuit to latch in both implementations. The alternating activity therefore depends on the specified sensory drive.

A separate Simulink implementation of a Deng-derived RG/PF circuit, with a fixed sodium-inactivation time constant of \qty{350}{\milli\second}, produced ten bursts over \qty{20}{\second} following a single \qty{10}{\nano\ampere}, \qty{1}{\milli\second} pulse. Its period was \qty{1.938}{\second}, compared with approximately \qty{1.94}{\second} in our NumPy reference, which was itself translated from the Nourse/Deng parameter tables. This test demonstrates self-sustained activity after a brief input, unlike the contact-driven comparison above. The original Deng AnimatLab project was extracted for connectivity and parameters but was not run for this comparison. The published Deng perturbation experiment therefore remains untested in translation. This comparison also does not demonstrate pointwise voltage agreement: the logged maximum voltage differences were approximately \qty{63}{\milli\volt}.

Additional tests checked the graphical implementation at different scales. In the five-neuron SNS-Toolbox tutorial circuit, final membrane voltages agreed with the NumPy implementation to within \qty{1.1e-5}{\milli\volt}. The persistent-sodium tutorial pair reproduced its winner and loser steady states, but retained transient differences. For the 410-neuron export, the maximum difference across all final membrane voltages at \qty{0.3}{\second} was \qty{4.201e-6}{\milli\volt} with a \qty{2}{\milli\second} Euler time step. This short-window check tests translation of the network equations and wiring; it does not establish long-duration agreement or transfer of a tuned walking controller.

A Li-derived contact-driven circuit was also assembled with 58 neurons and 98 connections. This implementation substitutes graded stance cells with slow adaptation for the original spiking cells. Under scripted alternating heel inputs, it produced rhythmic activity with an inter-leg correlation of $-0.271$. It is an adaptation tested with imposed contacts, rather than a reproduction of the original circuit's closed-loop walking experiment.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Model Coupling}

The MuJoCo--Simulink bridge was tested against an independent Python integration using single-muscle activation, different control rates, and sensor readback. Readback was bit-exact over the scored window. The exported neural network also drove all 92 actuators during a \qty{5}{\second} open-loop run without afferent return. A separate reflex experiment exercised commands and sensory measurements through the interface. These tests establish selected force and signal paths across the interface, rather than a complete transferred locomotor controller.

A separate W2L BPA-actuation test replaced all 12 muscle actuators with the Festo force model. Both knees completed at least three air-stepping excursions, with periods of approximately \qty{1.54}{\second}, and the model subsequently maintained height and foot loading with retained support. Peak pressure was \qty{372}{\kPa}, below the \qty{620}{\kPa} ceiling. This is direct evidence that the controller, BPA-force calculation, and mechanical model operated together. The retained harness supplied substantial support, and no matched Hill-muscle versus BPA comparison was performed. This W2L test is distinct from the converted 92-actuator human model used in the bridge tests.

The Simulink air-stepping demonstration connects eight BPA-force elements to two planar legs with a fixed pelvis and no ground contact. It establishes that neural commands can drive the actuator and joint calculations together. However, the approximately \qty{0.31}{\second} swing period differed substantially from the RG period of \qty{1.938}{\second}. The observed limb oscillation therefore does not establish that the mechanics follow the neural rhythm. Mechanical imports from OpenSim and SolidWorks remain another stage of integration; the standalone reflex and air-stepping demonstrations do not establish control of those imported mechanisms.

\subsection{Perturbation Robustness and Pathway Deletions}\label{sec:w2l_perturbation}

The W2L tests examine whether a controller retains bilateral contact cycling when the body or selected neural pathways change. An implementation check showed that the initial search applied only six of the 61 saved parameter fields; the other 55 fields did not reach the model. Those records describe a controller with the remaining pathways at their default strengths. The results reported here use a later replay with the stored individual fields applied. Because those values had not been optimized under the corrected mapping, this replay tests a candidate controller rather than a newly optimized solution.

With the stored individual fields applied and settings held fixed, the control completed 17 left and 16 right heel-contact cycles during the \qty{16}{\second} record (Figure~\ref{fig:w2l_perturbation}). Removing the crossed heel pathway produced 16/16 cycles, the Ia chain 13/13, the Renshaw pathways 16/14, the heel cascade 17/15, and commissural coupling 13/12. Removing Ib load feedback produced no heel-contact cycles on either side. The specific result is that Ib feedback was necessary for contact cycling in this configuration, while commissural removal reduced the count without abolishing it. Contact counts alone do not show that phase, posture, or gait quality remained unchanged.

One-second RG stimuli and \qty{40}{\newton}, \qty{0.15}{\second} fore-aft pushes also left bilateral contact cycling present. Nevertheless, the stronger stimulus reached approximately \ang{62.8} pelvis tilt, and the forward and backward pushes reached approximately \ang{49.6} and \ang{47.7}. These runs do not satisfy a \ang{45} posture criterion merely because cycling persists. Recovery of the original gait requires a separately defined phase, posture, and timing measure.

The corrected candidate was also tested with thigh or distal-leg masses and inertias changed by $\pm10$ and $\pm20$ percent, and whole-leg properties changed by $\pm10$ percent. All ten conditions retained bilateral contact cycling, but nine exceeded the \ang{45} tilt gate. Only one condition passed the recorded survival criterion. Minimum pelvis height across these records was approximately \qty{0.898}{\metre}. This is evidence of persistent cycling with inadequate posture robustness, rather than ten successful robust-walking tests.

All these evaluations retained a virtual harness with vertical height feedback and constraints on lateral motion, roll, and yaw. The nominal weight-support setting was \qty{25}{\percent}; the realized force also depended on the feedback terms. Earlier searches reduced the nominal setting from 70 to 10 percent with re-tuning between settings, but those records precede full application of the individual fields. Their failed local conductance probes, 9/12 at nominal 25 percent and 7/12 at nominal 10 percent, also describe the earlier effective controller. They cannot certify the robustness of the corrected candidate. These are W2L results, not demonstrations of the BPA-routed Gait2392 robot model or harness-free walking.

A separate controller derived from \citet{dirusso_investigating_2023} was coupled to the Gait2392 human model. It uses per-muscle reflex parameters and a staged optimization procedure, with changes to the source model and optimization method. The recorded second-stage stability search completed 120 trials without a configuration passing its survival criterion. This is a negative result for that implementation and search, rather than a reproduction of the published walking result. It also shows why completing a connectome transcription or obtaining a fitted neural output cannot substitute for testing the coupled mechanics.

\subsection{Feedback and Reduced Hand Drive}

The human-model feedback-tuning campaign provides a complementary test of sustained activity. With the selected feedback parameters fixed, bilateral stepping-like motion persisted as the added hand-drive currents were reduced to zero, with 8 and 11 contact cycles at zero scale. The original feedback configuration lost bilateral activity below 0.85 of those added currents. However, the descending drive remained active, the mechanics retained support, and the motion was in place. This comparison tests the effect of feedback re-tuning on dependence on added drive; it is not a fixed-controller feedback-intact versus feedback-disabled experiment. A matched feedback-ablation pair on the walking BPA model remains absent.

\section{Capabilities and Remaining Limits}\label{sec:sns_discussion}

The framework addresses the project's model-integration gap by making neural control, muscle mechanics, sensory return, and support conditions explicit and adjustable. It connects the Gait2392 reference model to neural controllers, provides force calculations for BPA-actuated models, and supplies procedures for staged tuning and controlled interventions. Graphical circuit construction in Simulink complements the scripted evaluations. These capabilities allow a controller to be examined at the circuit, interface, and coupled-model levels before a whole-model result is interpreted.

The tests show what that framework can establish. The Rybak-derived neural model reproduced selected phase-resetting behaviors, the contact-driven rhythm core agreed across implementations, and the BPA gate demonstrated air stepping and supported standing with the actuator force model inside the simulation. W2L pathway tests linked Ib feedback to bilateral contact cycling at one operating point. They also showed why cycling and walking must be distinguished: the corrected mass-change candidate exceeded the posture criterion in nine of ten conditions. The Li transcription changes the original neuron dynamics, and the Simulink air-stepping mechanics do not yet follow the RG period. These limits identify specific model and control problems for subsequent work.

The present contribution is a usable framework for developing and testing neuromechanical controllers, with demonstrated components and explicit limits. Establishing a general biological mechanism will require matched interventions and comparisons with the corresponding biological or published-model results. Achieving unsupported walking will require further balance and weight-bearing control. The framework makes those questions accessible to controlled simulation; it does not resolve them by the existence of a tuned trajectory. Chapter~\ref{ch:futurework} discusses the remaining integration and hardware work.
