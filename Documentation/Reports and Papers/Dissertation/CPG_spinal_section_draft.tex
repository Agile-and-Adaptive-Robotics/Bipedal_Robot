\section{Spinal Cord Circuit Model for the Human Neuromuscular Model}
\label{sec:spinal-cpg}

\subsection{Overall Architecture}
\label{sec:spinal-cpg-arch}

The spinal circuit driving the converted Gait2392 model is a two-level
central pattern generator (CPG) in the style of Rybak and McCrea
\citep{rybak2006, mccrea2008}, implemented with the synthetic nervous
system (SNS) toolbox \citep{nourse2023} and coupled to the MuJoCo
musculoskeletal plant at the motoneuron-pool level. The network
comprises 410 non-spiking neurons. Per side, it contains: a
rhythm-generator (RG) layer of two persistent-sodium half-center
neurons (RG-E, RG-F) whose mutual inhibition is routed through dedicated
interneurons (InE, InF); a pattern-formation (PF) layer of four
phase-window cells (E1, E2, F1, F2) whose cross-reciprocal inhibition
is likewise routed through interneurons (PF-IN-E, PF-IN-F); one
motoneuron (MN) pool per muscle (46 per side, 92 total) with a full
proprioceptor complement of Ia (length-velocity), II (length), and Ib
(force) afferent neurons per pool; Ia inhibitory interneurons (IaIN),
Renshaw cells (RC), and Ib load-sharing interneurons; heel and toe
contact mechanosensor interneurons; and commissural interneurons (c1,
V3) coordinating the two sides. Shared descending cells provide drive
(DRIVE, an MLR surrogate), posture tone, and a balance family (COM
anterior-posterior, lateral, and trunk-orientation controllers). The
standing posture is not hard-coded: it is solved once by static
optimization at the model keyframe and injected as per-motoneuron bias
currents.

\subsection{Rhythm Generation: Persistent-Sodium Half-Centers}
\label{sec:spinal-cpg-nap}

The RG half-centers are conditional bursters with an intrinsic
persistent sodium (NaP) current, following the mammalian CPG models of
Deng et al.~\citep{deng2022}, Shinohara et al.~\citep{shinohara2025},
and Rybak et al.~\citep{rybak2024}: each RG neuron carries a
voltage-gated inward current
$I_{\mathrm{NaP}} = g_{\mathrm{Na}}\,m_\infty(V)\,h\,(E_{\mathrm{Na}}-V)$
whose slow inactivation gate $h$ terminates bursts intrinsic to the
neuron, rather than relying on circuit-level adaptation. We
parameterize the SNS toolbox persistent-sodium neuron class
\citep{nourse2023} on the network's 0--5~mV operating range and hold
the $h$-gate time constant fixed at $\tau_h = 350$~ms, the semantics
used by the AnimatLab and Simulink realizations of this circuit
family \citep{deng2022}: with the toolbox's stock voltage-dependent
$\tau_h(V)$ the isolated half-centers oscillate an order of magnitude
too fast (50--120~ms period, measured in this work), whereas fixed
$\tau_h$ yields a $\sim$1.2~s cycle at $\tau_h = 350$~ms with an
approximately linear $\tau_h$-to-period scaling that serves as the
frequency knob during tuning. The mutual inhibition between RG-E and
RG-F is routed through the interneurons InE and InF (laminated
organization; \citealp{shevtsova2026}), with no direct half-center
synapse. A weak mutual excitation between the two half-centers
($G_W$, \citealp{deng2022}) raises the inhibited neuron's equilibrium
potential and enables escape-mode transitions; neuromodulation of this
conductance is the second speed-control pathway. Left-right
coordination follows \citet{shinohara2025}: each flexor half-center
inhibits its contralateral counterpart through a c1 commissural
interneuron, while each extensor half-center \emph{excites} its
contralateral counterpart through a V3 commissural interneuron
\citep{talpalar2013, zhang2022}, which additionally reinforces the
contralateral extensor motoneuron groups (crossed-extensor support).

\subsection{Pattern Formation and Motoneuron Output}
\label{sec:spinal-cpg-pf}

The PF layer distributes the rhythm in four staggered phase windows per
side — early stance (E1), push-off (E2), early swing (F1), and late
swing (F2) — set by per-cell membrane time constants, with
cross-reciprocal inhibition between conflicting windows routed through
the PF-IN interneurons. The PF-to-motoneuron weights form a
per-functional-group table $W_{\mathrm{PF}}$ whose entries were
back-solved from OpenSim static-optimization activations of the
subject walking trial (ridge-regularized least squares with
physiological bounds); biarticular muscles receive input from the
tables of both of their functional groups (primary at full weight,
secondary at half). This yields the phase-and-magnitude separation that
a half-center alone cannot produce \citep{rybak2006, mccrea2008}.

\subsection{Sensory Feedback Architecture}
\label{sec:spinal-cpg-sensory}

Sensory feedback reaches the rhythm and pattern layers directly, per
the state-dependent control scheme of Rybak et
al.~\citep{rybak2025}. Extensor force feedback is positive: each
extensor muscle's Ib afferent excites its ipsilateral extensor centers
(PF-E, RG-E, and the RG-layer interneuron InE), reinforcing stance and
weight support \citep{gossard1994, pearson2000, dominguez2020}; at
rest the same afferents are inhibitory (autogenic reflex reversal).
Flexor length-velocity feedback excites the flexor centers (PF-F,
RG-F, InF) through the Ia and II afferents of every flexor-side
muscle, promoting the extension-to-flexion transition
\citep{perreault2011, rybak2025}, and additionally inhibits the
contralateral flexor half-center, coupling phase transitions across
legs. Foot mechanosensors (heel and toe contact forces) ride the same
extensor pathway as the Ib afferents. Reciprocal inhibition of
antagonist motoneurons is routed through Ia inhibitory interneurons
that are phase-gated by the swing window and disinhibited by Renshaw
cells \citep{hultborn1971}; Renshaw cells additionally provide mutual
recurrent inhibition between antagonist pools. Presynaptic phase and
speed gating of the afferent gains follows the modulation described by
\citet{pearson1998} and \citet{jankowska2010}.

\subsection{Tuning Methodology: Staged Curriculum}
\label{sec:spinal-cpg-tuning}

% trial counts, rhythm-gate + small-gain-entry rules, contact-event drive
The network is tuned by a three-stage curriculum mirroring the
classical deafferentation hierarchy. Stage 1 tunes the rhythm core
(drive, $\tau_h$, descending split, RG-to-PF gain) in deafferented,
suspended-air stepping against an objective rewarding rhythmic cycles
and swing-knee flexion depth. Stage 2 reintroduces the afferented
preparation in air, adding the per-muscle afferent-to-central gains
and the heel and toe mechanosensor gains. Stage 3 adds ground contact
with the full reflex repertoire (stance-Ib group gains, the IaIN
pathway, contralateral flexor inhibition, V3-to-extensor
reinforcement, an ankle plantarflexor tone trim, and a contact-event
drive described below), optimized against a kinematic score comparing
cycle-normalized hip/knee/ankle trajectories, peak flexion, range,
duty, and cadence to the OpenSim reference cycle. Each stage is seeded
by the previous stage's winner with every not-yet-searched gain pinned
at zero, so trial 0 of each stage reproduces the previous winner
exactly and each stage's topology is a strict superset of the
previous; stages run 25--60 trials of tree-structured Parzen
estimation and are resumable.

Two objective-design rules proved necessary. First, the air objective
must be rhythm-gated: without an explicit minimum of three extensor
bursts, the optimizer exploits a static deep-flexion pose whose
knee-depth term alone outranks slow genuine rhythms --- the first
stage-2 campaign was won by such a pose, its score equal to the
knee-depth term with zero extensor bursts. Second, sensory gains new
to a stage must enter the search at small ranges: sampled at full
strength they extinguish the inherited rhythm in every trial, leaving
the optimizer no gradient back to oscillation.

The stage-3 contact-event drive implements the loading-edge trigger
motivated by the contact-driven rhythm generator of the AnimatLab
walker family and by the reset-to-extension reflex of
\citet{conway1987}: the heel and toe contact ports, which otherwise
carry the tonic contact level, additionally receive a brief current
transient at the loading edge (heel strike: reinforcing the extensor
half-center and suppressing the flexor) and an opposite transient at
the unloading edge (toe-off: releasing the extensor center), decaying
with a 0.25-s time constant. Its gain is searched in stage 3.

The ground-stage objective itself was rebuilt after a figure audit
invalidated its first form: each leg's mean gait cycle is cut at that
foot's own ground-contact loading onsets (per-foot heel-plus-toe
normal force, logged every step) and compared to that leg's own

phase lag — plus interleg antiphase lag, cadence and cycle-to-cycle
periodicity errors, per-foot contact-duty error, double-support
error, and guards that fail any foot which never loads or never
cycles (a frozen leg is scored from its walk-window statistics so
that staying still cannot dodge its penalties). The pelvis height the
support rig anchors to is searched per trial, as is a swing-phase
ankle plantarflexor inhibition providing swing dorsiflexion.

\section{Spinal Circuit Simulation Results}
\label{sec:results-cpg}

\subsection{Intrinsic Rhythm: Deafferented Air-Stepping}
\label{sec:results-cpg-air}

Before any tuning, the persistent-sodium architecture produces
physiologically structured air-stepping at default gains (drive
2.5~nA, $\tau_h = 350$~ms): five extensor-burst onsets (four complete
cycle intervals) in the 10-s walk window, a 2.108~s period (0.47~Hz,
the air-stepping regime of \citealp{ivanenko2002}), a cycle-mean
extensor duty of 0.691, peak knee flexion of $-106.1^\circ$, and hip
excursions of $-19.9^\circ$ to $+50.0^\circ$
(Figure~\ref{fig:nap-air}). Most significantly, the
extensor duty — the residual that never exceeded 0.34 across all prior
tuning of the circuit-level-adaptation architecture — falls in the
physiological range immediately, because burst duration is set by the
intrinsic NaP inactivation gate rather than by tuned feedback
strengths. At zero drive the network settles into a tonic extensor
state held by the posture bias (a plateau potential), providing
extensor tone for quiet standing.

\subsection{Plant Fidelity: Force Capacity and Torque Budget}
\label{sec:results-cpg-plant}

An audit of the patched simulation plant verified that the eight
actuators shipped at 1~N by the converter (bilateral erector spinae,
internal and external obliques, and extensor hallucis) are restored
exactly to their OpenSim peak forces. Of the 86 non-pruned active
actuators, 78 are within 15\% of the OpenSim source value after the
converter's range-of-motion normalization; eight exceed that tolerance,
with the largest differences in gluteal paths. Comparing the moment arms of every muscle (central
differences with path followers re-projected, the OpenSim definition)
against the inverse-dynamics joint demand of the reference walk yields
torque budgets of approximately 3.4/2.8/2.1/1.4$\times$ at hip, knee,
trunk, and ankle; the ankle is the tightest. Driving the MuJoCo
muscles with identical kinematics and OpenSim static-optimization
activations reproduces OpenSim Thelen muscle forces at $R^2 =
0.87$--$0.98$ on ten of twelve benchmark muscles with no phase lag;
the rigid-tendon conversion differs in shape (compliance) rather than
timing (Figure~\ref{fig:muscle-force-compare}).

\section{Architectural Lessons from the Spinal Circuit}
\label{sec:disc-cpg}

\subsection{Burst Termination Belongs Inside the Neuron}

The single largest behavioral change in this work came not from tuning
but from moving burst termination from a circuit-level adaptation loop
into an intrinsic persistent-sodium gate. Every prior tuning campaign
on the adaptation-loop architecture left the extensor duty below 0.35
against a physiological reference of 0.61; the persistent-sodium
half-centers produce 0.69 before any search begins. This is
consistent with the operating-regime analysis of Rybak et
al.~\citep{rybak2024, rybak2025}: at slow, sensory-informed gaits the
rhythm generator operates in state-machine and flexor-driven regimes
in which phase durations are set by intrinsic burst dynamics and
sensory inputs rather than by mutual-inhibition strength — so the
mechanism that terminates a burst is the mechanism that sets the duty
cycle. Attempts to substitute circuit-level weak excitation without
any slow intrinsic variable converged to tonic fixed points in our
hands, confirming that some slow gate — intrinsic (the NaP $h$-gate)
or circuit-level (an adaptation loop) — is a structural requirement
for half-center oscillation in non-spiking networks.

\subsection{Sensory Feedback Closes the Loop at the Rhythm Layer}

The per-muscle afferent-to-central projections implement the
force-to-extension and length-to-flexion pathways identified in cat
and rat preparations \citep{gossard1994, conway1987, perreault2011,
dominguez2020} and in the computational model of \citet{rybak2025},
with the flexor afferents additionally gating the contralateral
half-center. Placing these projections inside the RG layer — rather
than at the pattern-formation or motoneuron level — follows the locus
rule articulated by \citet{rybak2015}: only perturbations reaching the
rhythm layer can reset cycle phase. The laminated organization of all
mutual inhibition (through InE/InF, PF-IN, c1, and IaIN interneurons
rather than direct synapses) reproduces the genetically identified
connectivity of the mammalian locomotor network
\citep{shevtsova2026, talpalar2013, zhang2022} and was retained even
where a direct synapse would be functionally equivalent at a single
operating point.

\subsection{Scalar Tuning Converges; the Remaining Gaps Are
Architectural}

Both post-fix search fronts plateaued. In the air stage the
deafferented seed remained unbeaten, with the best afferented trial
tying it at gains an order of magnitude below range; on the ground the
front is flat within three score points across mechanistically
different winners --- one leaning on the contact-event drive at
mid-range gain, the eventual winner on elevated descending drive with
the contact pathway nearly off. Two lessons follow. First, reward-
shaped kinematic objectives must gate the phenomenon they presuppose:
an ungated rhythm reward is maximized by the best static pose, and the
failure is invisible in the score itself (it was caught only by
re-running the winner and counting bursts). Second, once the rhythm
is intrinsic, feedback-gain search saturates: under the corrected
both-leg objective the entire converged front shares a single
architectural failure — the stance leg never releases into swing
under load (left-foot duty 1.0, no countable cycles on that side) —
which no combination of the twenty searched gains, including the
contact-event drive, pelvis height, and swing dorsiflexion,
unlocked in 80 trials. The identified next mechanism is the
swing-release side of the contact pathway: a load-DECREASING
(toe-off/force-fall) trigger that actively terminates the stance
half-center rather than the loading-edge reinforcement alone, i.e.,
sensory phase reset entering the rhythm layer proper, for which the
edge-triggered drive built here is the vehicle; a data-driven
synergy pattern layer remains the second lever.

\subsection{Limitations and Remaining Gaps}

Three classes of limitation remain. First, the fixed $\tau_h$ used
here matches the AnimatLab/Simulink semantics of this circuit family
\citep{deng2022} but is a simplification of voltage-dependent gate
kinetics measured in mammalian neurons. Second, the plant's
rigid-tendon muscles reproduce activation-driven force faithfully but
omit series-elastic smoothing, which appears as a shape difference in
compliant-tendon muscles such as soleus. % >>> [ZCODE 2026-09-20] EB475WS4 — \fillme slot FILLED (corrected
% evening version: frozen-left characterization replaces the first
% fast-and-shallow numbers)
Third, the corrected both-leg objective exposes the true state of the
ground gait: both feet load, the right leg steps with dorsiflexed
ankles, but the left leg never unloads (duty 1.0, no cycles), and
every member of the 80-trial search front shares this frozen-stance
family. The residuals against the reference walk (contralateral duty
0.60, knee excursion $70^\circ$, period $1.23$~s) are attributable to
the missing stance-to-swing release pathway rather than to feedback
gains; the next levers are a force-fall (toe-off) trigger into the
rhythm generator, a synergy-based pattern layer, and a
compliant-tendon muscle model.

A synergy analysis of the
back-solved activations (non-negative matrix factorization) indicates
four to five coordinated muscle groups per side with task-based rather
than joint-based composition — supporting the current four-window
pattern layer as a phase decomposition, while suggesting that a
data-driven synergy layer is the natural generalization.
