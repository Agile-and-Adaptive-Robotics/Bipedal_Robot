\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:overall}\label{ch:futurework}
\graphicspath{{../Figures/70-overall/}{figs/70-overall/}}

This chapter reads the preceding studies as one program, states what that program established, and delimits the work the results open. The overview leads with the design factors that make improved first-pass robot design achievable, then attributes them to the developments behind them, ordered from the actuator outward to the whole body. The future-work subsections that follow preserve that order, and each is written for the researchers who will take it up.

\section{The Work as a Whole}\label{sec:foundation}

The outcome that organizes this discussion is that improved first-pass design of a BPA-actuated biomimetic leg is now achievable: a designer can predict actuator force, joint torque, and range of motion accurately enough to commit to an actuator set, a resting length, and a route before the hardware exists. At the center of that outcome are the identified design factors that remove the rigid-body assumptions from the torque model (Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}): a constant length offset $\chi_{0}$, effective stiffnesses $\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$ that lump the series compliance of the attachment brackets, fixtures, and artificial tendons into the bracket frame's principal directions, and a wrapping-loss factor $\chi_{3}$ that removes usable actuator length as an extensor bends over the joint. The terms were identified on the pinned knee, and they hold up beyond it: carried unchanged to the biomimetic knee with its reinforced frame, the corrected model explains the bulk of the measured torque variance there as well, cutting the biomimetic extensor's fraction of variance unexplained from 4.49 to 0.67 with the shared terms (Section~\ref{sec:ongoing}). Whether the set is complete is itself now an experimental question: a fourth term $\chi_{4}$, a stiffness acting along the force path independently of force orientation and presently absorbed by the stiffness pair ($\chi_{1}$ and $\chi_{2}$), is arguably the next candidate, and the identification machinery delivered here is the instrument for settling it.

Improved BPA characterization is the first enabler (Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles}). The maximum isometric force of Festo BPAs was shown to depend strongly on resting length, a previously unreported characteristic that corrects the manufacturer's tool at exactly the short lengths a leg design requires, and a nondimensional force surface predicts isometric force from relative strain and relative pressure with only three diameter-dependent coefficients. Because resting length is a primary degree of freedom in actuator selection, this model is what makes that selection rational. The quasi-static surface is complemented at the dynamic end by the state-space, optimization-based model of pressure and force dynamics in pulse-modulated BPAs \citep{elzein_pressure_2025}, which supplies the actuator behavior that the isometric maps cannot.

Attachment and routing optimization is the second enabler, and it is tiered. The placement optimization of Morrow, Bolen, and Hunt found artificial muscle attachment locations that reproduce human torque profiles and ranges of motion about the trunk and leg joints \citep{morrow_optimization_2020}, converting the earlier finding that transplanted human attachment locations do not transfer directly to a BPA-actuated robot \citep{bolen_bipedal_2019, bolen_determination_2019} into a usable design tool; the route redesigns of Section~\ref{sec:ongoing} rerun that machinery under the corrected torque model. At the route-selection tier, with two \qty{20}{\mm} BPAs per route and routes chosen with the actuators' bending in mind, the redesigned flexor and extensor each meet or exceed the magnitude of their human-scaled torque targets across the full knee range of motion, and the cam-profile extensor converts the direct route's thin margin into genuine design headroom (Section~\ref{sec:route_redesign_results}). Stated as a design program, the generalization is one or more BPAs replacing one or more human muscles across one or more joints, serving the two degrees of freedom that matter most at each joint, with routes grown outward from positions close to the bone and held to the spatial constraints of the candidate actuators and of the BPAs already mounted; the first whole-body pass over the robot's actuator set, with its successes and its failures, is reported in Section~\ref{sec:ongoing}. Where the attachments are fixed and only the route and the tendon length can change, the cam-shape solver applies: the tibial-side route points trace a designed cam profile, trading biomimicry for torque-profile optimization and reduced component forces.

The Sensory Afferent Database (SADb) is the third enabler (Chapter~\ref{ch:database}). It organizes the sensory-afferent literature into a structured afferent resource \citep{bolen_sensory_2023}: of the 561 papers retained after the audit and the prune, 523 carry structured curation notes, 86 carry an afferent classification, and 258 carry notes translating each paper toward robot or simulation use, and the full audited corpus of 943 papers, including the 382 pruned records preserved under an archived flag, remains reconstructible in the packaged tool. The toolset matters as much as the corpus. Its detailed table drills from any filter down to a paper's full notes; its pivot view counts the corpus along any two dimensions at once; its bubble map draws every paper as a neuron, soma sized by citations, with citation links rendered as excitatory and inhibitory synapses in the laboratory's convention and a focus mode that walks the citation neighborhood; and the monthly skimmer--curator workflow sweeps the recent literature for candidate records and routes every one of them to a human ruling. The evaluation of AI-assisted literature triage established the division of labor that makes expansion affordable \citep{bolen_using_2024}: the machine drafts, and the human rules.

The fourth enabler is the set of novel neuromechanical simulation tools (Chapter~\ref{ch:sns}): the audited OpenSim-to-MuJoCo conversion of the robot's musculoskeletal model, the force interface that computes actuator forces inside the loop from the validated BPA model, the two-level spinal networks built in SNS-Toolbox, the Simulink SNS library for assembly in a controls-engineering environment, the cross-engine verification harness, and the spiking-neuron mirrors that test the coding assumption. On these tools the controller program has matured from assembly into use. Production walking configurations have been tuned and evaluated in full-body ground simulations, the first pathway-deletion and drive-stimulus experiments have been run, the gait-validation sweep has measured how far the tuned networks remain from the diversity of human reference locomotion, and the contact-driven rhythm core has been shown to transfer between independent implementations with exact period agreement and trace correlations above 0.9999. In hardware, the companion balance-platform studies demonstrated that a BPA-actuated limb holds itself upright under pseudo-vestibular inertial feedback \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. What remains unfinished is equally specific, and it organizes the future work below: the balance and weight-bearing layers that unsupported bilateral walking requires, wider transfer across reference cycles, the supraspinal sensorium, and embedded real-time execution.

\section{Future Work}

The subsections that follow are options for the researchers who take this program up, ordered from the actuator outward to the whole body. Each draws directly on the delivered models, the database, and the simulation tools.

\subsection{Expanding the BPA Force Characterization}\label{sec:fw_bpa}

The isometric characterization of Chapter~\ref{ch:muscles} leaves one actuator curve unidentified and one regime unmeasured, and both are tractable next experiments. The unidentified curve is the maximum-contraction function: $\varepsilon_{620}$ could not be pinned to a predictive relationship with resting length, so each actuator's free contraction is recorded individually before use. Controlled tests designed for that relationship, across multiple product batches of the \qtylist{10;20;40}{\mm} diameters, would either produce general fit surfaces or establish batch variation as the binding uncertainty. The \qty{20}{\mm} actuators characterized here came from a single product batch, so batch-to-batch variation is unquantified and those fits in particular carry that limitation.

The unmeasured regime is everything off the isometric plateau. A biological muscle model separates active force from passive force and couples both to activation and contraction dynamics \citep{thelen_adjustment_2003, millard_flexing_2013}, and Appendix~\ref{app:muscle_calcs}, which collects the Thelen and Millard formulations used as torque targets in this work, is the natural home for a BPA counterpart: extending it with the remaining equations of those models, the Gaussian active-force shape factors, the activation and deactivation time constants, and the tendon force curves, would let the BPA model be decomposed into active pressurized-braid force, passive resistance of the unpressurized actuator and sleeve, and contraction dynamics. Isokinetic and isotonic or isobaric characterizations could then be assimilated into the same framework rather than fitted separately, with the pressure--force dynamics model of \citet{elzein_pressure_2025} supplying the actuator-side dynamics.

Two experimental programs would complete the mechanical picture. Instron tensile testing of actuators with their Onyx and aluminum end caps, unpressurized and pressurized, would measure the end-effect region that the characterization identifies as the mechanism behind the resting-length dependence of maximum force. A finite-element program sketched from the construction disclosed in Festo's patent literature would give the actuator a first-principles model to check the empirical surfaces against \citep{festo_2026}: a symmetry half-model of the actuator, a half circle fixed at the outer and inner diameter at one end with the diameter free to expand at the other and an optional lateral force or fixity, assembled from thin-shell or membrane layers in inter-layer contact, with the two handed aramid weaves of the braided sleeve represented as beam or truss elements.

\subsection{Expanding Attachment and Routing Design}\label{sec:fw_routing}

Each routing tier of Section~\ref{sec:foundation} leaves a named next step. The knee's flexor redesign assumes both actuators share one attachment route; making the mirrored pair explicit is the next refinement. The bi-pulley sweep showed that reeved routes do not yet fit the anatomical corridors, where geometry rather than torque was the binding constraint, so clearance-relaxed attachment sites are where that freedom should pay. The whole-body pass needs its route-builder failure fixed and either larger actuators or relaxed corridors on the two routes that satisfy geometry but fall short of torque.

The design element this program has so far declined is a patella. The torque model wraps the extensor BPA over the joint surface and accounts for the loss with $\chi_{3}$, which was easier than adding a patella, deterministically designing its location as a function of knee angle, and accounting for friction (Chapter~\ref{ch:torque}). A designed sesamoid is the biomimetic answer: an artificial patella, its location optimized over the range of motion the way route points now are, would buy back moment arm at high flexion and shield the actuator from the joint, at the cost of a friction model and a contact schedule the optimizer must respect. The cam-profile solver is the proof that a moving routing surface can be a design variable; a patella is its biomimetic limit case.

\subsection{Expanding the Sensory Afferent Database}\label{sec:fw_database}

The database's growth now has two engines: the monthly skimmer--curator workflow that sweeps the recent literature, and machine-assisted drafting under human ruling. The next step the corpus itself suggests is prompt-driven passes over the existing records, designed to discover the notes and rules latent in the corpus, candidate pathway records, missing cross-links, controlled-vocabulary amendments, and gaps in the afferent classification, and to draft entries for the flagged stubs and for the vocabulary gaps that the extensions of Section~\ref{sec:fw_senses} will need first, vestibular and visual preparations among them. Two constraints from the curation rules carry over unchanged. The passes must be economical and their accuracy measured against human rulings rather than assumed, and a human still rules on every write; the triage evaluation found acceleration with substantial oversight required \citep{bolen_using_2024}, and nothing in the pipeline removes the evaluator. What the passes buy is coverage at the corpus's edges, at a cost a laboratory can afford.

\subsection{Closing the Loop in Simulation}\label{sec:fw_pipeline}\label{sec:fw_cpg}

The simulation laboratory of Chapter~\ref{ch:sns} is assembled, audited, and in active use. The converted robot model carries all of its muscle-tendon actuators with audited geometry, and the BPA force interface computes actuator forces from the validated model inside the loop; both are documented as complete in the chapter's methods (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}). The 410-neuron SNS-Toolbox instantiation of the spinal network, with per-leg rhythm-generator half-centers, phase-shifted pattern-formation groups, motoneuron pools for every actuator, and Ia, Ib, and II afferent populations, couples to the converted model at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, and production walking configurations have been tuned against a walking reference and evaluated on the ground (Section~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}). A drive-weaning study has since produced a feedback-sustained bilateral configuration whose stepping persists with the hand-drive withdrawn entirely, still in place under the support rig, and the robot-side model progresses under its harness at weaned drive (Section~\ref{sec:sns_controller_comparison}). What those same evaluations localize as missing defines the work remaining here: the balance and weight-bearing layers that unsupported, bilateral walking requires; transfer from a single tuning reference to the diversity of the locomotion reference library; and the systematic verification series that follows.

The verification series comes first because it is cheap and decisive. The circuits' signs, gains, and phase structure are checked against the experimental literature with the stimulus protocols the physiology itself uses: a tonic stimulus applied at the motoneuron, pattern-formation, and rhythm-generator layers, with the resulting outputs compared against the tonic central and sensory stimulation that facilitates involuntary air-stepping in humans \citep{selionov_tonic_2009} and the deletion and stimulation phenomena reproduced by the two-level CPG models and their neuromechanical implementations \citep{rybak_modelling_2006, markin_afferent_2010, shevtsova_modeling_2016}. The first of these tests is complete at the rhythm-generator level, where tonic drive evokes an approximately \qty{0.9}{\second} antiphase cycle in the isolated network (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}); the motoneuron-level and pattern-formation-level series remain to be run, together with the motoneuron skipping-deletion phenomenon of the reference models.

The laboratory's hypothesis-testing program is stimulus, deletion, and addition. The stimulus and deletion families have their first sweeps (Section~\ref{sec:walker_variant_results}). Pathway addition is their complement and the one the tools have not yet exercised: adding a candidate circuit to a tuned configuration, a commissural class, a sensory gain, or a modulatory layer, and re-tuning, which turns connectome hypotheses into falsifiable experiments. The closed-loop comparison of spiking and non-spiking controllers of the same topology belongs to the same program, with the spiking mirror's untuned bilateral ground gait as the standing lead (Section~\ref{sec:disc_spiking}).

The tools also compose across programs, and the composition is now cheap enough to be a standard workflow. A full-body OpenSim model with arms and trunk, of the kind MyoSuite distributes \citep{caggiano_myosuite_2022}, can be brought into the SNS-Simscape library for graphical assembly and inspection of the controller, and the same network imported back into SNS-Toolbox on MuJoCo \citep{nourse_sns_2023} for the speed that tuning and long evaluations require. A controller built in that loop inherits the conversion audit, the cross-engine checks, and the deletion apparatus of Chapter~\ref{ch:sns} without rebuilding any of them.

\subsection{Extending the Sensorium}\label{sec:fw_senses}

Once stepping is stable, three supraspinal contributions will be added, and a fourth is in line behind them. First, vestibular feedback will be simulated with IMU-like sensing of body orientation and angular velocity, extending the balance-platform work, in which an inverted pendulum with pseudo-vestibular IMU feedback maintained balance on an oscillating platform \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024} and analytic neural networks reproduced human balance dynamics \citep{hilts_dynamic_2019}. Second, ocular feedback will be modeled to investigate how visual stabilization cues can help reset IMU drift, a well-known practical problem for inertial sensing on robots, by treating visual measurements of the horizon and surroundings as a low-frequency correction to the vestibular estimate. Third, a cerebellar layer will be explored as an adaptive gateway between higher-level locomotion commands and the CPG: a structure that can gate between gaits or movement patterns (e.g., walking, running, and turning) and tune the phase relationships among them, in line with the cerebellum's established role in adaptation and coordination. Multilevel spinal-plus-cerebellar synthetic nervous systems have already been demonstrated for insect-like legs \citep{grummer_multilevel_2024}, and hierarchical cortico-cerebellar-spinal models have been proposed for adaptive step-length control in bipedal locomotion \citep{chen_corticocerebellar_2025}. The fourth addition, basal-ganglia circuits realizing the inhibitory and excititory gating that selects among actions and locomotor modes, would give the controller a substrate for choosing between behaviors rather than only executing one. The database already holds the entry points for this program: the human balance and posture cluster and the multisensory recalibration cluster are curated and connected to the locomotor core through the citation graph (Chapter~\ref{ch:database}), and the proprioceptive--vestibular interaction literature will guide how the signals are weighted \citep{akay_relative_2021}.

\subsection{Real-Time Hardware Realization}\label{sec:fw_hardware}

The final stage returns to hardware, and its near version is a step-by-step integration problem for which the laboratory's existing equipment already stocks the parts. The stack is fixed: Teensy-class microcontrollers handle the low-latency per-joint loops (valve control, pressure sensing, and contact sensing), and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin serves as the companion computer for SNS network evaluation and activation computation. The integration steps then run in order. Execute the SNS-Toolbox network in real time on the treadmill biped. Close feedback from the sensors the lab already uses: the joint encoders, the actuator pressure sensors, the liquid-wire strain gauges, the pressure manifolds, and the IMUs. Compute the afferent signals from those streams exactly as the simulation tools compute them, Ia length and velocity and II length from the encoders, and Ib force from pressure-based force estimates checked against the strain gauges. Tune computer-in-the-loop, with the embedded stack executing the network against the simulated body before it executes against the physical one, so that gain changes are tested at simulation speed and validated on hardware only when they survive. Keep the serial connection to the host computer for data logging and high-level commands only (e.g., run, walk, or stimulate a named neural pathway), so the control loop stays fully embedded. The wiring schematics, the neural-pathway schematics, and the step-by-step commissioning procedure that this integration produces belong in a hardware appendix rather than in this chapter; the connectome figures of Appendix~\ref{app:neuron_equations} already serve as the pathway schematics for the spinal network.

The precedent and the margins both support feasibility. Walking control of a physical musculoskeletal biped with McKibben muscles has been demonstrated with spring-loaded inverted-pendulum controllers \citep{li_controller_2024}, and the AARL's quadruped has already traveled part of this road: a MuJoCo model of the BPA quadruped was coupled to SNS-Toolbox, produced closed-loop coordinated stepping, and was transferred to hardware with potentiometer and pressure feedback on a treadmill \citep{lutz_mechanical_2025}; the present program extends that approach from the quadruped to a leg built on human anatomy. An actuation-level design lever also remains open: the BPA's limited contraction can force long musculotendon routings, a burden visible in the extensor redesign bounds of Appendix~\ref{app:model_geometry} and encountered in the author's master's routing work \citep{bolen_determination_2019}. A block-and-tackle ``reverse pulley'' arrangement, demonstrated on the AARL's self-balancing inverted-pendulum platform, addresses this by trading transmitted force for travel: paired BPAs pulled through compound pulleys on a tendon coupled to the limb, increasing range of motion at the cost of reduced transmitted force and increased reaction force on the structure \citep{bolen_balance_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_2024, bolen_selfbalancing_srs_2024}. Because joint torque is computed in the body frame, configurations that turn that reaction force to advantage may exist, making the arrangement a candidate for joints whose optimized routes demand impractically long actuators.

Because the static force and torque models of Chapters~\ref{ch:muscles} and~\ref{ch:torque} are computationally inexpensive lookup-table candidates, they will serve as the feedforward model inside the real-time controller, while the dynamic pressure--force behavior under pulse-modulated control will draw on the state-space model of \citet{elzein_pressure_2025}. Embedded execution of the complete CPG-plus-reflex network on this stack is the intended demonstration of the dissertation project: a robot that walks with human-like biomechanics under a biologically grounded neural controller, and whose nervous system, unlike a living animal's, can be instrumented, stimulated, and lesioned at will.

\subsection{Toward Dynamic Whole-Body Maneuvers}\label{sec:fw_kickflip}

Beyond steady walking, the long-term goal of this line of research is dynamic, whole-body maneuvering: transitions between gaits, reactive balancing, and ultimately ballistic maneuvers that require coordinating the full actuator set through brief, precisely timed activation bursts. The author's aspiration for this program is a robot that can perform a kickflip, a maneuver that, like its skateboard namesake, demands explosive coordinated extension, precise timing, aerial body orientation, and controlled landing. It is exactly the kind of behavior that exercises every layer of the architecture described above, from contact receptors and spinal pattern generation to vestibular orientation sensing and cerebellar coordination, and it would demonstrate that biomimetic actuation and neuromechanical control scale from steady locomotion to dynamic whole-body movement.

These subsections are options, not prerequisites; each can be pursued on the delivered framework independently of the others. What they all draw on, the actuator and torque models, the routing machinery, the afferent database, and the simulation laboratory, is consolidated in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.
