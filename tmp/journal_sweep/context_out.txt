FILE 15-background

--- L8 [in baseline (grandfathered?)]
Biomimetic robots offer many potential applications, ranging from assisting in factories and environmental exploration to serving as physical models of human movement. By developing high-fidelity biomimetic robots, we can perform tests that help improve our understanding of both biomechanics and the underlying neuromechanical systems that control movement \citep{shin_constructive_2018, asano_musculoskeletal_2019, ijspeert_amphibious_2020, goldsmith_investigating_2021, hunt_development_2017, schilling_adaptive_2022}. A few such biomimetic robots exist, such as the impressive Kengoro and Kenshiro robots of \citet{asano_design_2017} and \citet{asano_musculoskeletal_2019}, which have proportionally human bodies, anatomically correct musculotendon arrangements, rolling-sliding joints, and joint ranges and output power comparable with human-measured values; their artificial muscles are implemented with brushless DC motors, wires as tendons, gears, and integrated tension measurement. Kengoro, the more recent of the two, stands \qty{1670}{\mm} tall, weighs \qty{56.5}{\kg}, and carries 174 degrees of freedom when the end effectors are included \citep{asano_design_2017}. A dog-like robot developed by \citet{aschenbeck_design_2006} and actuated with Festo BPAs was designed from a planar kinematic model scaled to an adult greyhound, with three sagittal degrees of freedom per leg driven by antagonistic BPA pairs; its front-leg pair alone lifted a \qty{13.5}{\kg} payload, almost double the complete robot's predicted mass. That platform was used to show how central pattern generators provide robust locomotion patterns that are adaptive to perturbations in ground speed \citep{hunt_development_2017}.

--- L66 [in baseline (grandfathered?)]
Previous studies have generated empirical models of the force--length--pressure relationship of BPAs. \citet{sarosi_comparative_2017} discuss several high-fidelity BPA force models. They present a static force model with a 21-coefficient polynomial function for a Festo MAS-20-200N (i.e., $\diameter$\qty{20}{\mm}, $l_0=\qty{200}{\mm}$) with an impressive $\text{R}^2=0.9994$, as well as S\'arosi's static force model for a Festo DMSP-20-400N-RM-RM (i.e., $\diameter$\qty{20}{\mm}, $l_0=\qty{400}{\mm}$), with six coefficients and a $\text{R}^2=0.9995$ \citep{sarosi_new_2012, sarosi_investigation_2013}. Martens and Boblan present an even more accurate model, in terms of absolute error, using five coefficients for a DMSP-10-250 (i.e., $\diameter$\qty{10}{\mm}, $l_0=\qty{250}{\mm}$) and a DMSP-20-300 (i.e., $\diameter$\qty{20}{\mm}, $l_0=\qty{300}{\mm}$) \citep{martens_modeling_2017}. However, as demonstrated in this work, different resting lengths produce different force--length curves, preventing the use of these single-length models for designing systems with different resting lengths.

--- L111 [in baseline (grandfathered?)]
Designing a biomimetic leg requires a quantitative benchmark of human muscle--joint behavior. This dissertation uses OpenSim \citep{delp_opensim_2007, seth_opensim_2011, seth_opensim_2018} and the Gait2392 model, which descends from the interactive lower-extremity model of \citet{delp_interactive_1990} and includes musculoskeletal geometry from \citet{hoy_musculoskeletal_1990}, planar knee kinematics from \citet{yamaguchi_planar_1989}, and a lower-limb model with 23 degrees of freedom and 54 musculotendon actuators \citep{arnold_model_2010}. Muscle moment arms, force-generating properties, and functional roles in such models have been characterized extensively \citep{zajac_determining_1989, brand_model_1982, menegaldo_moment_2004, millard_flexing_2013, sherman_what_2013, suderman_moving_2012}, and standard reporting conventions for kinematics and joint mechanics are established by the International Society of Biomechanics \citep{wu_for_1995, wu_isb_2002, wu_isb_2005, derrick_isb_2020}.

--- L328 [in baseline (grandfathered?)]
The presented work is capable of predicting force output for 10 mm and 20 mm muscles with different initial muscle lengths, internal pressure, and contraction amount with minimal initial data collection. However, it remains to be seen if these relationships are maintained over the full lifecycle of the muscles. There are not yet any peer reviewed studies investigating the long-term behavior of these artificial muscles, though the Festo datasheet provides some indication of what can be expected \citep{festo_2026} (p.19). They report that the 40 mm BPA can complete 1 million cycles at \qty{6000}{\N} (maximum rated load), or 10 million cycles at \qty{4000}{\N}. The service life of the other size BPAs is not reported; however, it is mentioned that thermal stressing may reduce the lifespan and they should be inspected every 500,000 cycles for signs of aging \citep{festo_2018} (pp.~10--11). Future research should look at the behavior of these muscles over time.

--- L410 [in baseline (grandfathered?)]
Furthermore, biological muscles can adapt the optimal operating length through structural remodeling (e.g., sarcomere addition or tendon compliance) while BPAs have a fixed geometric configuration. This allows biological systems to self-optimize for the particular tasks the animal is subjected to. In order to re-optimize a robot for new tasks, new actuators would have to be swapped into the system. This distinction highlights a key limitation of BPAs in biomimetic applications, while also reinforcing the importance of accurate nonlinear modeling for control and design.

--- L135 [in baseline (grandfathered?)]
The verification harness is a tool in its own right, and its existence is the chapter's answer to a trust problem: the toolchain spans several simulation engines, and a result that holds in only one software implementation is difficult to trust. Its fundamental demonstration is replication, in two forms; the same rhythm core rebuilt in two engines must reproduce the same rhythm, and the lineage's archived models must reproduce their published behavior in their original engine. The harness is defined around the contact-driven rhythm core of the Walker-2-Layer (W2L) bipedal architecture: four rhythm-generator half-centers, one extensor/flexor pair per leg, plus graded pattern-formation and sensory cells, driven by heel-contact mechanosensory feedback. Each half-center is built from spiking neurons whose persistent sodium current, a slowly inactivating inward current, terminates each burst, so the mutually inhibiting extensor and flexor populations of a pair alternate \citep{deng_neuromechanical_2019}. The same core was implemented twice from a common connectivity specification, once as a NumPy reference integration through the SNS-Toolbox backend and once as native Simulink blocks from the library of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}, and both were driven with an identical protocol: a \qty{12}{\second} run at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, tonic drive with a brief kickoff pulse, and alternating \qty{1}{\Hz} heel-contact trains. Agreement was scored on rhythm-generator burst period, left--right antiphase correlation, burst counts, and full-trace correlation against the NumPy reference. The archived AnimatLab reference models (the bilateral contact-driven model, the modern W2L model, and the Li walker) were additionally re-run headless from their standalone simulators with instrumented charts, so that engine-level agreement and network-level agreement could be separated. The protocol covers the rhythm core and the archived models only; full-network and whole-body behavior are reported as limitations alongside the results in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_results}.

FILE 70-overall

--- L42 [CHANGED/NEW vs baseline]
The simulation laboratory of Chapter~\ref{ch:sns} is assembled, audited, and in active use. The converted robot model carries all of its muscle-tendon actuators with audited geometry, and the BPA force interface computes actuator forces from the validated model inside the loop; both are documented as complete in the chapter's methods (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_sim_methods}). The 410-neuron SNS-Toolbox instantiation of the spinal network, with per-leg rhythm-generator half-centers, phase-shifted pattern-formation groups, motoneuron pools for every actuator, and Ia, Ib, and II afferent populations, couples to the converted model at a \qty{2}{\milli\second} neural step, and production walking configurations have been tuned against a walking reference and evaluated on the ground (Section~\ref{sec:gait_library_results}). A drive-weaning study has since produced a feedback-sustained bilateral configuration whose stepping persists with the hand-drive withdrawn entirely, still in place under the support rig, and the robot-side model progresses under its harness at weaned drive (Section~\ref{sec:sns_controller_comparison}). What those same evaluations localize as missing defines the work remaining here: the balance and weight-bearing layers that unsupported, bilateral walking requires; transfer from a single tuning reference to the diversity of the locomotion reference library; and the systematic verification series that follows.
