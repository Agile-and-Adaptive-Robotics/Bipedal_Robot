ABSTRACT2
Artificial muscles such as braided pnuematic actuators (BPAs) offer many advantages for robotic3
systems, including high durability and and strength-to-weight ratios. However, their use in robotic4
systems are still extremely limited, in part due to their poor characterization, especially with regard5
to their force and length capabilities. In this work, test setups are created to compare forces6
and torques produced by Festo fluidic braided pneumatic actuators (BPAs) with leading models.7
The muscle force setup consists of linear force recordings while the robotic system consists of a8
lower limb model of a bipedal humanoid robot that was designed to achieve biomimetic ranges of9
motion and isometric torque values. Predicted torque values based on developed models are10
compared with recorded torque data to determine the major sources of error in current modeling11
tools. Our analysis of the data has resulted in the development of new equations to calculate force12
as functions of pressure and strain for muscles with diameters of 10 mm and 20 mm. We have13
also developed a novel equation for maximum force in the 10 mm diameter BPA as a function14
of resting length. The improved force calculations reduced the difference between measured15
and calculated torque in the robot, but are unable to explain all the discrepancies. An improved16
model is better able to match the experimental data by including a series spring element in the17
BPA model. Finally, we show that a properly sized BPA can be used to meet or exceed human18
isometric knee torque values. This work demonstrates a greatly improved model that is better able19
to predict joint torques produced by Festo BPAs on biomimetic designs. This will lead to faster20
design processes and the development of biomimetic robots that are able to more accurately21
reproduce the range of motion and isometric torque profiles that exist in the animals they are22
mimicking.23

1 INTRODUCTION
Both academic researchers and the general public are keenly interested in biomimetic robots due to their25
many applications. In the discipline of biomimetic robotics, high fidelity robots can help improve our26
understanding of both biomechanics and the underlying neuromechanical systems that control them (Shin27

Bolen et al. Torque Values About Robotic Knee
et al., 2018; Asano et al., 2019). Experiments can be performed on robots that would not be practical or28
ethical if done on human test subjects. Additionally, modifying robotic platforms is potentially faster and29
cheaper than human observation studies.30
Biomimetic robots that are built with artificial muscles provide opportunities to investigate how muscles31
are controlled to produce desired motions. McKibben style braided pneumatic actuators (BPAs, also called32
Pneumatic Artificial Muscles or PAMs) are a promising method of actuation because they have low weight,33
high force/weight ratio, high efficiency, and a force-length curve that is similar to actual muscle []. [2-334
sentences of artificial muscle robots - Asano, Hosoda, Hunt] Combining these artificial muscles with35
muscle control schemes that have been developed using muscle models in simulation [] can allow for more36
effective investigations of dynamic interactions with the environment than current physics simulators have37
to offer. Unfortunately, these artificial muscles don’t fully match their biological counterpoints, and there38
are few tools or design methodologies for BPA attachment locations on robotic systems.39
Our previous research has worked to bridge this gap through the development of better models and muscle40
placement algorithms. We developed a more accurate force-pressure-length model ofϕ10 mm Festo BPAs41
that incorporates changes that arise from different initial lengths (Hunt et al., 2017). We also investigated42
how the location of muscle attachments affects the torque capabilities of each muscle and compared these43
capabilities with human data (Bolen and Hunt, 2019). We found that if artificial muscles are placed in44
the same locations as human muscles, the robot would not be able to achieve the same range of motion45
or torque that humans achieve. To improve the range of motion and torque produce by the muscles, we46
developed optimization methods that find attachment locations that can produce a torque curve and range47
of motion that match human capabilities (Morrow et al., 2020). However, these methods had not been48
validated on a physical robot body.49
Deviation between calculated and measured joint torques could occur through several mechanisms. Festo50
reports that there can be a 10% deviation from theoretical force due to manufacturing differences ((Festo,51
2022)), and it is unclear how well the previous model (Hunt et al., 2017) holds true at high forces. Bending52
and kinking of the artificial muscle as it wraps around a joint will also reduce the force produced by the53
muscle. Joint friction can cause a decrease in torque, as can non-rigid elements of the artificial leg. For54
these reasons we also hypothesize that the experimentally measured torque values will be lower than what55
is predicted by our theoretical model, and correction factors will need to be incorporated in the modeling56
system.57
This work sets out to fill in these gaps by developing a better model of maximum force produced by58
ϕ10 mm Festo BPAs, and extending this model toϕ20 mm Festo BPAs, which are needed to produce the59
high torques found about the human knee joint. We then built an isometric knee test stand that enables60
us to measure the torque about the knee joint at various configurations. Data collected from the test stand61
is compared with theoretical torque values, and used to develop an improved model that incorporates62
loses due to compliance. We also demonstrate that the improved attachment locations produced by the63
optimization method in previous work are able to produce torques that match or exceed human capabilities64
across a wide range of motion.65

2 METHODS AND MATERIALS
2.1 Overview66
First, we built a jig to measure artificial muscle force as a function of length and pressure. We tested 1067
mm and 20 mm muscles with various initial lengths and at different contraction lengths. This data was68
used to develop an improved model that better predicts maximum muscle force based on initial muscle69
length and current muscle length. We then built a jig to test the torque produced by the artificial muscles70
about a knee joint. With the expectation that measured torque will be less than that predicted by the model,71
the robot muscle arrangement and knee design were simplified to elucidate the variables that most effect72
the results. Two joints were used and tested: a simplified pinned knee joint and a biomimetic 1-DoF knee73
(Steele et al., 2018). Each joint was driven by a single flexor and single extensor muscle. We also varied74
muscle origin/insertion locations, initial length, diameters, and whether or not they used an artificial tendon.75
The results from these tests were compared with three virtual models: (1) the human biomechanical model,76
(2) the conceptual robot model, and (3) the actual robot model.77
2.2 Actuator force testing78
A test jig was made of 80/20 1515 series extruded aluminum (Fig. XX). Artificial muscles were placed79
vertically in the jig. The upper end was attached to an Loadstar S shaped load cell. The lower end was80
attached to an adjustable crossbeam used to change the length of the actuator. Compressed air was supplied81
through the building at 620 kPa (90 PSI) and measured with an MXPXXXX pressure sensor.82
Each BPAlrest was measured as the distance between the hose clamps. This is how Festo defineslrest,83
although in (Hunt et al., 2017) it was measured to also include end cap length.84
We then inflated each BPA toPmax = 620 kPa and measuredϵmax. The BPAs were then deflated, placed85
vertically in the test jig made out of 80/20 1515 series extruded aluminum pieces and fixed between two86
crossmembers. The force sensor was placed between the upper crossmember and the BPA. For120 mm,87
220 mm, 260 mm, 281 mm and 281 mmlrest, a Loadstar S Shaped load cell was used instead of the other88
force sensors previously mentioned. The distance between the crossmembers was adjusted to get different89
amounts ofϵ∗. The BPAs would then be inflated. BPAs withlrest of 120 mm, 220 mm, 260 mm, 281 mm90

values were taken at or nearPmax.F andP data are collected as described in a previous subsection, above.93
