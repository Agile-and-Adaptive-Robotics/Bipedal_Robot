Abstract 6
Artificial muscles such as braided pneumatic actuators (BPAs) offer many advantages for 7
robotic systems, including high durability and strength-to-weight ratios. However, their 8
use in robotic systems is still extremely limited, in part due to their poor force, length, and 9
pressure characterization. In this work, a test setup is created to compare force produced 10
by Festo fluidic BPAs with leading models. Our analysis of the data has resulted in 1) the 11
development of new equations to calculate force as functions of pressure and contraction 12
for Festo BPAs with uninflated diameters of 10mm and 20 mm, and 2) a novel equation for 13
the maximum force in 10 mm and 20 mm diameter Festo BPAs as a function of their resting 14
length. This will lead to faster design processes and the development of new systems such 15
as biomimetic robots that are able to more accurately reproduce the range of motion and 16
isometric torque profiles that exist in the animals they are mimicking.17

1. Introduction 19
Biomimetic robots provide a platform for investigating how mechanical structure and 20
control interact to generate movement, offering complementary insights to simulation- 21
based studies [1–3]. Musculoskeletal humanoids and limb systems driven by tendon-like 22
actuators have been shown to support biologically meaningful experiments on motion 23
generation, internal force transmission, and neural control hypotheses [ 4–8]. This dual 24
perspective of using robots to learn about biology and using biology to design more 25
adaptive robots has been emphasized in both biomimetic robotics and biomechanics [4,9].26
A wide range of actuation technologies have been explored in robotics, including 27
electric motors, hydraulic systems, smart-material actuators, and artificial muscles [10,11]. 28
Electric motors are widely used due to their controllability and maturity, but are limited by 29
thermal constraints, gearing requirements, and reduced compliance compared to muscle- 30
like actuators [12,13]. Hydraulic actuators offer exceptionally high power density and have 31
enabled highly dynamic legged systems, but they remain relatively heavy, less efficient than 32
biological muscle, and require a dedicated fluid infrastructure [13]. Dielectric elastomer 33
actuators (DEAs), a class of electronic electroactive polymers, demonstrate high specific 34
work and good bandwidth in comparative studies [ 11]. However, they are limited by 35
the need for kilovolt-range driving voltages [ 14], electromechanical instability, and the 36

requirement for compliant electrodes [11]. In contrast, McKibben-style braided pneumatic 37
actuators (BPAs) combine low mass, high force-to-weight ratio, and intrinsic compliance 38
with a force–length curve that qualitatively resembles that of skeletal muscle [11,15]. BPAs 39
have been used in quadrupedal robots [ 15,16], musculoskeletal humanoids [ 4,6], and 40
dexterous manipulation systems [8], demonstrating their ability to support biologically 41
motivated investigations and improve robot adaptability in unstructured environments.42
Despite these advantages, BPAs exhibit several important differences from biological 43
muscle. First, BPA maximum tensile force occurs at resting (i.e., uninflated) length (lrest) 44
rather than at an optimal fiber length [11]. Second, their maximum contraction ratio is sub- 45
stantially lower than that of human muscle, presenting challenges for achieving biomimetic 46
joint ranges of motion [ 11,15,17]. Third, BPAs have a highly nonlinear dependence on 47
internal pressure (P), contraction (ϵ), and loading state [15,18], which complicates control 48
and motivates high-fidelity force–length–pressure modeling.49
To address these challenges, numerous researchers have proposed static and dynamic 50
models for BPAs. Sárosi et al. [17] compared geometric and empirical models for uninflated 51
diameters (ϕ) 10 mm and 20 mm actuators and highlighted limitations when lrest varies. 52
Martens and Boblan [18] introduced a physically motivated static model for the Festo 53
DMSP series and demonstrated accurate predictions at specific lrest. Festo provides a 54
manufacturer tool for predicting force as a function of pressure and contraction [ 19,20], 55
but experimental comparisons indicate that it does not adequately account for variation in 56
lrest or actuator-specific contraction limits. Hunt et al. [15] developed a more generalizable 57
empirical model for ϕ10 mm Festo BPAs that incorporates differences between eccentric 58
and concentric loading states and accounts for the maximum achievable contraction (ϵmax ) 59
at high pressures. Comparisons with experimental data indicates that none of the existing 60
models effectively takes into account differences in maximum force due to initial actuator61
length. 62
The present work develops an improved force–pressure–length model for both 63
ϕ10 mm and ϕ20 mm Festo BPAs. We collected extensive force data for a wide range 64
of resting lengths, contraction ratios, and internal pressures. These data were first used 65
to find maximum force as a function of resting length. Then we normalized the data to 66
formulate normalized force (F∗) as a function of relative strain (ϵ∗) and relative pressure 67
(P∗). The resulting model provides improved predictive accuracy for isometric BPA force, 68
supporting the design and control of biomimetic robotic systems that rely on BPAs.69
2. Background 70
Previous studies have generated empirical models of the force-length-pressure rela- 71
tionship of BPA actuators. Sárosi et al. [17] discuss several high-fidelity BPA force models. 72
They present a static force model with a 21 coefficient polynomial function for a Festo MAS- 73
20-200N (i.e. ϕ20 mm, lrest = 200 mm) with an impressive R2 = 0.9994. They also present 74
Sárosi’s static force model for a Festo DMSP-20-400N-RM-RM (i.e. ϕ20 mm, lrest = 400 mm) 75
, with 6 coefficients and a R2 = 0.9995. Martins and Boblan present an even more accurate 76
model, in terms of absolute error, using five coefficients for a DMSP-10-250 (i.e. ϕ10 mm, 77
lrest = 250 mm) and a DMSP-20-300 (i.e. ϕ20 mm, lrest = 300 mm) [18]. However, our 78
work, as presented below, demonstrates that different resting lengths produce different 79
force-length curves, preventing the use of these models for designing systems with different 80
resting lengths. 81
Hunt et al. [15] looked at six resting lengths of ϕ10 mm Festo BPAs, accounting for 82
differences in maximum contractile percentages, and elucidating the force-length-pressure 83
relationship. In particular, for a given contraction percent and force F (in Newtons), the 84

3. Materials and Methods 106
3.1. Overview 107
To measure artificial muscle force as a function of length and pressure, we built a test 108
jig from extruded alumnium and 3D printed parts. Festo BPAs with 10 mm and 20 mm109
diameter were tested with various resting lengths and different amounts of contraction. 110
These data are used to develop an improved model that more accurately predicts maximum 111
muscle force based on lrest, current muscle length lm, and pressure P. The results are 112
compared with existing models and the new data is used to create an updated model.113
3.2. BP A force characterization experiment 114
A test jig was made of 80/20® brand 1515 series extruded aluminum (Figure 1). 115
Artificial muscles were placed vertically in the jig one at a time. The upper end was attached 116
to an S-shaped load cell. The lower end was attached to an adjustable crossmember that 117
was used to change the actuator length lm. Compressed air was supplied through the 118
building at 620 kPa and measured with a Freescale MPX5700 pressure sensor.119
The inner distance between the hose clamps on each BPA was measured to determine 120
the muscle’s resting length ( lrest). This is how Festo [19] defines lrest, although in Hunt 121
et al. [15] it was measured to also include end cap length. We then inflated each BPA to 122
P620 = 620 kPa, with one end allowed to move freely in the axial Degree of Freedom (DoF), 123
and measured the length l620 to calculate maximum contraction at 620kPa using Equation 3. 124
The distance between the crossmembers was then controlled to obtain different amounts of 125

recorded at the pressure-contraction pairs. This was done for ϕ10 mm BPA resting lengths 127

4.1. Maximum Force at 620 kPa 131
Data of the maximum force from BPA characterization tests of the 10 mm and 20 mm132
diameters at 620 kPa show a dependency on resting length (Figure 2). This is a previously 133
unreported characteristic of these artificial muscles. Detailed anaylsis of the Festo Tool 134
[19], do in fact predict a change in maximum force with the resting length, however the 135
Festo Tool predicts increased force with shorter lengths, while our collected data indicate 136
decreasing force with shorter lengths. The data show a force response resembling an arctan137
curve along the lrest dimension. Using the Nonlinear Least Squares method and a Least 138
Absolute Residual robustness, we fit an arctan curve to the data to get the maximum force 139
at 620 kPA given a resting length as: 140

4.4. Force as a function of pressure and contraction 164

by the actuator is also a function of the amount of actuator contraction, with less force 166
being applied as the actuator contracts. To build this model, the collected data of force, 167

This had the effect of compressing the data into a 3D surface ranging from 0 to 1 on all axes. 169
Normalized data are compared with pressure isoclines of force predicted by the Festo tool 170
divided by the maximum force equation described in the previous section (Figure 4. With 171
this comparison, it is clear that the Festo tool over-predicts the expected force, especially at 172

5. Discussion 183
We have developed newF620 equations for 10mm and 20 mm diameter Festo BPAs that 184
capture the change in maximum force produced by the actuators at 620 kPa as a function 185
of their resting length. When examining Equation 5 and measured data in Figure 2B, it 186
can be seen that as lrest goes to infinity, F62010 goes to 470 N. When the Festo tool [ 20] 187
was queried at lrest = 1 m as an approximation of infinity, it predicts the ϕ10 mm BPA 188
can produce F= 490 N at P620. This is within the 10% variability that Festo states may 189
occur in manufacturing tolerances. Similarly, the Festo data predict a ϕ20 mm BPA will 190
produce F= 1570 N. However, our results for Equation 6 show that F62020 goes to 1460 N 191
as lrest approaches infinity. Although the difference is much larger, it is also with the 10% 192
manufacturing tolerance. 193
Where our measured data differs significantly from the Festo model, is that as lrest 194
goes to zero. For the measured data, as the length gets smaller, the force does as well, 195
whereas the Festo tool predicts exponential force increase (see Figure 2). As a specific 196
example, when specifying lrest = 112 mm for the 10 mm diameter BPAs, the Festo predicted 197
F620 is 498.6 N. However, using Equation 5, F620 is calculated to be 335.3 N. Actual force in 198
the lrest = 112 mm was measured at 350.9 N Therefore, the error in predicted F620 for the 199
ϕ10 mm BPA is 4.5% for our model and 42.1% using the Festo tool. Researchers using BPA 200
resting lengths under 300 mm should take note of these results.201
It remains to be seen how ffl620 can be known a priori. We still suspect that it is a 202
function of lrest, but it might also be a function of product batch number. Modeling work 203
of ideal McKibben actuators describe how this is a function of the total number of twists, 204
and the angle of twist in the usable muscle area [21]. Uncovering this relationship for the 205
Festo DMSP/MAS artificial muscles, if it exists in a meaningful way, will require additional 206
controlled tests. Not knowing this relationship a priori means there is still the potential 207
for robots and other systems to go through an iterative design stage if it is discovered that 208
the system does not produce the torque or have the RoM that the design team expects. 209
However, the data and models provided here should enable for faster redesigns with fewer 210
additional measurements. 211
We have also introduced the concept of nondimensionalized isometric force that is a 212
function of relative strain and relative pressure (Equation 8). This elegant equation has only 213
3 coefficients with low error (Table 2. In the work presented here, we add in the relative 214
pressure term, P∗ =P/P 620. Our data shows that by normalizing force, contraction, and 215
pressure, we are able to create a simplified force equation as a function of contraction and216
pressure that scales well with initial actuator length, i.e.217
F(ϵ ∗,P ∗,l rest ) =F ∗(ϵ∗,P ∗)·F 620(lrest )(9)218
For example, given lrest = 54 mm and P= 300 kPa, the measured force was Factual =219

This is 161% greater than the measured force. Using Equation 5 and Equation 8 with 221
Equation 9 gives Fpredict =F ∗(0, 0.48)·F 620(0.054) = 0.49 · 230 N = 112 N. This is an error 222
of 8%, which is much more accurate than using the Festo tool only.223
In our work, P620 = 620 kPa is the maximum supply pressure for our system, and other 224
users of this actuator may use a different supply pressure. However, this supply pressure 225
is not required, and normalizing by this value was chosen as a method for improving the 226
optimization techniques by reducing the sensitivity of the term associated with pressure. 227
This equation will work for any pressure supplies below the 620 kPa. We also anticipate 228
the equation will also work for pressures up to 800 kPa (the maximum rated pressure for 229
Festo 10 mm BPAs), however we do not have the data to confirm this.230

The improved BPA characterization more accurately predicts isometric force produced 231
by the BPAs. Unlike Equation 9, it does not account for the hysteresis that occurs in BPAs. 232
BPA artificial muscles are often said to be analogous to biological muscles because they 233
have force length curves and can produce force only in tension. This analogy is worth 234
closer inspection, although it should be kept in mind that biological muscle optimum fiber 235
length lOFL is not equivalent to lrest. The improved Hill-muscle model of Thelen [22] shows 236
an exponential term for a muscle’s passive force-length property concentric loading.237
The analysis in this study has created novel equations for calculating force in Festo 238
10 mm and 20 mm diameter BPAs. This study has elucidated the relationship between 239
maximum BPA force at 620 kPa F620 as a function of resting length lrest (Equation 5, Equa- 240
tion 6, Table 1). We have also created more accurate equations for the nondimensionalized 241
force in a BPA as a function of relative strain ϵ∗ and relative pressure P∗, (Equation 8 and 242
Table 2). Taken together, the F620(lrest ) fits and the nondimensional force surface F∗(ϵ∗, P∗)243
provide a practical way to predict isometric BPA force across resting lengths using a small 244
number of measurements. In a design workflow, these equations make it easier to select 245
actuator diameter and resting length, and to evaluate whether a proposed routing can meet 246
force and range-of-motion requirements before committing to a full prototype. As a result, 247
BPA-driven mechanisms (e.g., joints in biomimetic legs or manipulators) can be developed 248
with fewer design iterations and with realistic expectations of force output at short resting 249
lengths. 250
