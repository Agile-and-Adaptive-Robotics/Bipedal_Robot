ABSTRACT2
Artificial muscles such as braided pneumatic actuators (BPAs) reproduce the force and con-3
traction behavior of skeletal muscle, making them attractive for the legs of musculoskeletal4
robots. However, the isometric torque a BPA delivers about a joint remains difficult to predict,5
because rigid-body torque models omit attachment compliance, effective-length offsets, and the6
contraction lost where the actuator wraps over the joint. In this work, we measured isometric7
knee torque on pinned and biomimetic knee test stands across actuator resting lengths, isolated8
error sources with a hybrid torque calculation, and identified correction terms by multiobjective9
optimization. Our analysis resulted in (1) a corrected torque model whose length offset, effective10
stiffness, and fictive-strain wrapping loss, expressed through the unloaded620 kPa contraction,11
reduce held-out prediction error on both pinned-knee test families and transfer to the biomimetic12
knee without refitting; (2) a cross-configuration-consistent stiffness pair selected by a simulta-13
neous design requirement; and (3) redesigned attachment routes that meet or exceed human14
isometric knee-torque targets with quantified margins, together with the first hardware torque15
measurements of the redesigned extensor route. The corrected model and redesign pipeline16
support first-iteration torque design of BPA-actuated robot joints.17

1 INTRODUCTION
Musculoskeletal robots built on biological design principles are experimental platforms for motor-control20
questions that are impractical or unethical to study in human subjects, from spinal reflex loops to the21
coupling of posture and locomotion (Ijspeert, 2020; Goldsmith et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2018). The same22
principles also yield machines that copy the anatomy of their biological counterparts: human-mimetic23
humanoids and anthropomorphic arms with anatomically arranged musculotendon networks (Asano et al.,24
2017, 2019; Hitzmann et al., 2018), lower-limb robots driven by multifilament muscles (Kurumaya et al.,25
2016), and platforms that isolate the function of a single muscle such as the gluteus medius (Shin et al.,26
2018) or select actions during adaptive locomotion (Schilling et al., 2022). Each of these machines asks its27

actuators to do what skeletal muscle does: pull as a lightweight tension member whose force depends on28
excitation and length, routed from origin to insertion across a joint. The joint-level quantity that matters is29
not force but torque, and torque depends as much on the routed path as on the actuator itself.30
No actuator family reproduces muscle exactly, and each alternative trades something away. Geared31
electric motors dominate position control, but they place mass and reflected inertia at the joint and gain32
compliance only through added series elasticity (Veale and Xie, 2016). Hydraulics supply high force33
density at the price of pumps, valves, and rigid supply lines (Suzumori and Faudzi, 2018). Shape-memory34
alloys respond slowly, and dielectric elastomers deliver low force at impractical drive voltages (Madden35
et al., 2004). Braided pneumatic actuators (BPAs), also called McKibben artificial muscles, occupy the36
practical middle ground: a pressurized sleeved bladder that shortens and pulls, producing force that falls37
with contraction in a muscle-like way while remaining a compliant tension member. Air-muscle quadrupeds38
have demonstrated robust, adaptive gaits (Aschenbeck et al., 2006), and the Festo fluidic BPAs used in this39
work are commercially available in the sizes a human-scale leg requires.40
For BPAs, the force side of the design problem is well characterized: we have published closed-form41
equations for the maximum force and the isometric force of10 mmand20 mm Festo actuators as functions42
of pressure, contraction, and resting length (Bolen et al., 2026). The torque side has received far less43
attention. Joint torque is the product of actuator force and the moment arm of the actuator’s line of action44
about the joint axis, and the moment arm is a property of the attachment route that changes with joint45
angle. Biomechanics treats moment-arm determination as its own measurement problem, from lower-limb46
muscular anatomy (Brand et al., 1982) and three-dimensional musculotendon estimation (Menegaldo et al.,47
2004) to curved paths with moving attachment points (Suderman and Vasavada, 2012), because short48
moment arms amplify path error (Young et al., 2019). The robot hardware adds further sources of error:49
brackets and fixtures deform under load, artificial tendons and cabling stretch, and at deep flexion the50
actuator bends over the joint and loses usable contraction. None of these effects appears in a rigid-body51
torque model built from a characterized actuator and nominal geometry.52
Torque modeling for braided actuators has consequently concentrated on the force law and on placement.53
Chou and Hannaford (1996) derived the static force relation of McKibben muscles from braid geometry54
and quantified its hysteresis, and later work refined static models and approximations for pneumatic and55
fluidic muscles (Tondu, 2022; S´arosi, 2012). For the actuator family used here, Martens and Boblan (2017)56
modeled static force from a geometric and material framework, and Hunt et al. (2017) modeled length57
effects. At the joint level, Bolen and Hunt (2019) determined artificial-muscle placement for biomimetic58
humanoid robot legs, and Morrow et al. (2020) optimized placements so that a bipedal robot matches59
human torque and range-of-motion capabilities. The present tests evaluate errors that remain when these60
force and routing models are applied to a physical joint. None of them represents fixture and tendon61
compliance in the load path, carries an effective-length offset between modeled and physical muscle length,62
or accounts for the contraction lost where the actuator wraps over the joint. To our knowledge, no BPA63
torque model has been validated across joint configurations, actuator resting lengths, and both antagonistic64
sides of a joint with a single consistent parameter set.65
The present work treats these mispredictions as identifiable design factors, not residual noise. We66
measured isometric knee torque on two test stands: a robot leg with a pinned revolute knee, and the same67
leg with a biomimetic knee whose instantaneous center of rotation migrates with flexion like that of the68
human joint (Steele, 2018; Steele et al., 2017). Each knee was driven by antagonistic flexor and extensor69
BPAs of 10 mmand20 mm diameter across a range of resting lengths, with and without artificial tendons.70

2 METHODS AND MATERIALS
2.1 Overview86
Isometric knee torque is the product of the force an actuator develops and the moment arm of its line87
of action about the joint axis, and the force itself is set by the actuator’s effective length through the88
force model. Each link in that chain is a design quantity: the attachment routing fixes the moment arm,89
the resting length and attachment compliance set the effective contraction, and wrapping over the joint90
removes usable length. We built two knee test stands, measured isometric torque across each joint’s91
range of motion with 10 mmand20 mm Festo braided pneumatic actuators (BPAs) in flexor and extensor92
configurations, and compared the measurements against the original rigid-body model, the improved93
actuator force characterization alone, and the corrected model developed here. A hybrid calculation isolated94
the error sources, a multiobjective identification produced the correction terms, and the corrected model95
then drove a redesign of the attachment routes against human isometric torque targets.96

2.4 Equipment and Data Collection127
A test jig was built to collect knee torque at set knee angles over the range of motion. The stand frame is128
made of 80/20 1515 series extruded aluminum. The femur is fixed to the frame while the knee rotates the129
tibia, with the joint axis co-axial with gravity to eliminate gravitational torque. A force sensor connects the130
tibia to a swing arm tied to a winch with 3/16-inch Kevlar rope (Quality Nylon Rope) routed over idler131
pulleys. At each measured θk the winch was locked and the BPA was inflated and then deflated, keeping the132
measurement as isometric as possible. Because the sensor and its rope load path tie the tibia to the locked133
winch, the sensor reading is the tension that holds the tibia at the set angle. The measured knee torque at134
each angle is that tension multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the knee axis to the rope’s line of135
action at its tibial attachment, the measured counterpart of the scalar reduction of Equation (10), with the136
attachment geometry and the sensor-line angle recorded at each test position.137
Force was collected with one of two sensors: a MODERN STEP 300 kg digital crane scale, or a CALT138
DYLY-103100 kg S-shaped load cell used with an HX711 load-cell amplifier. Pressure was measured139
with a Freescale MPX5700 GP 5 V pressure sensor. Supply pressure was controlled by two regulators140
in series, a Parker 20R113GC (0ψto120ψ ) and a Husky HDA72200, and air was delivered through a141
Festo VTUG-10 valve manifold. Pressure and load-cell data were sent to MATLAB through a Sparkfun142
BlackBoard C (Arduino Uno style) microcontroller. Lengths were measured with a tape measure, with a143
flexible string marking the axial length of the BPA when its curvature was sufficient, and angles with a144

2.9 Route-Redesign Procedure285
With the correction terms fixed, the attachment routes themselves become design variables, and their286
best values are the ones that make the redesigned routes meet or exceed human knee torque capability. The287
redesign was carried out on the robot-body model derived from the Gait2392 lower-extremity model (Delp288
et al., 2007; Seth et al., 2018). The human targets are maximum-isometric torque curves: OpenSim moment289
arms, the partial derivative of muscle-tendon length with respect to the knee coordinate,rpθkq “ Bl MT{Bθk,290
multiplied by each muscle’s maximum isometric force (Delp et al., 1990; Hoy et al., 1990; Yamaguchi291
and Zajac, 1989; Sherman et al., 2013; Millard et al., 2013; Thelen, 2003; Seth et al., 2011). The extensor292
is graded against vastus medialis and the flexor against biceps femoris short head; each target carries a293
required margin of 5 % above the human curve, so a route that merely touches the target at any angle is294
rejected.295
The design variables are the BPA origin p1, the insertion pend, the resting length, and the tendon length.296

to evaluate and only moderately smooth, the redesign minimizes a scalar cost assembled from weighted298

4 DISCUSSION
4.1 Sources of Error in Isometric Torque Generation411
The improved BPA force characterization removes part of the torque-prediction error, but only part412
(Figure 7). The remainder traces to the test system itself. The isometric system is not rigid: the winch413
cable, the artificial tendons, and the attachment brackets all bend and stretch under load, and the correction414
family χ0, χ1, and χ2 was created to represent them. Positioning tolerances, imperfect path modeling, and415
variation between individual actuators contribute as well.416
The modeling of extensor paths that wrap around the knee is an approximation with inherent error, and417
the test-stand geometry amplifies it. When the moment arm is short, small changes to it have a large effect418
on the resultant torque (Young et al., 2019), which is why moment-arm determination receives so much419
attention in musculoskeletal modeling (Brand et al., 1982; Menegaldo et al., 2004; Suderman and Vasavada,420
2012). We chose to wrap the BPA around the joint rather than add a patella with a deterministically designed421
location and friction; the resulting path, which passes between the femoral condyles close to the migrating422
center of rotation of the biomimetic knee (Steele, 2018), resists any straight-line idealization (Figure 9A).423
At high flexion, the shorter actuators were also observed to stretch and compress (Figure 1C), and the424
same loading occasionally slipped the actuator off the anterior bolt head that holds the femur to the knee,425
displacing the path by up to ˘20 mm in the Z direction and changing both the force vector and the torque.426
The pinned extensor tests exposed one further error source, with a signature distinct from compliance.427
Figure 8 shows a deviation between measured and originally predicted torque that widens as the expected428

angles. That combination points to a loss of functional muscle length rather than excess compliance, and it430
is why the wrapping-loss term χ3 was created in Equation (25). Bent over the joint, the actuator behaves431
like a beam, and the wrapped arc stops contributing usable contraction, so the actuator behaves as if it were432
more contracted than it physically is; this is the fictive strain of Section 2.7, and Section 4.2 quantifies it.433
Working from the simple pinned knee to the complex biomimetic one isolated each of these mechanisms434
in turn. The products of that progression, a predictor for bending and axial compliance, a constant length435
offset, and a measure of the usable length lost as the actuator bends around a joint, are what make436

4.5 Design Factors for Isometric Torque636
Read as a design problem, isometric torque about a BPA-actuated joint factors into three quantities the637
designer controls: the moment arm r, the normalized contraction ε˚ at which the actuator operates, and the638
compliance of the path and its fixtures, carried by the χ terms. Each result in this paper maps onto one639
corner of that triangle. Short moment arms amplify every error, so stiffness must be budgeted where the640
arm is short (Young et al., 2019). For a given l0, moment arm and contraction allowance trade against each641
other, and the cam redesign shows the trade can be favorable: it raises the moment arm from the direct642
route’s44.9 mm to 61.2 mm to a span of 59.4 mm to 102.8 mm while improving the torque margin. This643
increased moment arm comes at a cost to biomimicry. Pressure headroom, finally, is a design resource: the644
cam route meets its envelope between269 kPaand507 kPaagainst a620 kPaceiling.645
These factors situate our work among musculoskeletal robot legs. Multifilament muscle routing has646
produced human-scale lower limbs (Kurumaya et al., 2016), anthropomorphic arms (Hitzmann et al.,647
2018), and full human-mimetic humanoids (Asano et al., 2019), and tendon-driven bodies have supported648
investigations from reflexive hopping (Liu et al., 2018) to human-like manipulation (Chen et al., 2019)649
and cognitive locomotion (Schilling et al., 2022). Linkage-based knees manage the moment-arm problem650
geometrically (Romero et al., 2020), reviews of dynamic multilegged robots treat actuation routing as a651
first-order design decision (He and Gao, 2020), and tunable-compliance actuators make fixture compliance652
a design element rather than an error source (Leibach, 2020). What these designs share with ours is the653
recognition that the attachment route, not the actuator alone, sets the deliverable torque. What the corrected654
model adds is a quantitative accounting of the compliance and wrapping losses along that route, so that655
a route can be designed against a torque target rather than checked after the fact. Morrow et al. (2020)656
optimized artificial-muscle placement on a bipedal robot with a rigid-body torque model, and the correction657
terms quantified here are the physics such rigid-body placements leave unmodeled.658
The correction family also complements the BPA force-modeling literature. Static models of the actuator659
in isolation, from the classical McKibben analyses (Chou and Hannaford, 1996) through approximation660
formulas (S´arosi, 2012) to geometrically grounded models of the Festo muscle (Martens and Boblan,661
2017; Tondu, 2022), describe the actuator itself; none of them carries attachment compliance, length662
offsets, or wrapping losses into a joint-torque prediction. The correction terms used here attach to the force663
characterization of Bolen et al. (2026) and travel with it across joint configurations under one consistent664
parameter set.665

4.6 Limitations and Future Work666
The correction terms are identified for this test stand, these knee configurations, and these actuator667
families. Other joints or actuator types should repeat the two-stage procedure rather than reuse the values.668
Transfer to other systems requires validation against their measured torque.669
Isometric torque testing has yielded important design factors for roboticists seeking to design biomimetic670
humanoid robots. Isokinetic and isotonic tests would assess behavior outside the isometric conditions671
evaluated here.672
5 CONCLUSION
Isometric knee-torque measurements on pinned and biomimetic test stands showed that actuator force673
characterization alone does not determine delivered joint torque. A hybrid calculation using measured674
muscle length and moment arm helped separate force-model error from routing and attachment effects.675
The corrected torque model represents a constant length offset, effective attachment compliance, and an676
empirical wrapping loss. A two-stage identification shares one stiffness pair across flexor and extensor677
configurations and reduces prediction error on the held-out tests.678
Route redesign under the corrected model produces configurations with two I20 mm BPAs that meet679
the selected human-muscle torque targets over their design grids, with the required 5 % margin. The cam680
extensor increases predicted torque headroom while reducing the required operating pressure. Physical681
tests meet or exceed the respective human targets at all 15 measured flexor points and all nine measured682
extensor points. These measurements validate the tested isometric configurations; dynamic loading and683
transfer to other joints remain to be evaluated.684
